# The AI Layoff Trap: Why Replacing Workers Could Destroy the Very Economy AI Needs

## The Parable of the Factory Owner

Once upon a time — though it was very recent, and it happened at a company you probably know — a CEO walked into a board meeting and announced that half the company was being let go. The reason wasn't a recession. The product wasn't failing. The reason was a new kind of software that could write code, answer emails, and generate marketing copy faster than the humans who used to do those jobs. The board loved it. Investors loved it. The stock price jumped twenty-five percent in after-hours trading.

Everyone in the room understood the logic. Labor is a cost. Software is a sunk cost. If you replace the former with the latter, margins expand. The spreadsheet says so.

But here is the part no one in that room asked: if every company does this, who is left to buy the things they are now so efficiently making?

That question sits at the heart of what might be the most important economic debate of 2026. Not whether AI can do your job — that debate is essentially over, for a huge slice of white-collar work — but whether *doing* your job, and millions of others, will break the economic machine that makes AI profitable in the first place.

## A Paper With an Uncomfortable Answer

In March 2026, two economists at the Wharton School — Brett Hemenway Falk and Gerry Tsoukalas — published a working paper on arXiv with a title so direct it reads like a warning sign: **"The AI Layoff Trap."**

Their argument is mathematically rigorous but conceptually simple. Imagine a market with twenty competing firms. Each firm knows that replacing workers with AI will cut its own costs. Each firm also knows, vaguely, that if everyone does it, consumers will have less money to spend. But here is the catch: each firm feels only one-twentieth of the total demand destruction it helps cause. The other nineteen-twentieths land on everyone else. The rational choice, for every single firm, is to automate as aggressively as possible — even though the *collective* outcome is ruinous. This is not a market failure in the textbook sense. It is a classic prisoner's dilemma, dressed in shareholder reports and SaaS subscriptions.

The economists are clear about the timeline. "Improved productivity widens the profit gap from automating faster than your competitors," they write, "intensifying the arms race toward collective ruin." Better AI makes the trap worse, not better. More competition makes it worse. Wage adjustments cannot fix it. Universal basic income, worker equity participation, and retraining programs cannot eliminate the underlying dynamic. The only policy tool they identify as effective is a Pigouvian automation tax — essentially, making firms pay for the externalities they create when they displace human labor.

You can almost hear the spreadsheet jockeys objecting: *But we've automated before!*

True. We have. The agricultural share of the American workforce collapsed from roughly ninety percent to two percent over a century and a half. Civilization did not end. But as the economists who study these transitions point out, history is an observation, not a law of physics. The agricultural transition took about a hundred and forty years. The Industrial Revolution took roughly seventy years before wages and employment recovered for displaced workers. In the interim, wages stagnated, inequality soared, and the political consequences included the Chartist movement, widespread social upheaval, and a renegotiation of the social contract.

The AI industry is working on a timeline of years, not decades. Bharat Ramamurti, a former deputy director of the National Economic Council, drew the parallel to the China shock — the wave of manufacturing job losses that reshaped American politics. "The China shock unfolded over several years," he noted, "whereas this could happen over two years. These companies have spent so much money developing models that there's going to be immense pressure on them to generate revenue through quick adoption."

## The Dead Economy

If the Wharton paper is the academic frame, "The Dead Economy Theory" — a viral essay by Owen McGrann that exploded on Hacker News in late May, racking up nearly nine hundred points and over a thousand comments — is the narrative. McGrann starts with something most internet-weathered readers already accept: the "dead internet theory." The idea that most content online is now generated by bots, for bots, with humans reduced to a shrinking audience for machine noise. Over half of all new content on the internet in 2025 was AI-generated. The humans are still there, scrolling, but the show is no longer for them.

McGrann extends this logic to the economy. The AI industry has raised and spent hundreds of billions of dollars. OpenAI alone sits at an valuation north of $800 billion. Anthropic, which has never turned a profit, commands a valuation in the same stratosphere. These numbers only make sense if the addressable market is the global labor market — essentially, all human cognitive work. Every investor pitch about an AI agent "doing the work of ten analysts" is saying the same thing in gentler language. The product is labor replacement. The financial model requires the elimination of human cost centers at civilizational scale.

McGrann then walks through three turns of the economic engine.

**Turn one:** A company licenses AI and replaces a chunk of its workforce. Costs drop, margins expand, the stock price goes up. Everyone on the earnings call is happy.

**Turn two:** The replaced workers stop earning income. They stop spending. The businesses they used to buy from see revenue decline. Some of those businesses automate too, compounding the contraction. Consumer demand shrinks across the economy.

**Turn three:** The company that fired its workers discovers that its customers were, in aggregate, other companies' workers. Revenue growth stalls. The AI subscription meant to drive efficiency turns out to be a contribution to the destruction of its own market.

It is a macroeconomic ouroboros: the snake eating its own tail, except the tail is the consumer base.

## The Numbers Don't Add Up

There is a second, quieter problem that runs underneath the demand-destruction story: the technology may not even be good enough to justify the displacement it causes.

Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu — arguably the most rigorous academic voice on this topic — has found that between 1987 and 2017, "the displacement effect of new technologies far outweighed their productivity and reinstatement effects." New tasks did not materialize fast enough to absorb displaced workers. His current assessment of AI is even more pointed. Firms are deploying what he calls "excessive automation": using AI to eliminate jobs without generating significantly lower production costs, while imposing massive social costs.

Acemoglu's estimate? Only about **4.6% of tasks** in the economy are currently cost-effective to automate with AI. His projection for AI's total productivity impact over the next decade: **0.66%**. Goldman Sachs, in a much more optimistic 2023 forecast, projected 7% GDP growth from AI. McKinsey projects a range of 0.5% to 3.5% annually. Over ninety percent of firms surveyed in 2025 reported no measurable impact on employment or productivity, despite a quarter-trillion dollars in AI investment. Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo, put it bluntly: "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data."

In other words, companies are firing people not because AI is provably better, but because the *narrative* that AI is better makes the stock price go up. Zoë Hitzig, an economist who previously worked at OpenAI, described the dynamic to the *New York Times*: "When chief executives are saying they're cutting jobs because of A.I., other people feel like they have to too. That dynamic could make the changes happen sooner than efficiency would dictate." Herd behavior dressed in the language of innovation.

## Who Is the Customer?

There is a thought experiment that gets thrown around in economics circles, sometimes attributed to Henry Ford, sometimes apocryphal, but correct in principle: Ford's workers needed to earn enough to buy his cars. The AI economy is attempting to eliminate the workers and expect the cars — or software subscriptions, or cloud credits — to keep selling.

Except that software has near-zero marginal cost. The entire value proposition *is* the elimination of the human cost center. The product, in a sense, *is* the removal of the customer base.

This is not abstract. OpenAI has published benchmarks like GDPVal, explicitly measuring how well models perform across forty-four occupations. The AI Productivity Index evaluates models against investment banking associates, management consultants, Big Law associates, and primary care physicians. These are targeting reticles aimed at the professional class. An OpenAI evaluation lead told the *Times* that models now achieve "over an 80 percent win rate compared to human professionals" on tasks that, months earlier, no model could match.

The industry is telling the same story from two directions at once. In public-facing white papers, OpenAI has proposed radically progressive policies: a thirty-two-hour workweek, higher taxes on corporations, a "public wealth fund." In the same period, OpenAI's president helped fund a super PAC that spent over two million dollars on ads against a congressional candidate whose crime was introducing AI safety regulation and proposing to tax AI to fund direct payments to Americans. The company removed a profit cap that had previously limited investor returns. The public narrative and the private political spending are moving in opposite directions.

## The Human Cost is Not an Excel Row

Every proposed solution to mass AI displacement treats it as a resource distribution problem. Universal basic income. Retraining programs. The "leisure economy." The assumption is that if you send people checks, they'll find meaning in hobbies and community. They'll paint. They'll garden. They'll finally write that novel.

This, as McGrann notes, is ahistorical.

We do not have to speculate about what happens when economic function disappears from communities. Anne Case and Angus Deaton's research on "deaths of despair" — the rising tide of suicide, overdose, and alcohol-related liver disease concentrated in formerly manufacturing-dependent populations — tracks the mechanism precisely. It is not just poverty. It is the loss of economic purpose, social status, and a perceived future. Communities where the jobs left and what replaced them was opioids, domestic violence, and a life expectancy that dropped year over year in the richest country on earth.

Molly Kinder at Brookings drew the connection explicitly: the AI companies' narratives about abundance repeat the same promises of globalization, and the losers won't be limited to manufacturing towns. "I've interviewed so many college students who are super fearful about what the future means," she told the *Times*, "and their narrative is exactly the same as those blue-collar guys in the heartland."

Anthropic's own research has documented something worse than displacement: active deskilling. Junior engineers who relied on AI coding agents didn't complete tasks much faster and understood their work *less* when quizzed afterward. The tools are degrading the expertise of the next generation at the same time they are competing with them for jobs.

## The Political Dimension

An economy that doesn't need human labor is a political crisis of a kind democratic systems have never faced.

Democratic governance rests on a bargain: the governed have something the governors need — labor, tax revenue, military service, consumer spending. This dependency is the source of democratic leverage. Remove labor from the equation and the entire fiscal mechanism starves at once. The tax base erodes. Collective bargaining becomes vestigial. Consumer spending contracts. The engine of wealth concentration — what economist Thomas Piketty calls "r > g" — accelerates because AI severs the last link between capital accumulation and the need for human labor.

Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, has said this out loud: "The balance of power of democracy is premised on the average person having leverage through creating economic value. If that's not present, I think things become kind of scary." The CEO of one of the three leading AI companies is publicly stating that the technology he is building will undermine the material basis of democratic governance. When asked about policy advocacy, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark described it as "the end of a very, very long chain of work."

Autocracies are arguably better customers for this technology than democracies. A democratic government that deploys AI to replace its workforce faces electoral consequences. An authoritarian government faces no such constraint and gains a surveillance dividend on top of the economic efficiencies. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Singapore: vast capital, centralized decision-making, no electorate to answer to. The economic incentives for AI companies point toward the entities with the fewest democratic accountability mechanisms.

## What Would It Take to Avoid the Trap?

The interventions that could matter are not technologically difficult. They are politically difficult.

Public ownership stakes in AI infrastructure. Aggressive antitrust enforcement. A genuine tax regime on automated labor. Economist Branko Milanovic's prescription is direct: spread capital ownership more widely, tax the highest capital incomes more aggressively. A Pigouvian automation tax, as the Wharton paper argues, is the only policy tool that directly addresses the competitive incentive to over-automate.

But the companies that would need to be taxed are spending millions to defeat the politicians who propose it. The regulatory capture is already advanced: AI-related investments accounted for thirty-nine percent of US economic growth in the first three quarters of 2025, giving the federal government a vested interest in sustaining the boom. Amodei himself has acknowledged that this leads to "the reluctance of tech companies to criticize the U.S. government, and the government's support for extreme anti-regulatory policies on A.I."

The window for changing course is not infinite. The feedback loop — AI systems advising on the governance of AI systems — is closing.

## What This Means for the Rest of Us

If you are reading this as a software engineer, a knowledge worker, a manager, or anyone who makes strategic decisions about technology adoption, the takeaway is not to panic. It is to think strategically about where you place yourself in this transition.

The firms that will do well are not necessarily the ones that automate fastest. They are the ones that automate *discerningly* — using AI to augment human expertise rather than simply replace it, in domains where the quality of output matters and where customer relationships depend on trust. Healthcare, education, creative professional services, complex advisory work: these are not immune to AI, but they are domains where the human element is still economically valuable.

For individual professionals, the priority is to build skills that complement AI rather than compete with it on its home turf. Domain expertise, judgment, client relationships, ethical reasoning, and creative synthesis are all capabilities that current AI performs poorly at scale. The danger is not that AI will replace all work. It is that the transition will be so fast, so poorly managed, and so structurally biased toward capital over labor that the social and political costs will swamp the productivity gains.

The AI Layoff Trap is not a prophecy. It is a warning about incentives. The question is whether we can align the incentives of individual firms with the collective good before the trap springs shut.

The snake is already eating its tail. The only question is whether anyone in the boardroom is willing to look up from the spreadsheet long enough to notice.
