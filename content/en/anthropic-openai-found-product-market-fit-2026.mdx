# AI Finally Found Product-Market Fit — And the Bill Just Dropped

For three years, the AI industry had a popularity problem. ChatGPT was the fastest-growing consumer app in history — 900 million weekly active users by early 2026 — but only 5.6% of them were paying. A $20 subscription is a nice business. It is not a trillion-dollar business.

That changed in April.

In a [viral post](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/27/product-market-fit/) that topped Hacker News with nearly 800 upvotes and 900 comments, developer and entrepreneur [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net) made the case that OpenAI and Anthropic have finally found what Silicon Valley has been chasing since 2022: **real product-market fit**. Not hype. Not demo traffic. Revenue that actually scales.

The product is not the chatbot. It is the coding agent.

---

## The Pricing Pivot That Changed Everything

Here is the quiet bombshell that most people missed.

In April 2026, both Anthropic and OpenAI changed how they charge enterprise customers for their flagship coding tools — Claude Code (and its enterprise sibling Cowork) and Codex, respectively. They dropped the all-you-can-eat SaaS pricing and switched to **API-based billing**.

Anthropic moved its Enterprise plan from "$20 per seat with included usage" to a model where companies pay per seat *plus* API tokens consumed. OpenAI did the same for Codex on April 2, applying it to Plus, Pro, Business, and new Enterprise plans. By April 23, existing Enterprise, Edu, Health, and Gov contracts were locked in too.

Why does this matter? Because API pricing is radically more expensive for heavy users.

Willison runs a quick monthly tally: his personal usage of Claude Code and OpenAI Codex would cost about **$2,180 at API prices**. He pays $200 as a consumer subscriber. That is an 11x difference. For companies where dozens or hundreds of engineers are running agents — some of them autonomous — the gap is staggering.

The old enterprise model was subsidized. Companies got a bargain. That bargain is over.

---

## Why the Labs Are Doing This Now

There are two ways to read the sudden pricing aggression.

**Reading one: desperation.** OpenAI and Anthropic both have IPOs on the horizon. They need to show real revenue and a path to profitability. Charging $20 per user per month does not get you there when you are burning billions on training and inference. Charging $200, or $1,000, or more per power user per month just might.

**Reading two: confidence.** You only raise prices when customers cannot easily leave. The fact that both labs made this move simultaneously in April suggests they saw the same signal: enterprise customers were hooked. Coding agents had crossed the threshold from experiment to daily workflow. Engineers were not "trying" Claude Code. They were shipping code with it. That is the definition of product-market fit — and it is the first time the AI labs have had it in a revenue-generating form.

ChatGPT was always a consumer curiosity that happened to be useful. Coding agents are a **tool for extremely well-compensated professionals doing mission-critical work**. The average software engineer in the US earns well into six figures. If an agent makes them even 20% more productive, the company will pay $500 or $1,000 a month without blinking. Until, of course, the bills show up.

---

## The Bill Shock Is Already Here

The stories are piling up.

Uber's CTO, Praveen Neppalli Naga, reportedly told staff that the company had **"maxed out its full year AI budget just a few months into 2026"** — mostly because of Claude Code. That sounds alarming. Willison's take is more measured: Uber set its 2026 AI budget in late 2025, before Claude Code became genuinely useful in November. Of course they underestimated demand. That is what happens when a product finds fit.

Then there is Microsoft. [The Verge reported](https://www.theverge.com/tech/930447/microsoft-claude-code-discontinued-notepad) in mid-May that Microsoft had started canceling Claude Code licenses for internal engineers, pushing them toward Microsoft's own Copilot CLI agent instead. Officially, it is about dogfooding. Sources told reporter Tom Warren the decision is also financial — Microsoft, whose fiscal year ends June 30, was looking at ballooning costs.

These are not failure stories. They are **suck-air-through-your-teeth-and-say-yes stories** — the exact reaction you want when pricing a product that has real value.

Uber's COO, Andrew Macdonald, went on the [Rapid Response podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y_mQ6xLcKyc) and described the real challenge: 25% of Uber's code commits last quarter came via Claude Code, but linking that metric to actual consumer value is still hard. "That link is not there yet," he admitted. The company is spending more and shipping faster, but nobody can draw a straight line from token consumption to product velocity.

That is the tension at the heart of this moment. The tools are good enough to use every day. They are not yet good enough to justify their cost with hard ROI.

---

## The Scale of Spending Beggars Belief

If you want a sense of how much money is moving through this ecosystem, consider a single line item from SpaceX's recent IPO filing.

In May 2026, SpaceX disclosed that Anthropic had signed a cloud services agreement for access to the **COLOSSUS and COLOSSUS II** supercomputers. The price: **$1.25 billion per month**, running through May 2029. That is roughly $45 billion over three years from one contract with one vendor.

Anthropic has compute deals with other providers too. The fact that it needs another $15 billion per year from SpaceX alone tells you something about the scale of modern AI inference. When Willison talks about a trillion-dollar infrastructure buildout, he is not being metaphorical.

The labs are locked in a capital arms race. Training a frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars. Running it at scale for millions of enterprise users costs billions per month. The only way to cover that is to charge enterprise customers like they are consuming a utility — which, increasingly, they are.

---

## The Revenue Pivot: From API Middlemen to Enterprise Direct

There is another strategic layer here. Historically, Anthropic made much of its money from API customers — other companies building on top of Claude. In August 2025, VentureBeat reported that just **Cursor and GitHub Copilot** were responsible for $1.2 billion of Anthropic's then-$4 billion annual revenue. Anthropic was an infrastructure provider. Cursor and Copilot were the products.

Claude Code changes that equation. Anthropic is now building its own end-user product, competing directly with the very customers that made it rich. It is a classic platform-competitor conflict, and it explains why Cursor is investing in its own models. Nobody wants to be dependent on a supplier who is also a rival.

OpenAI has followed a similar arc. It started as an API company. Then it launched ChatGPT and became a consumer product. Now it is pushing Codex,agents, and enterprise tools — essentially saying, "We will be the product, not just the engine."

The result is a market where every major player is trying to be vertically integrated: model, agent, IDE, billing, support. It is expensive, risky, and probably unsustainable for more than two or three winners.

---

## What Happens Next

Willison ends his post with a simple, unsettling observation: the AI-failure stories are running thin.

For two years, the headlines alternated between "AI is replacing programmers" and "AI is hallucinating garbage." Both were exaggerated. What we are seeing now is more prosaic and more profound: **AI tools are genuinely useful, genuinely expensive, and genuinely uncertain in their returns.**

Companies are not ripping out their engineering teams. They are augmenting them — sometimes productively, sometimes messily — and paying through the nose for the privilege. The coding agent is the first AI product since ChatGPT itself that has achieved daily-use status for a large population of skilled workers. That is product-market fit.

Whether it is *profitable* product-market fit is a different question. Anthropic may have its first profitable quarter in Q2 2026, with revenue rumored to hit $10.9 billion. But one quarter of profit after years of billion-dollar losses does not make a sustainable business. It makes a promising one.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is reportedly targeting a valuation above $300 billion in its IPO. Anthropic is not far behind. Both companies are betting that enterprise AI spending will keep growing at rates that would make most SaaS executives dizzy. The only way that bet pays off is if coding agents — and the broader class of "task agents" they represent — become as essential to knowledge work as spreadsheets and email.

That day may come. It is not here yet. But for the first time since the launch of ChatGPT in late 2022, the path from "cool demo" to "indispensable tool" looks real — and the invoices prove it.

---

**Bottom line:** AI did not find product-market fit with chatbots. It found it with agents that write code, run tests, and file pull requests while you sleep. The catch? Those agents are eating compute budgets alive, and nobody yet knows if the productivity gains justify the bill. The next twelve months will tell us whether this is the foundation of a trillion-dollar industry — or the most expensive false dawn in tech history.
