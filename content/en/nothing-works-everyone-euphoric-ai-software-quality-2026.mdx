# Nothing Works and Everyone Is Euphoric

## 688 Upvotes. One Uncomfortable Question.

On 24 July 2026, a software developer published an essay with a title that landed like a gut punch: *"Nothing Works and Everyone Is Euphoric."* Within hours it had climbed to the top of Hacker News, collecting **688 upvotes** and **over 500 comments**. The post was not a technical deep-dive. It was not a benchmark. It was a scream of frustration from someone who had watched the software industry accelerate into a brick wall — and then celebrate the speed.

The question at the center of it all is deceptively simple. If AI has solved coding, why does software keep getting worse?

The author, who goes by ptrchm, is careful to say this is not an anti-AI rant. The essay opens with the observation that we are in the middle of an "AI-induced mass psychosis." Developers are literally *tokenmaxxing* themselves into hospital beds, scrambling to capture market value before everything is automated away. Programmers are being laid off left and right. We have been told repeatedly that AI will write one hundred percent of the code by the end of the year. Whether that is true or not, sitting back is not an option.

And yet.

## A Week in the Life of Broken Software

Ptrchm lists a few examples from a single week — all of them, they note, from software they pay for. Not edge cases. Not theoretical bugs. Daily software that millions of people rely on.

The banking app requires, on average, three FaceID logins before the 3D Secure confirmation view appears. Three. For a payment flow that used to take one tap.

Slack on macOS, after bouncing in the dock for a few seconds, finally opens its window and steals focus from Ghostty mid-keystroke. The result? A `git pull` command sent to a group chat. This is not a Slack bug in the traditional sense. It is a macOS focus-management failure amplified by an app that refuses to respect the window manager. Two teams. Zero accountability.

The LG fridge started making weird sounds, so the author tried to file a warranty claim through a multi-step form. Countless fields. Submission error at the very end. The only way to discover the failure? Open the JavaScript console.

Then there is the car. The infotainment system received a software update. It was never great, ptrchm admits, but at least it did not reboot itself during every drive. Now it does. The turn-signal sound randomly goes silent until the OS reboots. Tap Google Maps, get the radio app instead. A one-to-two-second lag before anything responds after a screen tap. These are not cosmetic issues. At highway speed, a two-second lag on a navigation tap is a safety problem.

A few months earlier, the author saw a LinkedIn thread by a product manager on the team that redesigned the car's operating system. They were congratulating themselves on what an amazing job they had done. Ptrchm says they keep thinking about that post every time they have to fight the product.

## The Incentive Structure Is the Bug

The essay's sharpest insight is not about technology. It is about economics.

Software vendors have long been KPI-oriented, and making things more stable does not always have a direct effect on the numbers. A product manager cannot walk into a quarterly review and announce: *"This quarter, we will not be releasing any new features, and we have no plans to redesign anything — we will exclusively focus on fixing bugs."* That does not look exciting in a slide deck. It does not drive engagement metrics. It does not generate LinkedIn congratulations.

What does look exciting is velocity. Features shipped per sprint. Lines of code generated by an AI agent. The ability to move ten times faster by convincing yourself that the code does not matter anymore. Companies are optimizing for the speed of the loop: write, ship, break, patch, repeat. The faster the loop, the better the quarterly numbers. The quality of the output is someone else's problem — usually the user's.

Ptrchm argues that LLMs can be genuinely good at squashing bugs if given the chance. But they are not given the chance. They are given the chance to generate new features, new redesigns, new frameworks. The bug-squashing is an afterthought, and when it happens, it is often done by the same AI that introduced the bug in the first place, in a recursive cycle of sloppiness that no one has time to audit.

A Hacker News commenter captured the recursive trap with disturbing precision. They described agent-judged results from an agent-built evaluation, derived from real work, and clearly part of a larger document. "This kind of slop is kind of useful," they wrote, "but Opus 4 was the first generation that was any good at writing its own prompts, evals, and rubrics, so there's a certain sloop to it." When AI models evaluate themselves using benchmarks they helped generate, the quality signal becomes noise. The metrics look good on dashboards. The software stays broken.

## The Nostalgia Trap

Some commenters on Hacker News pushed back with the nostalgia argument. Was software really better in the past? Wasn't the macOS Snow Leopard era just selective memory?

Ptrchm concedes the point. Software may have been better back in the day, but that was mainly because it was much simpler. Since then, we have kept coming up with new abstractions, new frontend frameworks, and more infrastructure complexity. The bar for user experience has kept rising, but everything has become increasingly fragile.

The author reaches a conclusion that resonated deeply in the comments: *"We have reached a point where an update to macOS — or to any app I rely on, really — is a source of dread rather than excitement. I now expect the new version to be worse."*

That sentence hit a nerve because it is true for millions of people. Updates used to mean new features, improvements, things getting better. Now they mean new telemetry, new dark patterns, new AI features you never asked for, and the creeping certainty that something you rely on will break in a way you cannot predict.

One commenter on Hacker News described the feeling perfectly: *"I remember when OS vendors shipped à la carte patches. You could pick the ones you wanted."* Another added that the only software category where they still look forward to updates is games, because games are relatively small purchases, paid for directly by the user, and expected to be enjoyable. Most other software fails at least one of those tests, reducing the ability to shop around and make an informed decision. When you cannot choose, you cannot discipline the market.

## The Parallel Debate: Software Factories

The same day the essay went viral, TLDR.tech's daily briefing highlighted another related piece: a forty-minute read on why software factories fail. The argument there is similar at the structural level. No amount of harness engineering or *loopsmaxxing* can solve what is fundamentally a model-training issue. Companies can move ten to one hundred times faster by convincing themselves that code does not matter anymore, but they could also embrace their constraints and move two to three times faster, safely.

The software factory debate and the "nothing works" essay are two sides of the same coin. One is about the organisational fantasy of infinite speed. The other is about the user reality of infinite fragility. Both point to the same conclusion: the industry has optimized for throughput over outcome.

## What Comes Next?

Ptrchm ends the essay on an unexpectedly optimistic note. As companies collectively spiral into what the author calls "AI debt," individual developers have a unique opportunity to build software that would previously have been beyond their reach. The frustration itself becomes the fuel for rebellion.

The author points to movements like Omarchy as early signs of that rebellion — developers and users who are choosing to reject the current state of macOS and Windows and build alternatives that prioritize stability, simplicity, and user control over engagement metrics and AI integration.

It is too early to say whether that rebellion will scale. The gravitational pull of the incumbent platforms is immense. But the essay's closing sentiment is powerful: *"I have no hope for my car's Android Auto or LG's stupid website — but I choose to believe that everyday software will get better as a result of this frustration."*

## Why This Matters Now

The timing of the essay is not accidental. In the first half of 2026, we have seen Claude Opus 5, Kimi K3, GPT-5.6, and a cascade of other models that can generate, refactor, and debug code with staggering competence. The capability ceiling of AI coding has never been higher. The average quality of the software produced with those tools has never been lower, at least in the mass-market products that most people interact with daily.

But the Hacker News thread revealed something deeper than frustration. It revealed a pricing anxiety that is shaping how developers choose tools. One engineer reported that Kimi K3 blew through a one-hundred-dollar budget at eleven percent completion on a security auditing benchmark, burning tokens at a rate that felt "terrifyingly expensive." Another compared subscription plans across providers, noting that OpenAI's twenty-dollar plan feels "one hundred times more generous" than competitors'. A third argued the opposite: that Claude Pro delivers over two hundred and fifty dollars of API-equivalent usage for twenty dollars monthly. The disagreement itself is the point. Developers are not choosing tools based on quality. They are choosing based on which pricing model least punishes their anxiety.

This economic dimension amplifies the quality problem. When every token costs money, the incentive is to ship faster to justify the burn rate. When every subscription tier feels like a gamble, the incentive is to optimize for demo polish over structural soundness. The AI tools are not the problem. The incentives they create are.

## The Deeper Paradox

The essay is not a call to abandon AI. It is a call to recalibrate incentives. To remember that the goal of software engineering is not to ship the maximum number of features per sprint. It is to build things that work reliably for the people who depend on them.

Until that becomes the metric that matters, nothing will work — and everyone will stay euphoric.
