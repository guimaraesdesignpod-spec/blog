# The Hidden Cost of the AI Boom: Why Tech Workers Are Burning Out in 2026

Alec Collon is not a doomer. He is not an AI skeptic, a Luddite, or a performance artist pretending to be outraged for clicks. He is a software engineer who uses Claude Code at work and Codex at home, who reads LLM output thoroughly before accepting it, and who considers himself "average" by current developer standards. Last week he published a short blog post with a title that landed like a quiet confession in a room full of people pretending they felt fine: **"I think I have LLM burnout."

The post went straight to the top of Hacker News. It gathered hundreds of comments. And it opened a valve on a conversation that the tech industry has been avoiding for months: what if the productivity miracle has a psychological price tag?

## The Shape of the Exhaustion

Collon describes his days with the flat precision of someone who has been observing himself from a distance. He spends hours each day interacting with LLMs. His job has shifted from designing and writing code to designing code, describing the design to an LLM, reviewing what the LLM produces, and then writing the final pieces himself. When he is not working with Claude to create tooling, he is sifting through the output of an unsupervised agent. Either way, he is reading LLM content. Probably every single day.

And lately, something has shifted. "Some small part of me has started to dread reading LLM output because I know what I am going to find," he writes. "False assumptions and hallucinations. Emphatic, staccato fragments. Sparkles and excessive emojis. It is not just me — these are real patterns."

The frustration is not that the tool is bad. It is that the tool is *consistent*. LLMs write in the same style. They make the same kinds of mistakes. "Dealing with the same thing over and over is wearing me out," Collon says. He does not know how to fix the feeling. He just knows it is there.

The Hacker News thread that followed was unusually vulnerable for that forum. One commenter said they did not have burnout per se, but LLMs were exhausting because of the pressure they generate. "At every moment there is always something ready, done by my clankers or clankers of other people that I could be unblocking." Another described receiving a zip file with thirty LLM-generated documents from a team member and being asked to review them immediately. "They are able to generate this stuff way faster than we can review." A third noted that their company requires token leaderboards and time-spent measurements as part of performance reviews, so they have to carve out time to "go do AI to show token use" — time taken away from actual productive work.

This is not the future we were sold.

## The Workforce Splits in Two

If Collon's post was the anecdote, Lenny Rachitsky's annual survey is the data. Rachitsky, who publishes one of the most-read newsletters in product management, ran a large-scale survey of how tech workers feel about their jobs in 2026. Last year's headline was "burned out, but optimistic." This year's is darker: **a tale of two workforces**.

The survey found that tech workers have sorted themselves into two camps. Forty-nine percent feel "amplified" by AI — they can do more, and better. Fourteen percent feel "destabilized" — less sure where they stand or what is really theirs. Five percent feel "diminished" — less essential, less valuable. The rest are somewhere in between, redefining their roles without knowing if the change is good or bad.

When Rachitsky's team ran the numbers, the AI-identity question turned out to be the single strongest predictor of how someone feels about their career — stronger than role, seniority, company size, or anything else they measured. The gap between "amplified" and "diminished" workers on optimism is roughly three times larger than the famously strong "founder effect" that makes startup founders the happiest people in tech. It is, by a wide margin, the biggest effect in the dataset.

And the numbers are moving in the wrong direction. Significant burnout jumped from 44.7% to 55.7% in one year. Career optimism fell from 54.8% to 48.7%. More than half of respondents — 53% — would steer a newcomer away from their field, even though many are optimistic about their own future. The industry, in their own words, is "chaotic."

Eighty-two percent say AI is making them measurably more productive. But the productivity is coming with a cost. The survey found that the most common fear is not losing a job to AI. It is being expected to do more for the same pay (51%), getting trapped in an unsustainable pace (46%), and watching the quality of work decline (41%).

The machine is not replacing the worker. It is squeezing more out of them.

## The Intensification Effect

This is not speculative. Researchers from UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business analyzed the digital activity of over ten thousand workers and found that when people adopted AI, their work life became *more* intense, not less. Time spent on email, messaging, and chat apps more than doubled. Use of business software rose by 94 percent. Workers started taking on tasks they had previously outsourced because coding and engineering became easier. They squeezed in work during evenings, weekends, and waiting rooms. They multitasked, supervising multiple bots simultaneously.

The Atlantic summarized the finding with a headline that should have been on every manager's desk: **AI is making people work more, not less.**

This is the productivity paradox of 2026. AI is undeniably a powerful tool. It can draft documents, generate code, summarize research, and automate routine tasks in minutes rather than hours. But in most organizations, those minutes of saved time are not being returned to the worker as rest. They are being redeployed as additional output expectations. The same person now does what used to be two people's work, or three, and the only thing that changed is the tool.

## The "Coworker" Problem

There is a subtler danger that compounds the exhaustion. As AI agents become more capable, companies have started marketing them as digital colleagues. Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have all released tools oriented toward managing teams of AI agents. Many are advertised as having the flexibility and cognitive power of actual humans.

This framing is not harmless branding. Research from Boston University business professor Emma Wiles, reported in MIT Technology Review, found that people catch **18% fewer errors** when AI work is described as coming from an "AI employee" rather than a chatbot. They also see themselves as **less responsible** for the output and are **44% more likely** to escalate questionable work to a manager rather than fixing it themselves. Nearly a third of the 1,261 managers Wiles surveyed said their companies already frame AI agents as employees. Twenty-three percent list them on org charts.

The problem is accountability inversion. When you treat a tool as a colleague, you treat yourself as its manager rather than its operator. You stop catching errors because you assume "Alex" is competent. You stop feeling ownership because "Alex" did it. And when something goes wrong, the blame bounces around the org chart until it lands on a human who was never really in control.

This matters beyond office culture. As agents are embedded into health care, warfare, education, and government, there is a growing risk they will become a convenient place to dump blame for failures that are actually the product of bad human decisions. Recall how the bomb strike on a girls' school in Iran was initially blamed on Claude, when reporting later showed it was a cascade of human errors.

## Why We Cannot Just Log Off

The obvious response to all of this is to use AI less. Set boundaries. Preserve analog work. Resist the loop. But the structural pressure makes individual opt-out difficult.

In the Hacker News thread, one developer noted that the more your codebase follows best practices, the better AI performs — because the patterns are predictable and the context is clean. Another said they had not written a single line of code since February and yet their team shipped more features at higher quality. The benefits are real, and they are competitive. If your competitors are using AI to ship ten times faster, opting out is not a lifestyle choice. It is a career risk.

Armin Ronacher, creator of Flask and now building the coding agent Pi, made this point in his viral essay "The Coming Loop": even if you do not use agent loops to build your software, attackers will use loops against it. Security researchers will flood you with AI-generated vulnerability reports. Competitors will out-build you with tiny teams orchestrating machines. The pressure to loop is structural.

The same logic applies to knowledge work. Even if you personally set boundaries, your manager, your teammates, and your industry may not. The speed is contagious.

## What to Do About It

So what is the answer?

For individuals, there are small, concrete steps. Collon mentions using personalization features if the interface offers them — changing the tone, the verbosity, the patterns — so the output feels less like the same voice on repeat. Others in the Hacker News thread suggested deliberately preserving creative, variable work that LLMs cannot do well: architecture decisions, user research, design taste, strategic thinking. The goal is not to reject AI but to protect the parts of the job that give it meaning.

For organizations, the fixes are harder because they require giving up the metrics that make managers feel in control. Stop measuring AI usage with token leaderboards and time-spent measurements. Stop framing agents as "coworkers" or "employees." Redesign workflows so that AI genuinely reduces workload rather than inflating it. If a task that used to take four hours now takes one, let the worker have three hours back — or at least two.

MIT economist Daron Acemoglu, who won the Nobel Prize in 2024, put it plainly: "AI agents right now are being marketed as things that can replace humans, and I think that is just a losing proposition. They should instead be optimized so that they can improve human capabilities."

The technology is not going away. The productivity gains are real. But the story of 2026 is not just about how fast we can build. It is about whether the humans doing the building can survive the speed.

Collon ends his post with a small, honest sentence: "For now, I will grit my teeth and hope I do not lose my lunch." He is not alone. And the fact that so many people recognized themselves in his confession suggests this is not a personal problem. It is an industry-wide reckoning that has only just begun.
