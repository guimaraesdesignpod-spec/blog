# The AI Observatory Found What the Labs Don't Want You to See

Here is a uncomfortable thought experiment. Imagine a pharmaceutical company that tests its own drugs, publishes only the results that make its products look effective, and then regulators write policy based on those self-published reports. No independent trials. No peer review. Just the company's word that everything is fine.

That is, more or less, where we are with AI right now.

Every few months, Anthropic releases its Economic Index. OpenAI publishes reports on how people use ChatGPT. The numbers look impressive — millions of conversations analyzed, colorful charts, careful language about "augmentation" versus "automation." But there is a catch that no one likes to talk about: these companies only show us the data they want us to see. They control the datasets, define the filters, and set the narrative. When a lab tells you that 70% of ChatGPT use is work-related, or that Claude is mostly a coding assistant, you are not getting the full picture. You are getting the picture the company chose to frame.

Enter the AI Observatory. Led by Anka Reuel at Stanford and Shayne Longpre from MIT, this project did something radical: it looked at real AI conversations from seven independent datasets, annotated 24,521 of them under a unified taxonomy of 145 features, and compared what it found to the carefully curated company reports. The results are not flattering for the industry. They are, however, exactly what independent research should look like.

## The Invisible Half

The first finding is the big one. When the AI Observatory researchers applied Anthropic's own occupational filtering method — the same approach used in its widely cited Economic Index — to their pooled dataset, nearly half of all conversations vanished. **Forty-eight percent** were classified as non-occupational and discarded.

That is not a rounding error. That is half the story being thrown in the trash.

The filtered-out conversations were not trivial. They were more likely to involve health and relationships — 44.2% versus 31.2% in Anthropic's work-focused analysis. They contained more adult or illicit topics — 7.9% versus 2.1%. They had substantially more harassment and hate speech — 27.5% versus 5.66%. And they included more sexual content — 16.7% versus 2.4%.

OpenAI's 2025 report on ChatGPT usage tells a similar story. The company found that only 30% of consumer use was work-related. Seventy percent was personal, educational, or social. Yet somehow, the industry conversation about AI's impact focuses almost entirely on productivity, coding, and workplace automation. The personal half of AI use — the messy, complicated, sometimes harmful half — is treated as an afterthought.

Anthropic has published separate blog posts about companionship use and even about Claude generating CSAM. But as David Widder, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin, told MIT Technology Review: "Having bird's-eye-view analysis rather than leaving that information sectioned off into a separate report helps researchers understand the different uses more consistently." In other words, context matters. When sensitive uses are buried in standalone posts, they do not inform the broader picture of what AI actually is in people's lives.

## Different Models, Different Cultures

One of the most striking findings from the AI Observatory is that AI use is not uniform. It differs significantly across models, and those differences tell a story about how platform design shapes behavior.

**Grok**, xAI's model integrated with X, became a magnet for news and politics. The Observatory found that Grok conversations skewed heavily toward information retrieval on current events. The problem? That is also where misinformation concentrated. The researchers noted this aligns with other studies showing how readily false information proliferates on Grok. xAI did not respond to requests for comment.

**Claude**, by contrast, attracted a very different crowd. Anthropic's model was the go-to platform for coding and technical tasks. If you wanted to debug a Python script or architect a system, Claude was your assistant. The Observatory's data confirms what many developers already suspected: Claude has become the engineer's model.

**Gemini** drew users interested in social and roleplay interactions. Google's model became the space for creative, conversational, and sometimes fantastical exchanges. Meanwhile, **ChatGPT** dominated homework assistance — a pattern that has held since the early days of GPT-3.5 and only intensified with newer versions.

There were even differences between versions of the same model. Conversations with GPT-3.5-powered ChatGPT were shorter and more transactional. Conversations with GPT-4o were longer, more iterative, and more emotionally involved — a pattern that makes sense given what we know about GPT-4o's voice mode leading to emotional addiction in some users.

"No single company report tells the whole story," Longpre told MIT Technology Review. And they cannot. OpenAI cannot see what happens on Claude. Anthropic cannot see what happens on Gemini. Each lab's report is a self-portrait, not a landscape.

## The Data Power Problem

The Observatory's dataset is impressive for an academic project: 24,521 conversations, 85,633 turns, seven sources, 52 models, spanning 2023 to 2025. But the researchers are refreshingly honest about its limitations. These conversations came from users who voluntarily shared their data. That almost certainly underrepresents the most sensitive uses — the ones people are least likely to disclose.

Compare that to the company data. Anthropic's latest Economic Index analyzes 1 million Claude conversations. OpenAI's usage report covers 1.5 million ChatGPT conversations. The scale difference is massive. But scale without independence is not necessarily better. As Anka Reuel put it: "There is no independent source to corroborate it."

This is the core problem. AI companies do not share their raw conversation data with independent researchers. They publish aggregate findings that reflect their research teams' specific questions and interests. An Anthropic representative told MIT Technology Review that the company's research reflects its teams' questions and that supporting external research is important. OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment at all.

The result is a paradox. We are making some of the most consequential technology policy decisions in history — about safety standards, deployment rules, liability frameworks, and national security — based on data that is proprietary, unverifiable, and almost certainly incomplete.

"When we want to ask, for example: is Anthropic's general-purpose AI system used mostly for good or mostly for bad, we don't have a way of answering that question because that information is proprietary," Widder explained. That should terrify anyone who cares about evidence-based policymaking.

## What Is Actually Changing Over Time

The Observatory's temporal analysis adds another layer that company reports rarely capture: how usage patterns evolve.

Conversations are getting longer and more elaborate. Prompt tokens, response tokens, and turns per conversation all increased between 2023 and 2025. Users are not just asking one-off questions anymore. They are engaging in sustained dialogues, iterating on ideas, and building relationships with their AI assistants.

Small talk increased over time, suggesting that AI companionship is growing as a use case. Meanwhile, the rate at which AI assistants disclosed that they were chatbots decreased — meaning users were either forgetting they were talking to machines, or the models were becoming better at maintaining the illusion.

Sensitive exchanges — those involving potentially harmful or restricted content — became less frequent over time. That might suggest platforms are deploying more effective safeguards, or it might reflect changing user behavior, or both. The data cannot fully distinguish.

What it can tell us is that static snapshots of AI use become obsolete quickly. Any policy framework built on 2023 assumptions is already behind the curve.

## The Bigger Picture

The AI Observatory is not the first project to raise concerns about AI data transparency. But it is one of the most systematic attempts to build an independent alternative. The platform is public. Its methods are documented. Its datasets will be available to other researchers. That is how science is supposed to work.

The uncomfortable question is whether it can keep up. The big labs are running ahead with proprietary datasets orders of magnitude larger. Their models are getting more capable, more embedded in daily life, and more opaque. Meanwhile, the policy machinery is grinding forward — the EU AI Act, US executive orders, national security reviews — based largely on what the companies choose to reveal.

Reuel's hope is that the Observatory will expand over time, adding more datasets and more models. Ideally, she says, AI companies would share their data with independent researchers in privacy-protecting ways. But as it currently stands, anyone making decisions based on AI usage data risks "completely operating in the wild and making these really consequential decisions without knowing what's actually happening beyond those company narratives."

That is not a research gap. That is a governance crisis wearing research clothing.

If you are a developer, a policymaker, or just someone who uses these tools daily, the lesson is clear: do not trust a single source. Not OpenAI's report. Not Anthropic's index. Not even this article. The truth about how AI is reshaping human behavior is distributed across millions of private conversations, and right now, almost none of the people who need to see it can.

The AI Observatory is a start. But a telescope pointed at half the sky is still only half a telescope.
