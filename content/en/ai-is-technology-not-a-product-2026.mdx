# AI Is Technology, Not a Product: Why the Apple Way Still Beats the AI Hype

Last week, Steven Levy published a provocative essay in *Wired* arguing that Apple's incoming CEO must "launch a killer AI product" or risk irrelevance. Levy painted a near-future where your phone becomes optional: you tell an always-on AI agent to hail a ride, and a car is waiting outside before you even ask. By the end of the decade, he warned, "There's an app for that" may be replaced by "Let the agent do that."

The article made waves on Hacker News, racking up close to 400 points and hundreds of comments. But the most devastating response came from John Gruber, the longtime Apple observer behind *Daring Fireball*. In a measured but unmistakably blunt rebuttal, Gruber declared the whole premise nonsense. AI, he argued, is not a product. It is a technology. And conflating the two is exactly the kind of category error that produces billion-dollar wastes of time.

## The Fantasy of the Killer AI Product

Levy's vision is seductive. Who wouldn't want a world where friction evaporates? You finish dinner, step outside, and your ride is already there. No swiping. No tapping. No app. Just intention, materialised. It is the ultimate fantasy of Silicon Valley in the mid-2020s: smart, predictive, and entirely invisible.

Gruber's counter-argument is simple. Actual products have to be real. Actual experiences rely on actual products. In Levy's scenario, what microphone listens to your command? What device do you carry to receive confirmation? How do you track the car's distance? What screen do you watch your video on when you are bored in the back seat?

"I'd bet a pretty large sum of money," Gruber wrote, "that in 2030, when someone hails a ride-share vehicle to take them home, the most common product they'll use to do that will be their phone." Levy's agentic utopia is, in other words, pure fever-dream hype. It imagines away the hardware, the interface, and the user psychology that make technology usable in the first place.

## What Apple Actually Ships

Gruber makes his case by looking backward. The iPod was not an MP3 player. It was not a celebration of the 1.8-inch hard drive inside it. It was about music. The iPhone did define the mobile era, but Apple never tried to build a social network to capitalise on it. Social media, as a product category, was left to Meta and Google. Apple was fine with that because the way people consumed social media was on an Apple device.

"Changing the iPhone ecosystem? Sure — that's already true," Gruber wrote. "Obviating the iPhone ecosystem? I don't see it."

This is the Apple playbook in miniature. The company does not ship technologies. It ships experiences. Technologies are the invisible plumbing; the experience is what you pay for. Wi-Fi is not a product you buy from Apple, yet it is inside every single device they sell. Cellular, Bluetooth, GPS, and proprietary wireless protocols are the same. There was a time, Gruber reminds us, when Apple made no wireless products at all. Now every product uses wireless connectivity. That is what AI will look like: not a separate aisle in the store, but a sensor in everything.

## Why AI Cannot Be Ignored, Either

Gruber is careful not to let the argument slip into complacency. "Only a fool would argue that Apple can stand on the sidelines and ignore AI," he wrote. "It's very different from, say, social media." Social media is a vertical. You can ignore it as a user and as a company. AI is horizontal. Like wireless networking, it pervades everything.

The difference is architectural. Social media sits on top of the stack. AI runs through the middle of it. That means Apple has no choice but to integrate it deeply. The question is not *whether* to do AI. It is *how* to do it in a way that feels like Apple: seamless, privacy-preserving, and quietly useful. The "quietly useful" part is the hardest. It means making AI so ambient that users never think about it: sorting your photos, refining your search results, autocorrecting your prose, suggesting a reply.

Gruber explicitly compares AI to the App Store or multitouch: foundational technologies that enable products, rather than products themselves. You do not buy "multitouch" at the Apple Store. You buy an iPad.

## Meanwhile, in Cupertino: The OpenAI Alliance Frays

If Gruber's theory is correct, then the current turbulence in Apple's AI partnerships makes perfect sense. This month Bloomberg reported that OpenAI is preparing legal action against Apple, claiming the two-year-old partnership has failed to deliver expected growth. OpenAI had hoped that deep integration into Apple's app ecosystem would funnel millions of users into ChatGPT subscriptions. Instead, Apple has been slow to open its walled garden and is now preparing to let rival providers — Anthropic, Perplexity, and others — plug into Siri and iOS later this year.

From Gruber's perspective, this is not betrayal. It is platform strategy. If AI is plumbing, you do not sign an exclusive deal with one pipe supplier. You standardise the joint and let anyone connect. That is exactly what Apple did with USB-C, with HomeKit, and with its slowly opening Siri API. Apple wants to be the neutral ground where AI agents compete, not the company betting its future on a single large language model.

The implications are seismic. OpenAI valued the Apple deal as a distribution moat. If Apple treats AI as a commodity layer, that moat evaporates. Apple's moat, meanwhile, remains intact: a billion loyal users and an operating system that controls the glass they stare at all day.

## What This Means for the Rest of Us

For developers and product managers, Gruber's essay is a timely sanity check. The market is saturated with standalone AI tools: chatbots, assistants, writing apps, coding copilots, image generators. Many are impressive. Few are essential. The ones that survive will be those that embed themselves into existing workflows so deeply that users forget they are using AI at all.

Think of how Grammarly faded into the background of every text field, or how Google Photos already uses facial recognition and clustering without branding it as an "AI product." The successful pattern is not a new category. It is an old category that suddenly works better.

This also reframes the fear of disruption. If Levy is right, AI agents will replace apps, phones, and interfaces. If Gruber is right, they will simply make apps, phones, and interfaces smarter. The first scenario justifies panic and billions in speculative investment. The second justifies patient, boring integration work. History tends to reward the boring approach.

Gruber draws an analogy to the cloud computing hype of the early 2010s. "Everything will soon be in the cloud," people said. It meant everything and nothing. The reality was more modest: some workloads moved to AWS, some stayed on-premise, and most companies adopted a hybrid mix. The cloud changed computing profoundly, but it did not eliminate the need for laptops, offices, or local files. Similarly, AI will change software profoundly without eliminating the need for screens, apps, or human intention.

## The Lesson: Build for the Experience, Not the Acronym

Levy's error is a common one in technology journalism: mistaking the enabler for the endgame. In 2007, the iPhone was not celebrated because it used capacitive touch. It was celebrated because pinch-to-zoom felt like magic. The technology was incidental; the gesture was the product.

AI is in the same position today. The models are remarkable. The science is real. But the product is still missing. Until someone packages AI into an experience as obvious and frictionless as the iPod wheel or the iPhone swipe, the "killer AI product" remains a journalist's headline, not an engineer's blueprint.

Gruber's advice, though unstated, is clear: if you are building in AI, stop asking what your AI can do. Start asking what your user wants to do. Then use AI to remove steps, not add features. The goal is not an AI product. It is a product that happens to use AI.

In that quiet distinction lies the difference between the companies that will survive the bubble and the ones that will not.
