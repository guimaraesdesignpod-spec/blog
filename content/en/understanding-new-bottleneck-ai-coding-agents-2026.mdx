# Understanding Is the New Bottleneck

A few years ago, the dominant anxiety among software engineers was straightforward: *Will AI take my job?* The fear was that some future model would write perfect code from a vague prompt, rendering human programmers obsolete. That dystopia never quite arrived. Instead, something more subtle and, in many ways, more challenging has emerged.

In a presentation that has been circulating through tech circles with unusual velocity, Geoffrey Litt — a design engineer at Notion — makes a provocative claim: **the new bottleneck in software development is not writing code, but understanding it.** AI agents are now so prolific that they can generate thousands of lines of working software in minutes. The problem? We, the humans, are struggling to keep up.

Litt's argument is not that we need to slow down AI. It is that we need to get better at staying inside the creative loop — not as mere validators who stamp approvals on opaque diffs, but as active participants who genuinely comprehend the systems being built. This is a shift in mindset as much as in tooling, and it carries profound implications for how teams will build software over the next decade.

## The Velocity Trap

Let us start with the obvious. Tools like Claude Code, Cursor, and GitHub Copilot have transformed the daily experience of writing software. What once took an afternoon of careful typing and debugging can now be delegated to an agent that churns out implementations while the developer grabs lunch. The raw throughput is staggering. Entire features materialise from prompts. Refactors that once spanned sprints now finish before stand-up.

But Litt warns that this velocity comes with a hidden cost. **The loop is running faster than the speed of human understanding.** Every time an agent finishes a task, it produces a diff — a wall of additions and deletions that the developer is expected to review. Most of us read these diffs line by line, searching for obvious bugs or security issues. We treat review as a quality gate: thumbs up or thumbs down. Correct or incorrect.

The agents, meanwhile, are getting better at verifying their own work. They can run tests, check for type errors, and even spot logical inconsistencies. So if our only role is to verify correctness, we are becoming increasingly redundant. Litt's point is sharp: verification is being automated away. The value humans must provide lies elsewhere.

## Why Understanding Matters Beyond Verification

Litt offers a second, more interesting reason to understand code: **not to verify, but to participate.** A software project is never a single loop with an agent. It is dozens, hundreds, thousands of iterative loops. Each loop builds on the previous one. And at every step, the developer's understanding of the system determines the quality of the next idea.

Think of it this way: you cannot creatively evolve a codebase if you lack a rich mental model of how it works. If an agent builds a new authentication layer and you only skimmed the diff, your ability to suggest the next improvement — say, adding passkey support or fine-grained permission scopes — is severely constrained. You have lost fluency. You have become a manager of black boxes rather than an engineer of systems.

This connects directly to the concept of **cognitive debt**, a term gaining serious traction in software engineering research. Coined and developed by Margaret-Anne Storey and popularised by Simon Willison, cognitive debt describes what happens when the human theory of a system fragments or disappears entirely. The code might compile and even pass tests, but nobody on the team can explain *why* certain design decisions were made or *how* different parts are supposed to interact.

Storey recounts a vivid example from a university entrepreneurship course. Student teams were building software products with heavy AI assistance, shipping features at breakneck speed. By week eight, one team hit a wall. They could no longer make simple changes without breaking something unexpected. Their initial diagnosis was technical debt — messy code, poor architecture. But the real problem was cognitive: no one could articulate the system's theory. The shared understanding had evaporated. The code was not merely messy; it was incomprehensible to the people who were supposed to own it.

As Storey notes, this is arguably a much bigger threat than traditional technical debt. Technical debt lives in the code; cognitive debt lives in developers' minds. And as AI accelerates development, the risk of cognitive debt compounds.

## Three Techniques to Reclaim Understanding

Litt does not leave us with a diagnosis and no cure. In his presentation, he outlines three practical techniques borrowed from education and adapted for the age of AI-generated code. Each is designed to slow the loop just enough for human comprehension to catch up.

### Code Explainer Documents

The first technique is what Litt calls **code explainer documents**. Instead of reviewing a raw diff — a pile of files edited in alphabetical order with no narrative — he uses a custom tool called `/explain-diff` that generates structured, literate explanations of what changed and why.

A good explainer, Litt argues, follows principles from actual pedagogy. It teaches background information before discussing changes. It leads with intuition and concepts before dumping code. It uses interactive figures where possible. And it structures the explanation as prose that walks through changes in a sensible order, embedding code snippets where they illuminate rather than overwhelm.

The result is something you can read at a café without staring at a screen full of red and green lines. It transforms code review from a tedious hunt for bugs into an opportunity to genuinely learn what the system is doing.

### Comprehension Quizzes

The second technique is perhaps the most counterintuitive: **quizzes**. Litt embeds short comprehension tests at the bottom of his explainer documents — five questions about the change — and refuses to send code to others until he can answer them correctly. He applies the same standard when reviewing others' work.

A quiz functions as a speed regulator. When working with AI, it is easy to let the loop race ahead of your understanding. The quiz is a mechanical, almost ritualistic way of asking: *do I actually understand this?* It forces a pause. It prevents the passive acceptance of generated code. And it ensures that the human remains a full creative participant, not just a rubber stamp.

This might sound pedantic to seasoned engineers who trust their gut. But Litt's argument is that the gut becomes unreliable precisely when the volume of generated code exceeds the brain's capacity to absorb it. The quiz is a guardrail for a new era.

### Interactive Micro-Worlds

The third technique is the most ambitious: **micro-worlds**. Inspired by the visionary educator Seymour Papert, who argued that the best way to learn mathematics is to "live in Mathland" — an environment where concepts are absorbed through playful exploration — Litt suggests building interactive sandboxes that let developers inhabit and intuit how a system works.

In one example, Litt describes building a Prolog interpreter with an agent's help. Understanding the logic engine's execution was difficult from static code alone, so he collaborated with the agent to build a visual debugger. The tool let him scrub through time, inspect the stack, and see which rules were evaluated at each step. He could even leave comments for himself. The key insight: agents can write code that helps humans understand *other* code.

Another example involved migrating a personal website between frameworks. Rather than accepting an opaque script, Litt asked the agent to build a "command centre" — a UI where he could trigger each migration step manually, watching the old and new sites evolve side by side. The result was a tactile, experiential understanding that rivalled doing the migration by hand, but at a fraction of the time.

## The Team Dimension

Litt's focus is largely individual, but the understanding bottleneck becomes even more acute at the team level. When multiple developers interact with the same AI-generated codebase, shared mental models become essential. Without them, communication breaks down. Decisions become tribal — known only to whoever happened to prompt the agent that week. Onboarding new team members becomes a nightmare.

This is where collaborative tools matter. Litt notes that at Notion, agents like Claude and Cursor can run inside shared pages by default. When an agent produces a technical plan, it lives in a document that the whole team can comment on, question, and refine. The understanding is built socially, not in isolation.

The implications for engineering culture are significant. Teams will need to establish new norms: at least one human must fully understand each AI-generated change before it ships; documentation must explain *why*, not just *what*; and regular checkpoints — code reviews, retrospectives, knowledge-sharing sessions — must be treated as sacred rather than optional.

## A Deeper Philosophical Thread

There is a beautiful irony at the heart of Litt's argument. For years, the tech industry has pursued automation as a way to remove humans from tedious loops. The promise was efficiency: let the machine handle the grunt work while the human focuses on higher-level thinking. But Litt flips this framing. The point of computing, he reminds us — borrowing from Alan Kay's fifty-year-old vision — was never merely to automate. It was to **augment**.

Kay imagined computers as a new medium, superior to books, for teaching people how to think about the world. In his vision, children would play with interactive simulations and edit the code behind them to deepen their understanding of physics, mathematics, and systems. The computer was meant to be a dynamic intellectual partner, not a black-box replacement for human thought.

AI now makes creating such simulations almost trivially easy. The opportunity, Litt argues, is to use this power to help us understand the world better than we ever could before. We do not have to take ourselves out of the loop. We can get deeper into it.

## What This Means for Developers Today

If you are a developer reading this in 2026, the practical takeaway is clear. The tools you use matter less than the habits you build around them.

Start by treating every agent-generated diff as a teaching moment, not a checklist item. Ask your agent to explain the change in plain language before you review the code. Experiment with explainer documents. Try embedding a short quiz — even if you are the only one taking it. When you encounter a system you do not fully grasp, ask the agent to build you a micro-world: a visualiser, a debugger, a step-by-step command centre.

More broadly, resist the seductive narrative that your job is becoming "managing AI." Management without understanding is just delegation into a void. The developers who will thrive in the coming years are those who can maintain fluency with their systems even as the systems are increasingly built by machines.

Litt closes his presentation with a note of optimism. **If we build the right tools, we can understand the world better than we ever could before.** The bottleneck is real, but it is also solvable. The question is whether we have the discipline to slow down enough to think.

In an industry obsessed with shipping faster, that might be the most radical idea of all.
