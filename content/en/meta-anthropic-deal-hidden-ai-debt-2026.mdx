## The Two Stories That Landed on the Same Day

On July 20, 2026, two pieces of news arrived within hours of each other, and together they tell a story that is bigger than either headline alone.

The first was a Nikkei Asia investigation. After analyzing the footnotes in recent financial reports from Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle, the newspaper found that the five companies had accumulated **$1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet obligations** tied to artificial intelligence infrastructure. That number is eight times higher than it was roughly four years ago. It exceeds their combined official debt of about $1.35 trillion. And it is almost entirely invisible to anyone who only glances at the liabilities column of a balance sheet.

The second story, reported by the New York Times and picked up by outlets from LiveMint to Android Headlines, was that **Meta is in early talks to lease computing power to Anthropic** in a deal that could reach **$10 billion over two years**. Anthropic needs the capacity. Meta needs the revenue. And both companies are navigating the same landscape: a world where the supply of AI infrastructure has been built on the assumption that demand will grow exponentially forever.

Taken together, the stories raise a question that is rarely asked in the breathless coverage of model releases and benchmark scores. Who is paying for all of this? And what happens if the bill comes due before the customers arrive?

## What Hidden Debt Actually Means

The term "invisible debt" sounds dramatic, but it is not a conspiracy. It is an accounting convention.

When a company signs a long-term lease for a data center or commits to a multi-year contract for GPU supply, it does not always have to record that obligation as a liability immediately. Under current accounting standards, recognition can be deferred until the asset is delivered or the facility is completed. The obligation still exists. The company is still on the hook. But it appears in the fine print of a footnote rather than in the bold numbers at the top of the balance sheet.

This is not new. Telecom companies used similar structures during the fiber-optic boom of the early 2000s. Banks used off-balance-sheet vehicles before 2008. The mechanism is well understood. What makes the current moment different is the speed and the scale.

According to the Nikkei study, Meta's hidden debt alone stands at roughly **$420 billion**, nearly triple its official debt. Oracle's invisible obligations surged to about **$273 billion**, a thirty-fold increase in four years, much of it tied to Stargate—the AI data center project it is building with OpenAI.

These are not small numbers. They are numbers that can reshape a company's risk profile without most investors noticing until the footnotes become headlines.

## Why the Money Is Being Spent

The logic behind the spending is straightforward. Training frontier AI models requires enormous amounts of specialized hardware. Running them at scale requires even more. The companies that control the most compute have an advantage in developing the most capable models. The companies that develop the most capable models attract the most users and enterprise customers. It is a flywheel, and everyone wants to be at the center of it.

Meta has been one of the most aggressive builders. The company plans to spend as much as **$145 billion** in 2026, a figure that more than doubles the $72 billion it spent in 2025. Much of that is going into data centers, GPU clusters, and the energy infrastructure needed to power them.

The bet is that demand for AI services will grow fast enough to fill all that capacity. But Zuckerberg himself has hinted that the company may be building ahead of need. "Almost every week, there are different companies that come to us from the outside asking us about computing power that they could buy from us at some premium to what we have bought it at," he told investors in May. "We haven't done that yet because we think we have a use for the compute."

The fact that Meta is now in talks to lease that compute to Anthropic suggests the calculus is shifting.

## The Anthropic Angle

Anthropic's need is just as real as Meta's excess. The company has been growing rapidly, but like every AI lab, it is constrained by the global shortage of high-end GPUs. Training the next generation of Claude models requires clusters that are difficult to secure at any price.

The proposed deal with Meta would give Anthropic a substantial supply of compute—potentially worth $10 billion over two years—with monthly payments and the option to opt out early. That flexibility matters. It means Anthropic is not locking itself into a capital expenditure that would strain its own balance sheet. It is renting instead of buying, turning a fixed cost into a variable one.

From Meta's perspective, the deal opens a new line of business. If finalized, Meta becomes not just a consumer of AI infrastructure but a vendor of it. It is a familiar playbook. Amazon built Amazon Web Services because it had already built the infrastructure for its retail business. Meta may be stumbling into a similar model, driven by the same dynamic: build first, find customers later.

## What Could Go Wrong

The optimistic case is that demand for AI keeps rising, the capacity gets absorbed, and the hidden debt never becomes a problem because the revenue arrives before the bills do. The pessimistic case is that demand disappoints, and the deferred obligations turn into immediate liquidity crises.

A tech industry source quoted in the Nikkei report put it sharply: "A commercial real estate lease retains asset value, but AI data centers rely on semiconductors that depreciate rapidly due to fast-paced technological advances. If utilization rates drop due to weak AI demand, big tech firms will face massive asset impairment charges."

The analogy to the telecom bubble is hard to ignore. In the late 1990s, companies laid fiber-optic cable based on projections of internet traffic growth that turned out to be years ahead of reality. The cable was real. The demand eventually arrived. But many of the companies that built it went bankrupt in the gap between investment and payoff.

The difference this time is that the companies spending the money are already enormously profitable. Meta, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon have business models that generate cash. They can absorb a temporary glut in infrastructure capacity without collapsing. But they may still have to write down assets, cut spending, or raise prices in ways that ripple through the broader tech economy.

## The User's Stake

Most people reading about a $1.65 trillion debt figure or a $10 billion compute deal will reasonably ask: why does this matter to me?

It matters because the economics of the AI tools you use every day are being shaped by these decisions. If infrastructure costs keep rising and companies need to monetize aggressively, the subscription price for the AI assistant you rely on could go up. If a capacity glut develops and prices crash, the quality of service could suffer as companies cut corners to stay afloat. If the debt dynamics force consolidation—stronger companies buying weaker ones—the competitive landscape changes, and with it the range of products available to you.

The tools feel free or cheap right now because they are subsidized by venture capital, corporate profits, and, increasingly, by debt that does not show up on the balance sheet in the way most people expect. That subsidy is not infinite.

## A Pattern, Not an Incident

The Meta-Anthropic talks are not an isolated event. They are part of a pattern.

Earlier this year, Anthropic signed a massive compute deal with SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center, reportedly worth up to $45 billion over three years. Amazon Web Services CEO Matt Garman has stated that current AI infrastructure investments are "not speculative," while SoftBank's Masayoshi Son has said that calling AI a bubble is "blasphemy." At the same time, Japan's power companies are planning to build or bolster thirty substations just to meet AI demand, and even national governments are treating AI infrastructure as a matter of sovereign importance.

Everyone is building. Everyone is betting. And the bill is being deferred.

The question is not whether AI demand will grow. It almost certainly will. The question is whether it will grow fast enough, and in the right directions, to justify the infrastructure that has already been committed. If it does, the companies that built early will dominate the next decade. If it does not, the $1.65 trillion in hidden debt will stop being invisible, and the adjustments will be painful.

For now, the most honest thing anyone can say is that nobody knows. The builders are building on faith. The analysts are analyzing on hope. And the rest of us are using the products while the foundation beneath them is still being poured.
