# The Eternal Sloptember: Why George Hotz Thinks AI Agents Can't Actually Code

## The Hacker Who Cracked the iPhone Just Called AI Agents a Statistical Slot Machine

George Hotz is not someone you dismiss easily. At seventeen, he was the first person to jailbreak an iPhone. He later reverse-engineered the PlayStation 3, built comma.ai — a self-driving car startup that ran on a shoestring budget — and founded tinygrad, a deep-learning framework that competes with PyTorch while being a fraction of the size. When Hotz says something about software, the industry listens.

So when he published a blog post last weekend titled *"The Eternal Sloptember"*, it hit the front page of Hacker News like a brick through a window. Within hours, the thread had 229 upvotes and hundreds of comments. And Hotz's argument was as blunt as it was unsettling: AI agents cannot program. Not really. They are "highly sophisticated statistical models designed to mimic the distribution of programming." The output *looks* right. It often compiles. But it is broken in ways that are getting harder and harder to detect.

"It is a golden era for buckets and buckets of slop," he wrote, "and a dark age for gems of quality."

The word he coined — *sloptember* — is already sticking. It describes a future where software output explodes while average quality collapses, where large organisations produce ten times as many pull requests but the median developer no longer understands what they are shipping. Hotz is not saying AI is useless. He uses it daily as a search engine and for quick prototypes. But as a replacement for software engineers? "Not close to the bar at any company I have worked at."

The post arrived at a peculiar moment. Just days earlier, Anthropic had wrapped its *Code with Claude* conference in London, where engineers claimed that most of Anthropic's software is now written by Claude. Spotify and Monday.com presented similar case studies. The narrative from the labs is that coding is "largely solved." Hotz's post is the loudest counter-narrative yet from someone with the credibility to back it up.

## The Argument: Why Agents Can't Program

Hotz spent six months trying to make agents work. He used them to write parts of tinygrad. He used them to reverse-engineer a USB-to-PCIe chip. Every time, he suspected he could have done it better and faster by hand. The agent, he says, "frontloads all the progress, then gives you a slot machine lever to pull to hope it gets the polish done. It never quite gets there."

The deeper issue is process. Human programmers do not learn to code by ingesting billions of examples and predicting the next token. They build mental models of the world, of causality, of how systems interact. A child learns that objects persist when out of sight before they ever write a line of code. Hotz argues that this embodied understanding — what Yann LeCun and Gary Marcus have called *world models* — is what separates genuine reasoning from statistical mimicry.

Without it, he says, agents produce what looks like code but lacks the structural integrity you get from actually understanding the problem. A syntax highlight makes it *look* like software. A passing unit test makes it *look* correct. But the gap between "passes tests" and "is correct" is where the slop lives. Hotz compares the situation to AFL, the fuzzer that found bugs in software for years without anyone feeling threatened by it. AFL was a tool. It did not claim to be an engineer. Agents, marketed as replacements, are something else entirely.

He also points to a critical organisational failure mode. High-performing individuals can usually spot when an agent has produced garbage. They have error-correction instincts. But in large companies, feedback loops are slower and alignment is weaker. "The bottom performers won't have that self check. They are the ones producing 10x output with the agents." The average output of the organisation falls. The average output of the world, he implies, falls with it.

## The Science: A New Paper Confirms the Fear

If Hotz's post was the shot across the bow, a paper uploaded to arXiv on 7 May 2026 is the ballistics report.

Titled *"Constraint Decay: The Fragility of LLM Agents in Backend Code Generation,"* the paper comes from researchers at EURECOM in France and the University of Basilicata in Italy. They designed a rigorous experiment: fix a single API specification (the well-known RealWorld Conduit API), then systematically layer structural constraints on top of it. Same task, same tests, but with increasing demands on architecture, database integration, and ORM layers.

Their finding is stark. They call it **constraint decay**: as structural requirements accumulate, agent performance degrades substantially. Capable configurations lose an average of 30 points in assertion pass rates from baseline to fully specified tasks. Some weaker configurations approach zero.

The breakdown is revealing. Agents do fine in lightweight, explicit frameworks like Flask. But in convention-heavy environments like FastAPI or Django — the frameworks most production systems actually use — they fall apart. The reasons are not surface-level syntax errors. They are deep data-layer defects: incorrect query composition, ORM runtime violations, and architectural violations that unit tests miss but integration tests catch.

The paper identifies these data-layer defects as the leading root cause, driving roughly 45% of agent logic failures. In other words, agents can scaffold a route handler. They struggle to write a query that respects the ORM and the database schema at the same time. That is exactly the kind of work that separates a demo from production software.

This is not a theoretical complaint. The authors open-sourced their evaluation pipeline, 80 greenfield generation tasks, and 20 feature-implementation tasks across eight frameworks. Anyone can reproduce the result. And the result is that *jointly satisfying functional and structural requirements remains a key open challenge for coding agents* — the paper's own conclusion.

## The Debate: What Hacker News Actually Thinks

The Hacker News thread on Hotz's post is one of the most divided I have seen in 2026. The top comment, with nearly 200 points, tried to find middle ground: "Either you're a luddite or 'ai pilled'. In most cases, LLMs can get you 80-95% of the way." Another developer agreed but added a caveat: "It's 60-95% solutions, with about 120-140% of lines of code required."

Then there is the experience report that should worry anyone managing a codebase. One developer described their organisation celebrating a "step change in number of created PRs" as if it were the second coming of the personal computer. Meanwhile, senior engineers were stuck "finding insta-reject level bugs in pull requests from people one-shotting 6,000-line PRs." The developer left the company.

Another comment used a medical analogy that stuck with me. They compared AI-generated code to grey-market peptides — chemicals people inject based on anecdotes, without knowing the long-term effects. "Your codebase is the body. Literally no one knows how maintainable this approach is, because there simply hasn't been enough time to find out. It could be fine. It could be a complete mess, with your entire engineering team falling asleep at the wheel."

But the other side is equally loud. Multiple commenters reported building full-scale applications with agents, insisting that the key is "harness engineering" — investing heavily in scaffolding that constrains the agent, tests its output, and iterates until CI passes. A senior engineer wrote that with the right harness and a staff-level human making architectural decisions, one person can now ship "what a whole team could ship before."

The disagreement is not about whether agents can produce text that looks like code. Everyone agrees they can. The disagreement is about whether that output has a ceiling — and whether most organisations are equipped to notice when they have hit it.

## What This Means for Engineering Teams

The synthesis of Hotz's essay, the Constraint Decay paper, and the Hacker News debate points to a more nuanced picture than either the AI labs or the skeptics want to admit.

**AI agents are extraordinary autocomplete.** They are faster than Stack Overflow, more adaptive than documentation, and genuinely useful for scaffolding, boilerplate, and exploration. No one credible disputes this.

**But agents are not architects.** When structural constraints pile up — ORM mappings, API contracts, multi-layer clean architecture, database migrations — performance drops off a cliff. The 30-point decline measured in the paper is the scientific version of the senior engineer's instinct that the last 10% of a feature takes 90% of the effort.

**Organisations without strong review cultures will ship slop at scale.** Hotz's warning about bottom performers generating 10x output is not hypothetical. It is already happening. The companies celebrating PR velocity without measuring defect density or maintenance cost are the ones most at risk.

**The real skill is now curation, not generation.** The developers who thrive in this era are not the ones who prompt best. They are the ones who know when to trust the agent, when to rewrite from scratch, and how to build the harnesses that catch failure before it reaches production. As one HN commenter put it: "Senior engineers are finding LLMs a really useful tool — because we've learned to think about all that other stuff before opening the text editor. Writing the actual code was always the easy (and least valuable) bit."

## The Path Forward: Let It Cook, but Check the Temperature

Hotz ends his post by saying he is "now in the LeCun/Marcus camp" on LLMs. He believes the current architecture will never produce true programming agents. The path forward, he suggests, requires world models — systems that reason about causality and structure, not just probability distributions over tokens.

That may be right in the long run. In the short run, the pragmatic path is simpler: treat agents as junior developers with photographic memory and no common sense. Pair them with seniors who review every line. Invest in test harnesses that enforce structural constraints before the agent even starts. And above all, resist the incentive to celebrate output volume over output quality.

Apple is reportedly pushing AI on all its engineers. Hotz asks a pointed question: *"Do you think macOS will get better or worse in the next two years?"* The answer to that question will tell us a lot about whether the Eternal Sloptember is just a dramatic blog post or the name of an era.

For now, the evidence is split. The arXiv paper shows real, reproducible fragility. The Hacker News crowds show real, reproducible success. The difference is not the tool. It is the human holding the reins. And that, more than any model release, is what will determine whether the next decade of software is a golden age or just a very productive slop factory.

The consensus among experienced developers is that agents are powerful tools when supervised, not replacements for engineering judgment.
