# Claude Now Wants Your Passport: What Anthropic's Identity Verification Means for AI

On a Sunday afternoon in mid-June, Andrew Marble — a developer and regular Claude user — sat down to write a blog post. The title was blunt: "Cancel Claude." The reason was simpler still. Anthropic had just updated its privacy policy, and buried in the fine print was a sentence that changed everything: starting July 8, 2026, the company reserved the right to demand your government-issued photo ID, a live selfie, and a scan of your facial geometry before letting you use Claude.

Marble's post hit Hacker News like a thunderclap. Within hours it had 151 points and over a hundred comments. By the next morning, a parallel thread about the official Anthropic policy page had ballooned to 679 points and nearly 600 comments. The consensus was not subtle. Users were furious.

The anger was not about one feature, one model, or one price hike. It was about a feeling that had been building for months: that the open, permissive era of frontier AI was ending, and something more controlled, more surveilled, and more gated was taking its place.

## The Policy Change Nobody Asked For

Anthropic's updated privacy policy went live quietly. The key paragraph is worth reading in full:

> "In certain circumstances, we may ask you to verify your age or identity. If you choose to do so, data we will collect includes, depending on the method: an image of your government-issued identity document and the information appearing on it (such as your ID number and date of birth); your image in photo or video form, facial geometry templates (which may be considered 'biometric data' in some jurisdictions); and the result of the verification."

The policy specifies that this applies to consumer accounts — Free, Pro, and Max. Enterprise and Team plans are excluded, at least for now. But for individual developers, researchers, and hobbyists who have built workflows around Claude, the implications are immediate and unsettling.

There is no published list of what triggers a verification request. Anthropic says it may happen when accessing certain capabilities, as part of routine platform integrity checks, or for "other safety and compliance measures." In plain English: they can ask whenever they want, for whatever reason they decide, and if you refuse, you may lose access.

The data is processed by Persona Identities, a San Francisco-based verification vendor. Anthropic stresses that it does not store ID images or selfies on its own servers. Persona handles the collection and storage. Anthropic can access verification records through Persona's platform when necessary — for appeals, for example — but claims it does not copy or independently store the raw biometric data.

That distinction matters legally. Whether it matters psychologically is another question.

## Who Is Persona, and Why Does It Matter?

Persona is not a household name, but it is rapidly becoming one in the world of online identity verification. The company provides automated ID checks and biometric facial matching for platforms ranging from startups to major social networks. It is backed by Founders Fund, the venture capital firm co-founded by Peter Thiel.

That connection to Thiel is part of what has inflamed the reaction. Thiel is also the co-founder of Palantir, the data analytics company with deep contracts in US defense, immigration enforcement, and intelligence. For users already uneasy about surveillance, the idea that their passport data and facial geometry are flowing through a Thiel-linked pipeline feels less like a security measure and more like a structural risk.

The discomfort turned to alarm in February 2026, when researchers discovered that Persona's front-end code — roughly 53 megabytes of it — had been left exposed on a public Google Cloud server linked to FedRAMP, the US government's cloud security authorization program. Discord, which had been testing Persona for age verification, promptly ended the partnership. Both companies confirmed the trial had lasted less than a month and involved only a small number of users. But the breach underscored a reality that privacy advocates have long warned about: biometric data is a high-value target, and even vendors serving critical infrastructure make elementary mistakes.

Anthropic's choice of Persona, in the wake of that incident, struck many observers as careless at best and cynical at worst. On Reddit's ClaudeAI forum, the top-voted comment in the announcement thread was a single word: "Absolutely not."

## The Citizenship Filter

If the privacy implications are concerning, the access implications are arguably worse.

The timing of the policy change is impossible to ignore. It comes less than two weeks after the Trump administration ordered Anthropic to restrict access to its Fable and Mythos models under export control rules. Non-US users suddenly found themselves locked out of the most capable AI systems Anthropic had ever built. The identity verification policy, effective July 8, arrives in the shadow of that ban.

For international users, the connection feels obvious. One Hacker News commenter, a non-US citizen, put it plainly: "Opus 4.8 is the best American LLM I will ever have access to. That's no longer up for debate. Each month that I pay Anthropic is now a depreciating value. Adding US-based identity verification through Persona is also incredibly off-putting. I think it's sufficient to kill my use of Claude altogether."

The fear is not that Anthropic will explicitly reject non-Americans. The fear is that the combination of export controls, US-centric identity verification, and opaque platform policies will create a de facto citizenship gate. If your government ID is not from a country Persona recognizes, or if your facial geometry does not match a document the system accepts, or if the appeal process requires navigating English-language support forms from a different timezone, you are functionally excluded — even if no one ever says so explicitly.

Anthropic's support page lists "most countries" as acceptable for government-issued IDs, but offers no exhaustive list. It notes that passports and driver's licenses are common examples. For users in regions with less standardized ID infrastructure, the uncertainty alone is a deterrent.

## Why Anthropic Says It Is Doing This

Anthropic's stated rationale is straightforward: safety, security, and legal compliance.

The company says identity verification helps prevent abuse of its platform, enforce usage policies, and comply with legal obligations. The policy page emphasizes that verification data is used only to confirm identity and not for "any other purposes." It repeats, in bold, that verification data is not used to train AI models.

These are not unreasonable goals. Frontier AI models are genuinely dangerous in the wrong hands. Anthropic's own research has shown that its Mythos-class models can find hundreds of security vulnerabilities, chain low-severity bugs into working exploits, and reason about biological synthesis pathways. If a malicious actor were using Claude to accelerate cyberattacks or develop harmful materials, knowing who that actor was would be valuable.

The problem is proportionality. Verification is a sledgehammer, not a scalpel. It sweeps up legitimate researchers, privacy-conscious developers, minors who may not possess government IDs, and users in countries with weak data protection laws — all in the service of catching a hypothetical bad actor who, if sufficiently motivated, could simply use a stolen identity or a VPN to bypass the check anyway.

Security researchers have been making this point for years. Identity verification is not a substitute for robust model-level safeguards. It is a compliance checkbox that shifts risk onto users while doing little to deter sophisticated adversaries.

## The Open-Source Alternative

The most interesting consequence of the verification backlash is not the outrage itself. It is the acceleration of a migration that was already underway.

Andrew Marble's "Cancel Claude" post was not a rant. It was a technical assessment. He laid out his current workflow — what percentage of his tasks could be handled by local open-weight models, which tasks still required frontier performance, and how close the gap had become.

His conclusion: "Open models are now very close to the leaders and typically trail only by a few months. This doesn't feel like 2008 Linux versus Windows. It's much closer."

That sentiment is echoed across the community. On Hacker News, multiple commenters reported migrating from Claude to local Qwen, Mistral, and Llama variants. Some acknowledged a short-term productivity hit. Others argued that the peace of mind — no ID checks, no data retention anxiety, no risk of sudden capability restrictions — more than compensated for the performance delta.

The argument is structural. Proprietary AI services are converging on a predictable pattern: launch open, accumulate users, tighten controls, raise prices, and eventually gate the best capabilities behind identity walls and geography checks. Open-weight models, by contrast, cannot revoke your access. They cannot update their privacy policy overnight. They cannot be embargoed by a single government. Once downloaded, the weights are yours.

This does not mean open models are perfect. Running them locally is expensive, complicated, and slow compared to an API call. Privacy through third-party hosts is still uncertain. And for the absolute frontier of reasoning and coding, Claude and GPT-class models retain an edge.

But the gap is closing. And for a growing cohort of developers, the question is no longer "Can open models match Claude?" It is "How close is close enough?"

## What Happens Now

July 8 is two weeks away. Anthropic has not indicated any intention to delay the policy. The company has emphasized that verification will be rolled out gradually and only for "certain capabilities," not as a blanket requirement for every login. But the privacy policy grants broad discretion, and users have learned to be skeptical of gradual rollouts that become universal norms.

In the meantime, the Hacker News threads have become a real-time experiment in collective exit. Developers are sharing Docker configs for local inference, benchmark comparisons between open and proprietary models, and tips for preserving Claude-quality workflows with self-hosted alternatives. The community is doing what communities do when trust erodes: building the replacements themselves.

For Anthropic, the reputational damage may outlast the policy itself. The company built its brand on being the "safety-first" AI lab — careful, transparent, principled. The identity verification episode has chipped away at that narrative. When your safety measures feel indistinguishable from surveillance, and your compliance posture feels indistinguishable from gatekeeping, you lose the moral high ground that differentiated you from competitors.

The deeper irony is that Anthropic's CEO, Dario Amodei, has been one of the most vocal advocates for AI regulation. He has testified before Congress, written extensively about catastrophic risk, and argued that frontier labs have a duty to self-regulate. Those positions are defensible. But they create expectations. When a lab that preaches responsibility suddenly demands your passport, users notice the asymmetry. The burden of safety is falling on individuals, not institutions.

## A Rubicon Crossed

Whether or not Anthropic's identity verification achieves its stated safety goals, it has already changed the landscape. It has normalized the idea that using a chatbot may require proving your humanity to a third-party vendor. It has established a precedent that biometric data is an acceptable price of admission for frontier AI. And it has accelerated the fragmentation of the AI ecosystem into two camps: those who accept the gate, and those who build around it.

The next few months will reveal whether this was a tactical misstep or a strategic pivot. If verification remains limited to high-risk capabilities and is implemented with genuine transparency, the backlash may subside. If it expands to general usage and is enforced arbitrarily, Anthropic may discover that the users who value privacy are also the users who are most capable of leaving.

For now, the message from the community is clear. The passport stays in the drawer. The models stay on the local machine. And the future of AI is being written by the people who refused to show their papers.
