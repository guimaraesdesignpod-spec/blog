# OpenAI’s Super PAC Is Running an AI News Site With Fake Reporters

## The Email That Felt Wrong

Nathan Calvin, vice president at the AI advocacy group Encode, received a press inquiry last week that made him pause. The sender identified himself as Michael Chen, a reporter for a publication called *The Wire by Acutus*. The email asked for comment on an AI bill in Tennessee. The full headline was already written. The format offered was written Q&A only. And something about the framing felt aggressively loaded, like a legal complaint dressed up as journalism.

Calvin did what anyone in his position would do. He searched for Michael Chen. Nothing. He searched for Acutus. A sparse website with no masthead, no bylines, no named editors, and an about page that described the operation as a collaborative journalism platform. The email address was generic: reporter@acutuswire.com. When Calvin forwarded the message to Tyler Johnston, executive director of The Midas Project, Johnston ran it through Pangram, an AI content detector. The result: fully AI-generated.

Michael Chen, it turns out, almost certainly does not exist. And Acutus, it turns out, almost certainly does not employ any real journalists at all.

## The Machine Behind the Curtain

What Johnston uncovered over the next several days is one of the most brazen examples of automated astroturfing in the short history of generative AI. *The Wire by Acutus* launched on December 29, 2025. In less than eight months, it has published 94 articles covering AI policy, pharmacy reform, nuclear energy, crypto regulation, and multiple 2026 Republican Senate campaigns. The site claims to offer expert-sourced independent journalism. The reality, according to Johnston’s investigation published in *Model Republic*, is a fully automated content factory with no humans in the editorial loop.

The website is a React application, and its client-side JavaScript contains what Johnston describes as an internal editorial dashboard sitting in plain sight. Fields visible in the code include AI Background Context, described as background information for the AI to use when generating questions and writing the story. Another field, Question Prompts, contains suggested questions for the AI interviewer to ask. A large button reads Generate Story Draft. After generation, a Regenerate button allows operators to rerun the pipeline if the output is unsatisfactory.

But the real smoking gun is the API. The endpoint acutuswire.com/api/wire, accessible in any browser, returns not just finished articles but the complete internal production record for each piece. That record includes a multi-pass editorial review conducted by AI, scored across categories like AP style compliance, quote accuracy, source verification, and a catch-all metric simply labeled acutus. The AI flags issues, proposes corrections, and logs resolutions with timestamps.

The timestamps are damning. Within a single article, the entire review completes in a median of 44 seconds. Publication follows a median of 10 seconds later. Of the 94 stories in the database, 42 carry an automated overall status of needs_revision. Every single one of them was published anyway.

## Interviewing Humans With Software

If the writing is automated, where do the quotes come from? Some are lifted from previously published web content, consistent with standard LLM scraping. But at least some come from real people who were approached by what they thought was a human reporter.

Harvard Business School professor Joseph Fuller wrote on LinkedIn that he was pleased to share his thoughts with Acutuswire, a production of a number of independent journalists. He linked to an article on skills-based hiring in which he was quoted. He had no idea he was corresponding with software. Nathan Calvin, the original recipient of the Michael Chen email, was also asked for comment by what appears to be an AI agent posing as a reporter.

The client-side code contains references to an AI interviewer and a reporter agent. Default text for uploaded interviews reads: No interviews uploaded via the reporter API yet. Interviews submitted by the agent will appear here for review. In other words, when Acutus needs a quote from a living expert, it sends a bot. The human expert answers in good faith. The bot transcribes. The article is generated. The human is never told they were interviewed by a machine.

Some articles also cite unnamed Republican Senate staffers and strategists close to Senate leadership. These extremely convenient anonymous quotes align neatly with whichever stakeholder the piece favors, suggesting they may be fed directly into the AI during story generation rather than gathered through any journalistic process.

## The OpenAI Connection

Acutus has almost no public profile. Google searches return almost nothing beyond the site itself. On X, the site has been linked only four times, by relatively obscure accounts. But two of those four posts were written or retweeted by Patrick Hynes, president of a Republican PR firm called Novus Public Affairs.

Novus lists Targeted Victory among its clients. Targeted Victory is a GOP consulting firm whose CEO, Zac Moffatt, co-founded Leading The Future, a $125 million super PAC backed primarily by OpenAI president Greg Brockman and venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz. The PAC launched in August 2025 with the explicit goal of opposing state-level AI regulation and supporting pro-AI candidates.

The articles on Acutus read like a direct translation of that agenda. Around 15% of the site’s coverage focuses on AI policy, and the angle is consistent: cast aspersions on Anthropic, criticize blue states and red states that regulate AI, blame journalists and grassroots organizers for violence against AI companies, and frame safety advocates as dangerous extremists. One article criticizes AI safety advocate John Sherman for comments he made on his podcast, then contacts every organization listed as a client for Sherman’s consulting firm to ask whether they intended to continue working with him. Another article quotes Patrick Hynes himself, speaking on behalf of Novus, with no disclosure that his firm appears to be operating the publication that is quoting him.

Johnston’s conclusion is cautious but pointed: if he is right, OpenAI’s super PAC may be using Acutus to push its political agenda under the guise of independent journalism.

## This Is Not New, But It Is Worse

Tech companies have been astroturfing for decades. Microsoft famously created a front organization called Americans for Technology Leadership to generate letters to newspapers supporting the company during its antitrust trial. Amazon has been accused of using fake grassroots campaigns to oppose warehouse unionization. The difference now is scale, speed, and invisibility. An AI system can generate a hundred articles, conduct a hundred interviews, and publish them all in the time it used to take a human PR team to draft a single press release. And because the content is generated rather than copied, it leaves no fingerprints.

The deception is also more intimate. It is one thing to read a press release and know it came from a corporation. It is another to receive an email from someone who appears to be a journalist, to answer their questions in good faith, and to discover later that you were talking to a machine designed to make a political argument you were never shown in full.

## What This Means for Journalism, and for Trust

The journalism industry has spent the last two decades losing public trust to social media algorithms, partisan outlets, and clickbait farms. The threat of AI-generated content mills has been discussed extensively, but mostly in the abstract. Most newsrooms experimenting with AI have been careful, even timid, using it for transcription, summarization, or headline generation while keeping humans in charge of reporting and editing.

Acutus is not careful. It is the opposite of careful. It is a system designed to eliminate the human from every step of the process except the sourcing, and even there it deceives the humans it contacts. The result is content that looks like journalism, quotes real people, carries a Creative Commons license for syndication, and is entirely manufactured.

For readers, the practical implication is stark: you can no longer assume that a site with a professional layout, an RSS feed, and quoted experts is producing journalism in any meaningful sense. The visual markers of credibility — clean design, formal language, attribution — can now be generated at industrial scale by systems that have no understanding of truth, fairness, or accountability.

## What Happens Next

OpenAI has not publicly responded to the Acutus investigation. The company prohibits the use of its products for political campaigning in its usage policies, though the language in its safety framework warning about AI-generated political influence campaigns was quietly removed at some point. Whether OpenAI models were used to generate Acutus content is unknown, but the technical possibility is obvious.

The legal implications are murky. In the United States, astroturfing exists in a gray zone. The Federal Trade Commission can prosecute deceptive endorsements, and campaign finance law requires disclosure of political advertising. But a website that claims to be independent journalism while secretly pushing a corporate agenda is not straightforwardly illegal — it is just dishonest.

The deeper problem is cultural. If the tech industry responds to criticism by building fake newsrooms with fake reporters to interview real people and generate fake stories, the concept of public discourse collapses into a hall of mirrors. Every source becomes suspect. Every quote becomes questionable. And the people who actually do the work of journalism — who call sources, verify facts, and publish under their own names — find themselves competing with machines that can do none of those things but can imitate all of them.

That is the real threat of Acutus. Not that it will replace journalism, but that it will make journalism harder to distinguish from its counterfeit. And in a world where trust is already scarce, that may be the most damaging thing of all.
