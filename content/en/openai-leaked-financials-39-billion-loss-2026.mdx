# OpenAI's Leaked Financials Show a $39 Billion Loss — and the Clock Is Ticking

The document arrived quietly, passed between reporters and venture capitalists like a ghost story at a campfire. Audited financial statements for OpenAI, the world's most famous AI lab, had been leaked. And the numbers were sobering.

In 2025, OpenAI generated **$13.07 billion** in revenue. Up from $3.7 billion the year before, that figure represents growth most startups would kill for. Monthly revenues reportedly neared $2 billion by December. Over **900 million** weekly active users. About **50 million** paying subscribers. The demand is real, the brand is dominant, and the product — ChatGPT and its siblings — has become the Kleenex of generative AI.

And yet, the company lost **$38.5 billion**, maybe closer to $39 billion depending on how you count the accounting charges.

That is not a typo. Thirty-nine billion dollars. In twelve months. On thirteen billion in sales.

## The Anatomy of a Massive Loss

Let's walk through the line items because they tell a story bigger than any single headline.

**Research & Development:** **$19.18 billion**, up from $7.81 billion in 2024. This is the heart of the beast. Training frontier models — the kind that power GPT-5, Codex, and whatever comes next — requires clusters of GPUs that consume more electricity than small nations. OpenAI pays Microsoft **$10.59 billion** annually just for cloud compute services. That's not a line item; that's a gravitational field.

**Cost of Revenue:** **$7.5 billion**, versus $2.65 billion in 2024. Every API call, every ChatGPT conversation, every image generation carries inference costs. As usage scales, so does the electricity, bandwidth, and depreciation of hardware.

**Sales and Marketing:** **$5.73 billion**, a five-fold jump from $1.11 billion in 2024. Apparently you do need billboards in Portland and Super Bowl ads to remind people that the chatbot they've already heard of exists. Discounted subscriptions — perhaps booked partially as marketing — also inflate this figure.

Add it up, subtract revenue, and you get an **operating loss of $20.92 billion**. The extra $18 billion or so that brings the net loss to $39 billion is largely a one-time accounting adjustment related to OpenAI's conversion from a nonprofit to a for-profit structure — warrants, convertible interests, fair-value remeasurements. Financial engineering, not cash burn. But even stripping that away, the underlying business is bleeding roughly **$8 billion a year** in what most people would call real money.

## Why Revenue Growth Isn't Enough

Here's the uncomfortable truth for OpenAI bulls: the revenue growth is genuine, but the cost curve is steeper.

In 2024, revenue was $3.7 billion and expenses were roughly $12.5 billion. In 2025, revenue more than tripled to $13.1 billion — but expenses nearly tripled too, hitting $34 billion. The ratio barely budged. If your expenses grow in lockstep with your revenue, you are not on a path to profitability. You are on a treadmill that accelerates.

The company has told investors it aims to be profitable by **2030**. That gives it five years. But five years is an eternity in technology, especially when competitors are circling.

Anthropic, for instance, has reportedly spent less on R&D than OpenAI while forecasting comparable revenue, and rumors suggest it could reach profitability sooner. Meta pours billions into AI but subsidizes it with an advertising empire that prints cash. Google has search margins to absorb DeepMind's burn. Apple sells hardware. Microsoft sells clouds. Everyone else has a cushion. OpenAI, despite its name, is increasingly just one more company trying to monetize intelligence before the bill collectors arrive.

## The IPO Trap

OpenAI has confidentially filed for an initial public offering, seeking a valuation between **$850 billion and $1 trillion**. That would make it one of the largest tech IPOs in history, rivaling the debuts of Alibaba and Facebook.

But IPOs are valuation events, not validation events. And public-market investors are notably less patient than SoftBank or Thrive Capital.

SaaS companies historically trade at multiples of revenue based on expected margins. A fast-growing software firm with a path to 25% operating margins might command 10–15× revenue. OpenAI, at $850 billion on $13 billion revenue, is trading at **65× revenue**. That only makes sense if investors believe the company can eventually generate margins that justify the premium. Current numbers suggest the opposite: margins are deeply negative, and the cost structure is tied to semiconductor physics, not software economics.

Moreover, OpenAI faces a demand problem disguised as a supply problem. **Enterprise customers are beginning to balk at token-based pricing.** A Fortune 500 company spending millions on API calls wants a line-item justification: which workflows improved, which headcount was reduced, which revenue was generated. So far, those answers remain murky. Studies from early 2026 suggested that a majority of CEOs saw zero measurable ROI from AI spending. If that perception hardens, revenue growth could stall even as infrastructure costs climb.

## What This Means for the AI Industry

OpenAI's financials are not just OpenAI's problem. They are a proxy for the entire foundation-model economy.

The prevailing narrative of the last three years went something like this: scale compute, scale data, scale parameters, and eventually the models become so capable that monetization becomes trivial. The leaked documents suggest the opposite scaling law: scale compute, scale expenses, scale marketing, and hope nobody asks about the bottom line.

This has several implications.

First, **pricing power may shift to cloud providers.** OpenAI's largest supplier is Microsoft, which controls the GPUs, the datacenters, and the Azure platform. If OpenAI becomes dependent on Microsoft's infrastructure while simultaneously competing with Microsoft's Copilot products, the power asymmetry becomes dangerous. Every dollar OpenAI pays Microsoft is a dollar that strengthens a rival ecosystem.

Second, **open-source and local inference become existential threats.** Models like Llama, Qwen, Gemma, and Mistral are not yet as capable as GPT-5 at the frontier, but they are improving fast. More importantly, they are free or cheap to run. A company that pays $2 million a month for OpenAI API calls can slash that cost by 80% or more by switching to self-hosted open weights. The capability gap narrows every quarter. The cost gap is already a chasm.

Third, **regulatory scrutiny intensifies.** A company losing $39 billion a year while preparing a trillion-dollar IPO invites every regulator in the world to ask questions. About antitrust. About consumer protection. About whether this is a sustainable business or a very expensive lottery ticket sold to pension funds.

## Can OpenAI Turn It Around?

Yes, theoretically. But the path is narrow.

Option one: **raise prices.** GPT-5 Pro already costs $200 per month. API users pay by the token. Push prices higher, and customers defect to cheaper competitors. This is a delicate dance.

Option two: **cut R&D.** Unlikely and suicidal. The moment OpenAI slows model development, Anthropic, Google, or a Chinese lab overtakes it. In a winner-take-most market, capability is oxygen.

Option three: **find a killer enterprise use case.** Right now, ChatGPT is a general-purpose tool. General-purpose tools are hard to price because value is diffuse. If OpenAI can build vertical solutions — legal research, pharmaceutical discovery, financial analysis — with measurable returns, enterprise budgets expand. But verticalization takes time, and OpenAI's culture is optimized for research, not industry-specific sales cycles.

Option four: **get acquired or merged.** Rumors of a Microsoft full acquisition have circulated for years. A SpaceX-style rollup is unlikely given OpenAI's nonprofit origins and governance structure. But stranger things have happened in 2026.

## The Bottom Line

OpenAI is the most visible symbol of the AI boom. It has the users, the brand, the talent, and the compute. What it does not yet have is a business model that squares the circle between astronomical costs and earthbound revenue.

The leaked financials are a wake-up call not because they reveal fraud or failure, but because they expose the magnitude of the bet. OpenAI is betting that artificial general intelligence — or something close enough to justify these numbers — arrives before the money runs out. It is betting that enterprise customers will eventually accept AI as a line item as non-negotiable as cloud storage. It is betting that regulators will let a nonprofit-turned-trillion-dollar-company sail into public markets without demanding answers about sustainability, safety, or competition.

Those are big bets. And in the leaked documents, we can see the price tag attached to every one of them: **$39 billion and counting.**

The IPO roadshow begins soon. Wall Street will listen to the story. Then it will ask to see the math.
