# AI;DR (AI; Didn't Read): The Viral Slogan That Just Named Our AI Exhaustion

## 751 Points. 484 Comments. And Four Words That Finally Put a Label on What We Were All Feeling.

On August 15, 2026, a Twitter user with the handle @seclilc posted four words:

> **"AI;DR (AI; didn't read)."**

By the next morning, the tweet had 366,000 views, 17,000 likes, and thousands of replies from people who felt like someone had finally articulated what they'd been experiencing for months. Two days later, it hit the front page of Hacker News with **751 points** and **484 comments** — one of the most upvoted and discussed non-technical posts on the site in 2026.

The term is a play on the old internet acronym TL;DR, which stands for "Too Long; Didn't Read." But where TL;DR was about length, AI;DR is about provenance. It captures a new kind of fatigue: not the fatigue of reading too much, but the fatigue of reading something that was never really written by the person who sent it. Something produced by a machine, pasted without review, and presented as if it were human thought.

This isn't about hating AI. It's about something far more specific, and far more important.

---

## The Physical Flinch

Rick Manelius, a software executive and writer who picked up the idea and expanded it in a newsletter post, described his own reaction with unsettling honesty:

> "I am getting to the point where I physically flinch (sometimes dropping my shoulders and hunching, or having a slight eye twitch) when someone I respect sends me unfiltered and unedited AI output."

Think about that for a moment. This isn't abstract philosophical discomfort. This is a physical, involuntary reaction — the kind of thing your body does when it detects something wrong before your conscious mind can process it. A flinch. A twitch. The same instinct that makes you pull your hand back from a hot stove.

Manelius isn't alone. The Hacker News thread is full of developers describing similar reactions. One commenter writes about "hundreds of lines of AI documentation in every PR" where "every other line of code has between one and ten lines of AI generated comments, talking about the real unlock and how things are byte for byte identical on the load bearing path." Another describes their codebase as a "post readability code base" — not because the code is bad, but because the comments and documentation have become so bloated, so filled with AI-generated filler, that reading them is now harder than reading the code itself.

The most devastating comment in the entire thread might be this one:

> "I don't really know how to address this situation or if it needs addressed. I certainly don't read the long-winded AI comments or the AI documentation, but perhaps it's useful for the AI on its next pass."

Let that sink in. We have reached a point where developers are writing code that they don't expect other humans to read. The intended audience for documentation has become — the next AI.

---

## The Four-Word Policy

Manelius distilled the entire issue into a single policy statement:

> "If you're not bothered enough to review and edit it... then I'm not going to bother reading it."

This is the core of AI;DR. It's not a blanket rejection of AI-generated content. Manelius explicitly acknowledges that "everyone is utilizing AI at SOME point in their process." The objection isn't to AI assistance. It's to AI *abdication* — the decision to let the machine produce the final output without a human ever touching it.

The post makes a crucial distinction between contexts where AI output is appropriate and contexts where it isn't. Customer support is one example: "We're not looking for artisanal 'did you make sure to reset your phone' style dialogue." In transactional, impersonal contexts, machine-generated text is fine. The problem is when the context calls for human judgment, personality, or relationship — and what you get instead is a machine's best approximation of those things.

"It's your name on it," Manelius writes. "Are you proud of the prose and weird AI-isms sprinkled throughout it? If so, great. But I can ask Claude directly if I wanted to."

That last sentence cuts to the heart of the matter. The value of human communication is not that it's *better* than AI output in some absolute sense. It's that it comes from a specific person with specific experiences, a specific perspective, and a specific relationship to you. When you replace that with generic AI output, you haven't just outsourced the writing. You've outsourced the relationship.

## The Hacker News Verdict: A Community in Exasperation

The Hacker News discussion on this post is one of the most revealing snapshots of developer culture in 2026. With 484 comments, it covers every dimension of the AI;DR phenomenon — from code comments to social media to the nature of authorship itself.

### AI-isms: The New Corporate Speak

Several commenters catalogued the verbal tics that have become instant signals of AI authorship:

> "Good instinct. Fair challenge. This corrects my framing. It makes your point sharp. This is a significant finding. Positively confirmed. That settles it, and it flips the picture. Honest verdict — this is not small. Let me wire it in."

This isn't real human dialogue. It's what one commenter called "performative agreement" — the AI's habit of validating everything, restating the obvious, and building elaborate scaffolding around simple points. Another commenter captured the frustration perfectly:

> "I hate that I have to change the way I write to avoid AI-isms. I loved using 'load bearing' to describe weird code that you think you can delete but is actually holding everything together. Now people think I'm just parroting what Claude said."

This is one of the most insidious effects of AI saturation: it contaminates the language itself. Once a phrase becomes associated with AI output, humans stop using it — even if they coined it. The linguistic commons gets polluted, and everyone loses.

### The Documentation Disaster

The most technically detailed comments focused on what AI does to code documentation. Multiple developers reported the same pattern: AI-generated comments that reference "decisions not made," mention "conversations" that happened only between the user and the AI, or cite documents that exist only in the AI's hallucinated context.

One developer described asking an AI to reduce documentation length and make it more concise. After twenty minutes of processing, the line count had somehow *increased*. Another built a utility specifically to strip all comments from their codebase, because "now the code is fully uncommented and it has saved lots of input tokens and also lots of meandering because the model is no longer getting stuck on bad ideas it told itself about."

Perhaps the most telling detail: one developer tried to include rules telling the AI to only include "relevant, evergreen comments" but found "it's to no avail." The models are not designed to be concise. They're designed to be helpful, and in the context of documentation, "helpful" means "verbose" — even when verbosity is actively harmful.

### The Generational Divide

Not everyone in the thread agreed. Several commenters pushed back, arguing that the complaints were overblown or elitist. One suggested that the solution was simply to "talk about it" in a team setting — advice that was quickly met with the observation that in real workplaces, raising concerns about AI output is politically fraught. Management may not understand the problem, may not accept that it's a problem, or may not prioritize fixing it over shipping features.

This tension — between individual developers who see the problem clearly and organizational structures that can't address it — is one of the most important subtexts of the AI;DR discussion. It's not just about personal preference. It's about a systemic shift in how software gets written, documented, and maintained — and the fact that the people closest to the work often have the least power to change it.

## From TL;DR to AI;DR: What Changed?

The original TL;DR emerged from a specific internet culture: forums, long-form blogs, and email threads where people would write multi-paragraph responses. The problem was volume. Readers were overwhelmed by the sheer amount of text.

AI;DR is different. The problem isn't volume. It's *inauthenticity*. A 500-word human email might be long, but it's genuinely the product of someone's mind. A 500-word AI email is long *and* empty — it occupies the same amount of your time and attention without offering the thing that makes reading human writing worthwhile: the sense that you're encountering another person's actual thinking.

This is why AI;DR is landing with such force in 2026. We've had two years of rapid AI adoption — long enough for the novelty to wear off, and for the costs to become visible. The honeymoon phase, where every AI-generated paragraph felt slightly magical, is over. What remains is the daily grind of wading through machine output that looks like communication but doesn't function like it.

The timeline matters here. In early 2024, using AI to draft emails and documentation was still somewhat unusual. By mid-2025, it was standard practice in many tech companies. By mid-2026, we've reached saturation — and saturation is revealing the quality problem that was always there, just hidden by novelty and scale.

---

## Why "Just Don't Use It" Isn't the Answer

The reflexive response to AI;DR — from both AI advocates and AI skeptics — is often some version of "just don't use AI then." This misses the point entirely.

Nobody in this discussion is arguing for a return to pre-AI workflows. The developers complaining about AI-generated comments are almost certainly using AI themselves for other tasks. Manelius explicitly states that "everyone is utilizing AI at SOME point in their process." The question isn't whether to use AI. It's *how*.

The real insight of AI;DR is that AI is a tool for thinking, not a substitute for it. When you use AI to generate an outline, then write the actual piece yourself, you're using AI as a tool. When you paste AI output directly into a conversation with a colleague, you're using AI as a crutch — and the cost of that crutch is borne by the person on the other end.

This is the asymmetry that makes AI;DR so powerful. The sender of unfiltered AI output pays almost nothing — a few seconds to paste, maybe a prompt or two. The recipient pays the real cost: their time, their attention, their willingness to keep engaging. And because the cost is hidden in the recipient's experience rather than the sender's workflow, the incentive structure is broken. There's no feedback loop telling the sender to stop, except the slow erosion of relationships and trust.

## What AI;DR Means for the Future of Work

The implications of this viral moment extend far beyond Twitter and Hacker News. We're watching a cultural norm form in real time — a shared understanding that certain uses of AI are socially acceptable and others are not.

In some contexts, this norm is already well-established. Nobody expects a handwritten birthday card from a corporate HR system. Nobody is offended when a chatbot handles their password reset. But in contexts that require judgment, relationship, or accountability — a performance review, a difficult conversation with a colleague, a piece of writing with your name on it — the expectation of human authorship remains strong.

The question is where the line falls, and it's moving. What felt like an acceptable use of AI in 2024 — drafting a LinkedIn post, generating a newsletter — now triggers eye-rolls in 2026. The window of "acceptable AI assistance" is narrowing as the quality of AI output plateaus and the volume of AI-generated content explodes.

For organizations, this means rethinking policies. If your team's Slack channels are full of AI-generated paragraphs, your culture is already being shaped by machines in ways you may not have intended. If your codebase is accumulating AI comments that reference hallucinated documents, your technical debt is growing in a dimension that traditional code review doesn't catch.

For individuals, AI;DR is a reminder that the value of your communication isn't in its grammatical correctness or its information density. It's in the fact that it comes from *you* — your specific mind, your specific experiences, your specific relationship to the person you're communicating with. When you outsource that to a machine, you're not just saving time. You're spending something far more valuable: the trust and attention of the people you work with.

---

## The Verdict

AI;DR isn't a rejection of technology. It's a rejection of laziness disguised as efficiency. It's a demand that if you're going to occupy someone's attention — their most finite resource — you owe them the respect of having thought about what you're saying.

The tweet that started it all was four words. The discussion it generated spans hundreds of thousands of words. That ratio — four words to hundreds of thousands of responses — tells you everything you need to know about how ready the culture was for this message.

We don't need less AI. We need more human judgment about when and how to use it. The machine can generate the words. But only you can decide if those words are worth sending.

And if you're not bothered enough to make that decision?

Then, well. AI;DR.
