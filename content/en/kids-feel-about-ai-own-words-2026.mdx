# How Kids Really Feel About AI, in Their Own Words

For the last three years, the conversation about artificial intelligence and young people has been conducted almost entirely by adults. Legislators write bans. School districts issue policy memos. Tech CEOs promise that AI tutors will democratize education. Parents panic about cheating. And through it all, the people actually living with this technology — kids aged ten to eighteen — have been treated as subjects of concern rather than sources of insight.

That changed this month. MIT Technology Review published a sweeping feature in which reporters spoke directly with American teenagers about how they use, think about, and feel about AI. The piece is not a poll. It is a portrait, built from long interviews with seven young people across the country: a coder in New York, an organizer in California, a naturalist in New York, a storyteller in Ohio, an artist in Michigan, a pre-premed student in Oregon, and an inventor in Ontario. Their voices, combined with the quantitative backbone of a February 2026 Pew Research Center survey of 1,458 U.S. teens, paint a picture far more textured than the binary narrative of "AI is either destroying childhood or saving it."

The honest answer, it turns out, is that kids are doing what generations have always done with new technology. They are experimenting, resisting, repurposing, and occasionally rolling their eyes.

## The Numbers: What Teens Actually Do With Chatbots

Before diving into the interviews, it is worth grounding the discussion in data. In late 2025, Pew Research Center surveyed 1,458 American teenagers and their parents. The results, published in February 2026, show that AI is already embedded in teen life, but not in the ways panic headlines usually suggest.

Fifty-seven percent of teens say they have used chatbots to search for information. Fifty-four percent have used them for schoolwork. Forty-seven percent have used them for fun or entertainment. Those are solid majorities, but they describe usage that is mostly functional and low-stakes. The more worrying categories — emotional support, casual conversation, image generation — are far smaller. Only sixteen percent of teens say they have had casual conversations with chatbots, and just twelve percent have used them for emotional support or advice. Teens are over four times more likely to be using AI for homework help than for companionship.

The schoolwork numbers deserve a closer look. About one in ten teens say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbot help. Another twenty-one percent say some, and twenty-three percent say a little. Forty-five percent have not used chatbots for schoolwork at all. So while AI is clearly present in classrooms, it is not yet replacing student effort for the majority. About a quarter of all teens find chatbots extremely or very helpful for schoolwork, and another quarter find them somewhat helpful. Just three percent say the tools were of little to no help.

Yet the perception of cheating is pervasive. Fifty-nine percent of teens believe that using AI to cheat is a regular occurrence at their school, including about a third who say it happens extremely or very often. This gap — between modest personal use and inflated perception of peer misuse — is a classic social phenomenon. Teens, like adults, tend to overestimate how much other people are doing the thing everyone is worried about.

## "Indifferent": The Coder Who Sees AI as Crude

Remy is sixteen, lives in New York, and teaches tae kwon do when he is not at school. He uses the free version of Claude mostly for programming projects outside class. When MIT Technology Review asked him for one word to describe AI, he said "indifferent."

His reasoning is refreshingly specific. Remy does not think AI is evil. He just does not find current applications exciting for his own life. He goes to school, reads, plays games with friends. None of that needs AI. When he does use it, he gets utility: Claude helped him write a program to tweak his computer's overclock. But when he tested AI for game development, he found it made sloppy code that was bad at blending mechanics into something cohesive. He would spend more time correcting it than writing it himself.

At school, Remy thinks AI has actually made his assignments worse. Because teachers do not want kids cheating, English classes have shifted to in-class writing. Students now have seventy minutes to write a whole essay. Remy believes that hinders his writing. He also notes that Princeton voted to let faculty proctor exams for the first time in over a century — their honor code, dating to 1893, effectively ended because of AI.

What Remy cares about is precision. He points to AI systems trained to flag breast cancer more accurately, designed to catch their own false positives so a human still verifies. "That's the version I care about," he says. His overall verdict: "AI right now is sort of like the first car or the first airplane. It's interesting but crude. It's obviously an amazing invention but not actually good yet."

## "We Want to Deal People the Cards": The Organizer Building Civic Tools

Danielle is eighteen, studying engineering in California, and she sees AI as a tool whose morality depends entirely on who uses it and how. Her biggest project is Next Voters, a multi-agent system that turns dense government documents into plain-language bullet points delivered to your inbox.

The architecture is elegant. One AI agent finds official government sources for a given city or state — council websites, proposed bills, meeting transcripts. Another verifies they are real and credible. Another scrapes them weekly for updates. Another sorts them into categories like civil rights, immigration, and economics. The last one writes the newsletter. Everything is cited, so readers can click straight to the actual policy.

Danielle's motivation is democratic participation. She was in English class when the war in Ukraine came up and a peer asked, "There's a war going on?" That gap in awareness, combined with emotionally charged social media misinformation, makes her nervous for the next generation of voters. Her team does not want AI to think for people. They want to use it to disperse knowledge. "We want to deal people the cards and let them play them however they want," she says, "but we have to make sure they have the cards in the first place."

## "Angry": The Naturalist Who Boycotts AI for Environmental Reasons

Hazel is seventeen, a rock climber and hiker in New York who wants to be an ecologist. When asked how AI makes her feel, she answered with one word: "Angry."

Her anger is not abstract. It is rooted in the physical infrastructure of AI. Data centers housing enormous server blocks consume vast amounts of water, often drawn from local towns that are left with shortages. When large enough, they emit enough heat to raise local temperatures. Hazel spends her time in the wild spaces she wants to protect, and she sees AI infrastructure as a direct threat to those places.

Her response is a quiet boycott. When AI pops up somewhere, she simply does not engage with it. It can feel isolating when everyone around her is using it, but she does not want AI to be the thing that kills the places she loves. She describes climbing as a refuge: when she is on a wall, she is not thinking about her phone. That is why she loves it.

Hazel represents a perspective rarely heard in AI discourse: a young person whose opposition is not based on fear of job loss or mental decline, but on a tangible, ecological grievance. She is not asking for regulation. She is opting out.

## "I Want My Creativity to Be My Own": The Storyteller Drawing Boundaries

Wesley is fourteen, from Ohio, and he has a clear rule: AI can help with technical tasks, but it must not touch creative ones.

He wrote a short story and ran it through ChatGPT to catch grammar and spelling errors. He used it to debug a small game he had coded for a project. Those are acceptable uses. But when it comes to generating parts of a creative project or writing a paper, he draws a hard line. He worries about AI robbing people of the ability to think creatively or think for themselves.

Wesley has also experimented with Character.AI for fun, dropping himself into the pirate crew of his favorite show, One Piece. He finds it entertaining but notes the limits quickly. On the free version, memory runs out. Get far enough into a chat and it slows down and forgets what happened. His verdict: "It's like everything else with AI. If you trust it to run on its own, it falls apart. You have to keep steering it where you want it to go."

That last sentence could serve as a generational motto.

## "A Little Like a Calculator": The Artist Who Is Still Deciding

Sylvia is ten, from Michigan, and she has not used ChatGPT or Claude herself. Her mom does. Sylvia likes to draw and write songs, and she does not use AI for that. She does not have strong feelings about AI either way. Her comparison is simple and striking: "It's a little like a calculator. A calculator does the math for you, and AI does other things for you."

She did have one memorable negative experience. Her mom found songs she liked on Spotify, looked up the artist, and discovered the entire track was AI-generated. Sylvia was surprised, even though she still liked the song. At school, she uses a program called SchoolAI, which gives her ideas and helps her revise writing. You cannot have it just write for you, but you can use it to help.

Sylvia wants to be an artist, or maybe a librarian. She will probably use some technology either way. But the drawing and the songwriting? Those she wants to keep doing herself. At ten, she has already arrived at a position many adults are still struggling to articulate.

## "I Never Want It to Be My Doctor": The Pre-Premed Using AI for Diabetes

Evelyn is thirteen, from Oregon, and she was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes in January. That diagnosis made AI a bigger part of her life, but in a very specific, medical way.

Her glucose monitor and insulin pump talk to each other using their own kind of AI to predict dosing. The monitor tracks what her blood sugar actually is, and the pump does the math. If it predicts her blood sugar will be high in thirty minutes, it gives a correction dose. If it predicts she is about to go low, it stops the insulin before that happens. Evelyn says she gets to sleep more because of it. When she was doing shots manually, she went low almost every night. It was stressful.

She also uses ChatGPT for school — memory tricks, project ideas — and for cooking. They run recipes through it, specify serving size, and it works out how many carbs there are. But Evelyn has a firm boundary: "I never want it to be my doctor." She wants to be an endocrinologist someday, and she believes people are still in control of AI. The benefits, in her view, outweigh the risks. But only when the tool stays in its lane.

## "We Made It Because It Felt Necessary": The Inventor Scaling Personalized Education

Krishiv is eighteen, from Ontario, and his origin story with AI began with a frustration millions of students share: he wanted to build things, but school demanded endless studying. So he looked for ways to get good grades while studying less, and when Khan Academy's AI tutor was stuck behind a waitlist, he decided to build his own.

The result was Aceflow. Feed it anything a teacher assigned — a thirty-minute lecture video, a blog post, a PDF of textbook slides — and it generates endless practice questions with a tutor that explains things the way your teacher does. He built it for himself, showed it to friends, posted it on TikTok, and gained thousands of users.

Krishiv's defense against the cheating accusation is sharp. A tool like this, he argues, is not so different from well-off families hiring expensive private tutors. Except everyone gets one. He believes kids are capable of far more than people think, and AI can help scale personalized attention to everyone. That unlocks potential.

His trajectory since Aceflow is remarkable. He landed part-time roles at BenchSci and Simple Ventures. He joined an AI lab at MIT, co-instructed an AI agents course with an MIT professor, and launched CheetahPrep.com, an SAT prep platform that adapts to each student using AI. When other kids' first reaction is fear, he sees that as an important signal too. "It's a reminder that we should be excited about the future while still being mindful of the risks, working together to make AI work for humanity."

## What Adults Keep Getting Wrong

Reading these interviews alongside the Pew data, a few patterns emerge that contradict the dominant adult narratives.

First, teens are not using AI primarily for emotional support or companionship. The moral panic about AI boyfriends and parasocial chatbot relationships is not matched by the numbers. Twelve percent is not zero, but it is not a generation drifting into digital solipsism either.

Second, teens are more worried about societal harm than personal job loss. Ask an adult what teens fear about AI, and the answer is usually "their future careers." Ask the teens, and they talk about misinformation, critical thinking, environmental damage, and democratic participation. Their concerns are collective, not merely selfish.

Third, the teens who use AI most thoughtfully are often the ones who also build with it. Remy, Danielle, Krishiv, and Wesley are not passive consumers. They are active critics and creators. Their skepticism is not rooted in ignorance. It is rooted in hands-on experience.

Fourth, teens want boundaries. Hazel boycotts AI for ecological reasons. Wesley bans it from creative work. Evelyn refuses to let it be her doctor. Sylvia wants to keep her art human. These are not Luddites. They are young people making informed, principled choices about where technology belongs in their lives.

Finally, the environmental cost of AI is registering with young people in ways that seem to surprise adults. Hazel is not the only one. Pew did not ask about energy specifically, but the MIT interviewers found that environmental concerns surfaced repeatedly, especially among teens who spend time outdoors. For a generation that has grown up with climate anxiety, the idea that AI is powered by massive, water-guzzling, heat-emitting data centers is not an abstraction. It is a visible contradiction.

## The Generation Negotiating the Terms

The most important takeaway from the MIT Technology Review feature is not any single opinion. It is the fact that these opinions exist, and that they are sophisticated. The teenagers interviewed do not fit into the convenient categories that adult discourse prefers. They are not "digital natives" who blindly trust technology. They are not "victims" of screen addiction who need to be rescued by policy. They are users with agency, boundaries, and evolving ethics.

Remy calls AI crude. Danielle builds civic tools with it. Hazel rejects it for ecological reasons. Wesley uses it for grammar but not for stories. Sylvia compares it to a calculator. Evelyn lets it manage her diabetes but not her medical decisions. Krishiv sees it as a democratizer of education.

All of them are right, in their own contexts. And that multiplicity is the point. AI is not one thing to one generation. It is many things to many people, and the people who will live with it longest are the ones whose voices have been missing from the conversation.

The adults should listen. Not because teens have all the answers. But because they are the ones who will spend the next six decades living in the world the adults are building right now. If we are going to get this right, we need their eyes on the road too.
