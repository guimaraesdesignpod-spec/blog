# China's Embodied AI Gamble: Why the World Is Building a Future of Flesh and Bots

The first thing you notice is the cheering.

Not the whir of servo motors or the sterile hum of a server farm, but actual human applause — loud, enthusiastic, coming from families with children who have gathered under a tent on the outskirts of Shanghai to watch a humanoid robot do a front flip. The machine is wearing what the journalist You Xiaoying described as a "blinged-out top." Its companion is performing drunken-boxing martial arts. The crowd chants "More, more, more." An eleven-year-old boy is showing off a robotic dog his mother just bought him. Staffers are teaching children how to make quadrupedal robots climb stairs.

This is not a research lab. It is not a factory floor. It is a carnival.

And it is, arguably, the most honest photograph of where artificial intelligence is actually heading in 2026.

## The Party Line

On paper, China's bet on embodied intelligence — what policymakers there call "embodied AI" or "physical AI" — is a matter of national strategy. Xi Jinping's government has made it a priority in the latest five-year plan. The official documents talk about "deep integration" of AI into the "real economy." The vision is robots in factories, autonomous vehicles on highways, and intelligent systems woven into daily life.

But the carnival tells you something the white papers do not. China is not just building robots. It is *selling* them. To children. To tourists. To anyone who will watch.

That matters because embodied AI has a problem that large language models do not: it needs bodies. And bodies need social acceptance. A chatbot that hallucinates a footnote is embarrassing. A humanoid that falls over in a factory is expensive. A robot that terrifies your grandmother is a political liability. The fastest way to normalize a technology is to make it fun first and useful later. The smartphone started as a novelty. The internet started as a curiosity. China seems to have learned the lesson.

## Why This Bet, Why Now

The obvious answer is geopolitics. American export controls have made it difficult for Chinese companies to buy the most advanced AI chips. Nvidia's cutting-edge GPUs are restricted. The supply chain for training frontier models is partly walled off. In response, Beijing has done what any strategist would do: it has shifted to ground it can still win.

If you cannot train the biggest model, maybe you can deploy the most agents. If you cannot access the best chips, maybe you can generate the best data — the messy, physical, real-world interactions that happen when an AI actually has to balance on two legs, fold a shirt, or hand you a coffee.

As Gordon Saft noted in a recent dispatch for Rest of World after visiting China with a delegation of tech leaders, the similarities to Silicon Valley were more striking than the differences. The dynamism, the optimism, the sense that something enormous is being built — it all felt familiar. The difference was what was being built. In the United States, the frontier is still mostly digital: larger models, longer context windows, better reasoning. In China, the frontier is increasingly physical.

Saft put it simply: "China's intense focus on deploying AI into the physical world, in robotics, manufacturing, electric vehicles, and embodied intelligence." Whether that focus is conviction or necessity, he added, "may not matter. If embedding AI across an economy ends up mattering more than owning the best frontier model, China's constraint may have become its strategy."

## The Numbers Behind the Show

Let's step away from the carnival for a moment and look at the data, because the numbers are genuinely remarkable.

According to a report from MERICS, the German think tank that monitors Chinese technology policy, China produced roughly 12,800 humanoid robots in 2025. That is about 90% of the global total. Nearly 90% of all two-armed, two-legged robots delivered anywhere on Earth last year were made in China. The country is already the world's largest market for industrial robots, having installed more of them over the last five years than every other country combined.

The domestic market has also shifted decisively toward homegrown manufacturers. Chinese companies supplied over 57% of the country's industrial robots in 2024, overtaking foreign competitors for the first time. That is not a niche. That is a structural shift.

But the MERICS report also includes a reality check that is easy to miss amid the spectacle. Humanoids in Chinese factories are still, according to UBTech's own founder, only about half as efficient as human workers. They lack dexterity. Their battery life is short. Their price tags are still far too high for widespread commercial use. For the technology to become viable, costs would need to fall by at least half.

So what is the carnival for? It is for collecting the data that will eventually make the robots work. Every stumble, every successful flip, every interaction with a curious child is a data point. Foundation models for robotics need physical experience the way large language models need text. China is generating that experience at a scale no one else is attempting.

## The Two Maps

Saft's observation about two powers staring at different maps is worth sitting with.

In the United States, the AI conversation is dominated by a binary that has become almost theological: open versus closed, diffusion versus frontier, safety versus speed. The debate over whether to release model weights, whether to regulate inference compute, whether to slow down training — these are the fights that fill conference halls and congressional hearing rooms.

In China, the conversation keeps returning to a different question: how quickly can we put this into the world?

That is not because Chinese technologists are naive about risk. Multiple analysts told Saft that AI safety is increasingly treated as a national security responsibility inside China's government. Advanced AI is viewed as strategic capability first and consumer technology second. But the capacity to move quickly is inseparable from a political system that places far less weight on individual liberty, due process, and constraints on state power.

The surveillance cameras on every block are impossible to ignore. The same infrastructure that trains robots to navigate streets also trains systems to track citizens. There is no clean separation between China's embodied AI push and its broader surveillance apparatus. That is a feature of the system, not a bug, and it is the reason many Western observers are rightfully uneasy about where this leads.

But unease does not change the physics. If the scarce resource in the next phase of AI turns out to be high-quality interaction with the physical world, then China is generating that resource at industrial scale while America is still debating whether to let people download model weights.

## The Competition Heats Up

China is not alone in this race, of course. South Korea unveiled a nearly $1 trillion plan earlier this year to build semiconductor fabs, AI data centers, and humanoid robot manufacturing at scale. Hyundai Motor Group has pledged to mass-produce Boston Dynamics Atlas robots by 2028, and the country's labor unions have already authorized strikes to demand job protections against automation. Japan has Toyota and Sony. The United States has Boston Dynamics, Tesla's Optimus, and a handful of well-funded startups.

But no one else has made embodied AI a formal national priority in a five-year plan. No one else is producing 90% of the world's humanoids. And no one else is using public festivals to normalize the idea that your next coworker, neighbor, or babysitter might be made of metal.

The MERICS report warns European firms that they could easily find themselves "outpaced by China's blend of industrial capacity and state support," much as happened with electric vehicles. BYD overtook Tesla in global EV sales. Xiaomi went from making phones to becoming one of China's largest EV manufacturers in just a few years. Saft saw the evidence firsthand: at a Huawei flagship store in Shanghai, where an Apple Store might have a Genius Bar, Huawei had filled the back with electric vehicles. The message was not subtle.

## What This Means for Everyone Else

If you are a software engineer in San Francisco or São Paulo, it is easy to think of AI as a text box. You type. It answers. The action is all in the cloud, in the weights, in the training run. The physical world is someone else's problem.

China is betting that this assumption is wrong. The next frontier may not be a bigger model. It may be a model that can walk.

That has implications for everyone. It means the competition for AI dominance is not just about who has the most compute. It is about who has the most real-world experience to train on. It means that robotics startups, manufacturing companies, and logistics firms are about to become as strategically important as chip designers and data center operators. It means that the labor question — what happens when humanoid robots are genuinely half as efficient as humans, then three-quarters, then equal — is not theoretical. It is a policy question for this decade.

And it means that the ethical questions we have been asking about chatbots — bias, hallucination, misalignment — are about to get a lot more physical. A biased hiring algorithm is bad. A biased robot security guard is worse. A misaligned autonomous system that decides a warehouse worker is an obstacle is not a Twitter thread. It is a hospital visit.

## The Carnival and the Long Game

Back in Shanghai, the robot carnival is winding down. The children have taken their robotic dogs home. The humanoids have stopped flipping. The staffers are packing up the water-bead battle ring.

What happened here was not just entertainment. It was calibration. China is calibrating its population to accept a world where robots are normal. It is calibrating its industry to produce them at scale. And it is calibrating its strategy to match a constraint — chip access — with a strength that no export control can limit: the willingness to deploy.

The bet is simple. If AI is going to change the world, it will have to do so in the world. Not in a chat window. Not on a benchmark leaderboard. In factories, on streets, in homes, and at public festivals where an eleven-year-old boy learns that his new best friend is a machine.

Whether that bet pays off depends on a lot of things: whether the robots get cheaper, whether they get smarter, whether societies accept them, and whether the labor disruptions they cause can be managed without political upheaval.

But one thing is already clear. The future of AI is not just digital. It is embodied. And China is putting bodies in the field faster than anyone else.
