# AI and a Brain Implant Just Restored Movement and Touch to a Paralyzed Man

*Published on July 20, 2026*

Six years ago, Keith Thomas dove into a swimming pool and broke his neck. He woke up unable to move his arms, his legs, or anything below his shoulders. The doctors called it complete tetraplegia — a full disconnection between brain and body. For most of medical history, that verdict would have been final.

It wasn't.

In a study published last week in *Nature Medicine*, researchers at the Feinstein Institutes for Medical Research — the research arm of Northwell Health in New York — announced that Thomas can now feed himself, drink from a cup, and feel the fur on his dog. The technology behind it is what they call a **"double neural bypass,"** and it may be the most convincing evidence yet that a severed nervous system can be taught to rewire itself.

## A Closed Loop of Thought and Sensation

The concept is elegant, even if the engineering is brutal. During a 15-hour operation, surgeons implanted five microelectrode arrays into Thomas's brain. These arrays read his neural activity. When he thinks about moving his hand, an AI decoder translates that intention into commands. Those commands are sent to electrodes that stimulate his forearm muscles, and his hand moves.

But movement without feedback is only half the story. So the team added a second channel — a **sensory bypass**. Pressure sensors mounted on a 3D-printed brace detect when Thomas's fingers touch an object. That tactile information is routed back to his brain's sensory cortex, creating the subjective experience of touch.

The result is a closed loop: brain thinks, hand moves, sensors feel, brain senses. It is, in a very real sense, an artificial spinal cord.

## The Numbers

The trial lasted three years. After 35 weeks of training with the movement decoder, Thomas's right arm strength had increased by **86%**. His left arm improved by **62%**. Tasks that had been impossible — lifting his hands to his face, scratching his nose, wiping his mouth — became routine.

The AI decoder itself held up well. It maintained **84.6% accuracy** over five months without retraining. In one particularly telling test, Thomas could lift empty eggshells without breaking them **87% of the time**, even while holding a conversation. Delicate control, in other words, was not just restored but refined.

Then came the sensory side. The researchers developed a technique called **"cortical mirroring"**: they recorded Thomas's brain activity while he imagined being touched, then replayed those patterns into his sensory cortex while simultaneously stimulating his skin and spinal cord. After about 25 weeks targeting his right wrist, Thomas regained sensation in an area that had been numb since his accident.

## Why the Lasting Effect Changes Everything

Brain-computer interfaces have produced remarkable demonstrations before. Paralyzed patients have moved robotic arms, typed text with their thoughts, and even walked with the help of exoskeletons. But in most cases, the effect stops when the machine stops. Remove the electrodes or switch off the stimulation, and the patient returns to baseline.

That is not what happened here.

On a follow-up assessment more than **two years** after the active training ended, Thomas's gains were still present. The researchers believe the bypass did not merely substitute for his broken spinal cord — it triggered **neuroplasticity**, the brain's ability to reorganize its own wiring. In effect, the system trained his nervous system to build new pathways around the injury.

"We're not just bypassing the injury; we're actually rewiring the nervous system," said Chad Bouton, the study's corresponding author. For Thomas, the difference is visceral. "Being able to feel my sister's hand, to pet my dog and feel her fur, these experiences that the injury took away have been restored," he told reporters.

## Where This Fits in the BCI Race

The Feinstein Institutes' work arrives at a moment of extraordinary ferment in brain-computer interfaces. Neuralink has demonstrated its first human patient controlling a computer cursor with thought alone. Paradromics, a rival startup, has implanted its first clinical brain chip designed to restore speech. In China, regulators recently cleared the country's first commercial brain implant, opening a market of 1.4 billion people.

What distinguishes the double neural bypass is the combination of **movement and sensation** in a single system, and the durability of the results. Most BCIs focus on output — decoding the brain's commands. Fewer tackle input, the return of sensation from the world to the brain. The Feinstein team argues that both are necessary for meaningful recovery, because motor control without proprioception and touch is inherently unstable.

The global stakes are considerable. Roughly **15 million people** live with spinal cord injury worldwide, and most with tetraplegia rank hand function as their top priority. If the double neural bypass can be replicated and scaled, it would represent a genuine leap from assistive technology to restorative medicine.

## The Caveats

It is important to keep the enthusiasm in check. This is a single patient. The surgery required a 15-hour operation and the implantation of multiple electrode arrays. The hardware — brain electrodes, stimulators, AI decoders, and sensory feedback loops — is complex, expensive, and not yet miniaturized for broad clinical use.

Larger trials are needed to see how well the approach works for different types of spinal cord injury, and whether the neuroplastic gains hold for years rather than months. The team is already planning those trials, and is testing the system for other conditions, including stroke.

There is also the open question of how far neuroplasticity can go. A partial rewiring is not a cure. Thomas still cannot walk, and his injury remains severe. But the fact that any permanent restoration is possible at all challenges the long-held assumption that spinal cord damage is irreversible.

## The Bottom Line

For decades, the frontier of paralysis treatment was defined by what medicine could compensate for: wheelchairs, voice controls, robotic limbs. The double neural bypass suggests a different future — one in which the nervous system is not replaced but rehabilitated, using AI as the bridge across a break that once seemed unbridgeable.

Keith Thomas went from unable to lift his hands to his face, to feeding himself, to feeling his dog's fur. The machine helped. But the recovery, it seems, was his own.
