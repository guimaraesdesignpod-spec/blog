# Frontier AI Just Killed Competitive Hacking: What the Death of CTFs Teaches Us About Engineering

> "The scene that grew my love for CTFs is emptying out. The CTFTime leaderboard has almost no semblance of history or human skill anymore."

Kabir started playing Capture The Flag competitions in 2021, the same year he started university. Within months he was winning Australia's largest CTF. Within a few years he was placing in the top 10 globally with TheHackersCrew, one of the most respected teams in the world. He is not a casual observer. He is exactly the kind of person the CTF scene was built to produce and retain.

Two weeks ago, he published a post titled "The CTF scene is dead." It hit the front page of Hacker News and pulled 358 upvotes and nearly 360 comments. The response was not confusion. It was recognition. Everyone in the thread already knew what he was describing. They just had not seen it written down with that level of clarity and pain.

This article is not a rehash of Kabir's points. It is an expansion. Because what happened to CTFs is not a niche infosec story. It is a preview of what happens when frontier AI meets any competitive or educational format that relies on closed, solvable problems as its core challenge.

## What CTFs Were Before

For the uninitiated, Capture The Flag in cybersecurity is not a video game mode. It is a worldwide competition format where teams solve technical puzzles across categories like cryptography, reverse engineering, web exploitation, forensics, and binary analysis. Each solved challenge yields a "flag" — a text string like `flag{y0u_f0und_1t}` — and the team with the most flags wins.

CTFs were more than puzzles. They were a ladder.

You started as a beginner, solving easy challenges and learning basic techniques. As you improved, you climbed. You joined better teams. You tackled harder problems. Eventually you reached a level where you were inventing techniques that had never been seen before, building challenges for others, and getting recruited by top-tier security firms or bug bounty programmes.

The format worked because the scoreboard was legible. Your placement reflected your skill, your creativity, and your depth of understanding. Recruiters used it. Conference speakers came from it. The community was held together by a shared belief that clever humans solving difficult problems was worth celebrating.

That belief is now under direct assault.

## The Tipping Point: Claude Opus 4.5

AI has been helping CTF players for years. Tools like decompilers, debuggers, and automated solvers have always been part of the toolkit. The difference was that the human still had to do the reasoning. The tool assisted. It did not replace.

Claude Opus 4.5 changed that calculation.

When it dropped, almost every medium-difficulty challenge — and a significant portion of hard challenges — became agent-solvable. With Claude Code packaged as a CLI, it became trivial to connect an orchestrator to the CTFd API and spin up a Claude instance for every challenge. You could let the system run for the first hour of a 48-hour competition, then only start working on whatever was left.

The effect was immediate. Teams that refused to use AI were not missing a convenience. They were playing a slower version of the same game. The scoreboard stopped measuring security skill and started measuring orchestration skill: how many agents you could spin up, how much context you could feed them, and how much money you were willing to spend on tokens.

This was not theoretical. Legendary teams that were consistently near the top started appearing less often. The CTFTime leaderboard began feeling wrong to people who had watched it for years. Some of the best CTFs, like Plaid CTF, stopped running entirely.

## GPT-5.5 Seals It

Then GPT-5.5 arrived. By benchmark metrics, it sits close to Claude Mythos' capability, and the Pro variant likely surpasses it. Kabir's direct testing confirmed what the benchmarks suggested: GPT-5.5 Pro can one-shot "Insane" difficulty active leakless heap pwn challenges on HackTheBox.

Think about what that means. Heap exploitation — the act of corrupting memory allocation structures to gain arbitrary code execution — is one of the most technically demanding disciplines in computer security. It requires deep understanding of allocator internals, careful manipulation of memory layouts, and creative problem-solving under constraints. A model that can one-shot these challenges is not "helping" a human. It is replacing the human entirely.

This makes open CTFs pay-to-win. The competition is no longer "who understands security best?" It is "who can afford to run enough agents, with enough context, for long enough?" Specialised cybersecurity models like alias1 by Alias Robotics are becoming less relevant compared to general frontier LLMs. The general models have absorbed so much security knowledge from training data that niche fine-tuning cannot keep up.

## Why Organisers Cannot Fix It

The obvious response is: ban AI. Change the rules. Make challenges that resist LLMs.

But CTF organisers have tried all of these things, and they have failed.

Prompt injection tricks that worked against earlier models no longer fool Claude Code. Frontier models are getting better at noticing adversarial framing. Web search capabilities weaken challenges that rely on training cutoffs. Rules asking people not to use LLMs are ignored in open online events, where enforcement is impossible.

If organisers make normal challenges, agents solve too much. If they make challenges deliberately hostile to agents, the challenges become guessy, overengineered, or unpleasant for humans. The cure is worse than the disease.

One commenter in the HN thread compared it to chess engines. Chess has been dominated by computers for decades, but engines are banned during competitive play. They are used for analysis, training, and commentary — enriching the game around the competition without replacing the person competing. The difference is enforcement. In chess, you can physically prevent someone from using a phone or computer during a match. In an online CTF, you cannot.

## The Deeper Problem: Learning Without Struggle

The loss is not just a scoreboard. It is the ladder from beginner curiosity to elite competition.

CTFs were not just a set of puzzles. They were a feedback loop. You struggled with a challenge, learned from the struggle, improved, and saw your ranking climb. That visible progress was motivating. It made the effort feel worthwhile.

If the visible scoreboard is dominated by teams quietly pasting challenges into Claude instances, beginners are pushed toward using AI before they have built the instincts the AI replaces. That prevents active learning, and active struggle is the part that actually teaches. It is also completely demotivating to put in real effort and see no visible progress because the ladder above you has been automated.

This is not a speculative concern. Organisers of beginner-friendly CTFs have already reported seeing multiple groups at the top of the leaderboard who clearly abused LLMs. When questioned, these participants did not understand CTF terminology, the challenge categories, or the underlying concepts. They did not fit the profile of top-tier players. They were simply running the best orchestration.

The community is already fragmenting. Platforms like picoGym and HackTheBox are shifting focus back to pure education, where the expectation is learning rather than competition. The social fabric that held the scene together — pride in human skill, respect for clever solutions, the joy of watching someone solve something difficult because they understood it deeply — is coming apart.

## What This Means Beyond Infosec

The CTF story is specific, but the pattern is general. Any competitive or educational format that meets these three conditions is vulnerable to the same collapse:

1. **The problems are closed and solvable.** There is a correct answer, and it can be reached through reasoning.
2. **The format is online and unproctored.** Enforcement of tool restrictions is impossible.
3. **The skill being measured is reasoning over a known domain.** The AI's training data already contains enough information to perform well.

Math competitions. Programming contests. Standardised tests. Technical interviews that rely on algorithmic puzzles. Certification exams where the questions are fixed or predictable. All of these are in the blast radius.

The response cannot be "just ban AI." That works only where physical presence makes enforcement possible. For everything else, the format has to change.

What comes next depends on what we are actually trying to measure. If we want to test human reasoning under constraints, we need open-ended problems with no single correct answer, evaluated by humans who can probe depth of understanding. If we want to teach skills, we need environments where the goal is mastery, not ranking. If we want competitions, they may have to move to in-person events with proctored hardware — expensive, logistically difficult, but the only proven model that works.

## A Personal Reflection

I have never played CTFs at a competitive level. But I recognise the grief in Kabir's essay because I have felt it in my own field. The moment when a tool you love using becomes a tool that uses you. The moment when a community built around shared struggle realises the struggle has been automated away. The moment when you have to decide whether to adapt into something you do not recognise, or walk away from something that defined you.

The community response has been predictably divided. Some call the article Luddite. Others call it prophetic. The truth is somewhere in the uncomfortable middle. Kabir is not arguing against AI in security research. He uses GPT-5.5 heavily in his professional work. His argument is narrower and more precise: unrestricted AI in competitive CTFs removes the human from the puzzle almost entirely and reduces the art of security to a prompt.

That argument cannot be refuted by pointing out that AI is useful elsewhere. It can only be addressed by rebuilding the format around something AI cannot do — or by accepting that the old format is dead and moving on.

## What Now?

For cybersecurity education, the path forward is already emerging. Lab-based platforms without scoreboards. In-person training with human mentors. Community events like SecTalks and local meetups where the point is connection, not competition. The International Cybersecurity Championship and events like Pwn2Own still exist, but they rely on physical presence and carefully controlled environments.

The lesson for the broader tech industry is this: every skill ladder that depends on closed, solvable problems is a temporary structure. AI will climb it faster than humans can. The only sustainable ladders are the ones built on open-ended exploration, creative synthesis, and human judgment in ambiguous situations.

CTFs taught a generation of security professionals how to think like attackers. Their loss is not just a niche inconvenience. It is a signal that the way we train, test, and recognise expertise is being rewritten in real time.

The question is not whether other fields will follow. The question is which field is next — and whether we will notice in time to build something better.
