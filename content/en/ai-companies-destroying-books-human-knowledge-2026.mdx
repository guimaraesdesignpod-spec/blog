There is a photograph that does not exist yet, but it should. It would show a warehouse conveyor belt, somewhere in Delaware or Nevada, where hardcover books slide one by one into an industrial scanner. On the other side, a shredder. The pages that emerge are confetti. The words that emerge are training vectors. The knowledge that emerges belongs to a single company.

Anna's Archive, the world's largest shadow library, published a report this week alleging exactly this scenario. According to the post, several AI companies are quietly buying vast quantities of secondhand books through intermediaries, scanning them to extract training data, and then destroying the physical copies. The goal is to capture text written by humans before the generative AI flood began — text that is, in industry parlance, "untouched by machines."

The most specific allegation concerns Anthropic. The report claims that an internal initiative called "Project Panama," exposed during a $1.5 billion copyright settlement, involved the company spending tens of millions of dollars to purchase millions of paper books, scanning them to train Claude, and then destroying them all. Anthropic has not publicly confirmed or denied the specific details of the report at the time of writing, but the broader pattern — AI companies vacuuming up physical books for training data — has been documented in multiple legal filings and investigative reports over the past eighteen months.

So why destroy the books? The Anna's Archive report offers three reasons, and they are chilling in their logic. First, it prevents competitors from scanning the same titles for their own models. Second, it avoids legal risk — destroying the physical copy reduces the paper trail. Third, and perhaps most prosaically, destroying books is cheaper than storing them. Once a book has been scanned and shredded, the company that bought it becomes the only entity in the world with a digital copy. Knowledge, which for centuries has existed in redundant physical form across libraries and homes, is suddenly a single point of failure inside a private server rack.

## The paradox of democratisation

This is where the story becomes philosophically uncomfortable. The public pitch of AI companies has always been about access. Large language models are marketed as tools that make human knowledge searchable, summarisable, and available to anyone with an internet connection. Ask Claude a question about medieval philosophy, and it will synthesise an answer drawn from thousands of books you will never have time to read. That is the promise.

But the practice described by Anna's Archive inverts that promise. While the public may gain a more intelligent assistant, the source material is vanishing from the public domain. The books are not being digitised and shared. They are being digitised, ingested, and eliminated. The knowledge still exists, technically, inside the model's weights. But you cannot read it. You cannot quote it. You cannot verify what the model learned from it. The text has been digested, not preserved.

A Hacker News commenter captured the tension with precision. "At least here it gets scanned by the AI company," one user wrote, defending the practice. Another replied: "But that scan is never made available to us in its original form. So it getting scanned by the AI company does nothing to preserve the book." The exchange distills the entire debate. Scanning is not archiving. Ingestion is not preservation. A model that has read a book is not a library that lends it.

## The Aaron Swartz parallel

There is another layer to this story, and it is about justice — or the lack of it.

In 2011, Aaron Swartz, a co-creator of the RSS protocol and a prodigious open-access activist, was arrested for using a hidden laptop at MIT to download approximately 70 gigabytes of academic articles from JSTOR. He faced federal charges that carried a maximum penalty of 35 years in prison and a $1 million fine. The prosecution was relentless. In January 2013, facing what he described as a "court circus" and impending financial ruin, Swartz took his own life. He was 26 years old.

Swartz's stated intention was to disseminate and archive knowledge. He wanted academic research, much of it funded by public money, to be freely available to the public.

Fast forward to 2025. Meta, according to unsealed emails in an ongoing lawsuit, allegedly torrented over 80 terabytes of pirated books to train its Llama models. One internal email reportedly read: "Torrenting from a corporate laptop doesn't feel right." But the company did it anyway. Meta now faces civil lawsuits from major publishers, but the likely outcome — a financial settlement, perhaps a few hundred million dollars — is a rounding error for a company with quarterly revenue in the tens of billions. No one is facing 35 years in prison. No one has lost their life.

The comparison is not perfect. Swartz's method — physically hiding a laptop in a server closet — was technically unauthorised access. Meta's method — downloading pirated torrents — is also legally questionable but exists in a different enforcement universe. The point is not legal equivalence. The point is cultural. One young activist was destroyed for 70 gigabytes of academic papers. A trillion-dollar corporation faces a parking ticket for 80 terabytes of books. The disparity tells you everything about whose knowledge-sharing gets punished and whose gets venture capital.

## Why pre-2022 text is the new oil

To understand why companies would go to the extreme of buying and destroying physical books, you need to understand the synthetic data problem.

Since roughly the beginning of 2025, AI-generated content has accounted for more than half of all newly published material on the internet. That includes blog posts, product reviews, news articles, forum comments, and social media posts. Much of it is low-quality, repetitive, or outright hallucinated. Researchers have warned for years about "model collapse" — the phenomenon where an AI model trained primarily on AI-generated text degrades in quality over successive generations, because the training data loses the statistical noise, errors, and genuine creativity that make human text rich and unpredictable.

Books written before 2022 are therefore extremely valuable. They are guaranteed to be human-written. They cover topics that LLMs have not yet memorised from Reddit threads. They contain long-form reasoning, narrative structure, and domain expertise that is hard to find in web-scraped corpora. And many of them — especially out-of-print titles, obscure technical manuals, and foreign-language works — exist only in physical form, or in such limited digital availability that buying the paper copy is the only way to access the text.

This is the context for the book-buying spree. It is not bibliophilia. It is data acquisition under scarcity. The companies are not collecting first editions because they love literature. They are collecting training data because they are running out of clean, human-written text on the internet, and the synthetic alternative is a downward spiral.

## What gets lost

The Anna's Archive report raises a genuinely scary possibility. If AI companies continue to buy, scan, and destroy physical books, and if publishers simultaneously tighten copyright enforcement and withdraw digital access, then the historical record itself becomes fragile. Not in the abstract sense of "we should remember the past," but in the concrete sense of: there may not be a copy left to remember it with.

A commenter on Hacker News pointed out that many of the books being purchased are not rare first editions. They are ex-library copies, vanity press titles, technical manuals for discontinued products, and obscure works that have not been checked out in decades. Their alternative fate, the argument goes, was probably a recycling centre. At least the AI company scans them before shredding.

This argument is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The question is not whether a rotting copy of a 1987 FORTRAN manual would have survived another decade in a library basement. The question is whether anyone besides the AI company will ever be able to read it. A book in a landfill is inaccessible. A book inside a proprietary training corpus is also inaccessible, but with the added insult that someone is monetising the knowledge it contained while denying access to the source. A landfill is honest. A locked server is a lie dressed as preservation.

There is also the matter of what happens to the model itself. If a future copyright ruling forces AI companies to delete training data, or if a model is discontinued, the knowledge inside it does not automatically return to the public. It is gone. The physical books are confetti. The digital weights are encrypted. The public has neither.

## The call to arms

Anna's Archive is not merely reporting the problem. It is proposing a solution, and the proposal has the ring of a digital-age Library of Alexandria project. The organisation is calling for volunteers worldwide to scan books, journal articles, newspapers, magazines, and ancient texts, and upload them to shadow libraries. The logic is mathematical and inspiring: if ten million people each scan one book, the world gains ten million preserved works.

The incentives are real. Small contributors receive recognition and lifetime membership. Large contributors can have their scanning fees reimbursed. The project is framed explicitly as a race against time — not against AI companies alone, but against publishers who are simultaneously locking knowledge behind paywalls and against a future where most new text is synthetic, recursive, and increasingly hollow.

"Since the beginning of 2025, AI-generated content has accounted for more than half of newly published internet content," the report states. "A frightening reality emerges: if much of the future content consists of AI-generated books and papers, will humans be able to distinguish them? Once AI has absorbed even the last sentence written by humans on paper, all that will remain on the internet will be AI's own words. In such a world, how can human civilization be preserved?"

Shadow libraries, the report argues, are the best answer. Not because they are illegal — though many are — but because they are the only infrastructure currently capable of preserving knowledge outside the control of both corporate AI labs and corporate publishers. They are the inextinguishable light, in the report's own words, of a civilisation that has forgotten how fragile its memory is.

## The uncomfortable truth

There is a final irony worth sitting with. The AI companies destroying books are doing so, in part, because their own products have made the open internet less trustworthy. When half the web is synthetic content, the remaining half — old books, pre-digital archives, physical libraries — becomes more valuable. The very technology that promised to democratise knowledge has made the raw material of knowledge scarce. And the response to that scarcity is not to share what remains, but to hoard it.

This is not a story with villains and heroes. Anna's Archive operates in a legal grey zone. Publishers have legitimate interests in compensation. AI companies have legitimate interests in high-quality training data. The public has a legitimate interest in access to human knowledge. These interests are not reconcilable under current law, and the law is not changing fast enough.

What the report makes clear is that the default outcome, if nothing is done, is not a compromise. It is a monopoly. A small number of companies will control the largest corpus of human-written text ever assembled. They will have obtained it by purchasing and destroying the physical objects that carried it. And they will have done so while telling the public that they are making knowledge more accessible.

The next time you ask an AI assistant a question about history, or philosophy, or an obscure technical topic, consider the possibility that the answer is drawn from a book that no longer exists. Not because it burned in a fire, or because it was lost to time, but because someone bought it, scanned it, and fed it to a shredder. The knowledge is inside the model now. The book is not. And that difference — between access and preservation, between consumption and memory — is the story of our generation.
