# "Code Was Never the Hard Part" Is an Insult to All Programmers

## The Sentence That Broke Hacker News

Last week, a software developer named Senko published a short essay on his personal blog. The title was confrontational. The content, more so. Within hours, it had climbed to the top of Hacker News, racking up **677 points** and **over 400 comments** — not because it revealed a new technology, but because it named a feeling that millions of developers had been too afraid to say out loud.

The sentence that started it all is everywhere now. You hear it in boardrooms, on podcasts, in LinkedIn posts written by people who have never shipped a production bug at 3 a.m.:

> "LLMs may be good at coding, but software was never the hard part."

Or its even more condescending cousin:

> "Coding is easy. It's figuring out what to code that's hard."

Senko's response was simple, direct, and unmistakably angry: *I believe that's a gross insult to all programmers everywhere.*

And the internet, it seems, was ready to argue about it.

## If Coding Is Easy, Why Is Software So Buggy?

The essay builds its case through a series of rhetorical questions that land like body blows. If coding is so trivial, Senko asks, why were programmers in such high demand for decades? Why did they command large salaries even before zero-interest-rate policy flooded tech with cheap capital? Why did companies subject candidates to gruelling LeetCode interviews if any junior fresh out of college could churn out the solution?

If coding is easy, why do doorstoppers like *Clean Code* and *The Pragmatic Programmer* exist? Is Donald Knuth's *The Art of Computer Programming* a light summer read? Is SICP — the legendary MIT textbook that has broken more egos than any compiler error — merely a coffee-table book? Why do bootcamps exist? Why do entire university degrees exist?

And if coding is so trivial, why is software so damn buggy?

These are not trick questions. They are the accumulated weight of an entire profession's experience. The argument that coding is "easy" only makes sense if you have never tried to make a distributed system consistent, never chased a memory leak through three layers of abstraction, never had to explain to a CEO why a "simple button" requires six weeks of backend work. It makes sense if you think software is the stuff that appears on a screen, rather than the invisible architecture that keeps civilisation running.

Senko drives the point home with names. Was John Carmack — the man who taught the world how to render 3D worlds in real time — just in the right place at the right time? Is Fabrice Bellard, who built QEMU and FFmpeg and a JavaScript engine that runs Linux in a browser, merely lucky? The absurdity of the proposition reveals the absurdity of the original claim.

## The Revenge of the Product Manager

The essay does not stop at defending code. It pivots, sharply, to the other half of the argument: that *figuring out what to build* is the truly difficult part.

Senko's response is deliciously petty and devastatingly effective. If deciding what to build is so hard, he asks, why do so many product managers seem clueless? Why are there no rigorous ten-step interview loops for them? Why are market researchers and usability experts not treated like rockstars in every software company? If understanding the customer is genuinely harder than writing a distributed database, why are business analysts looked down on as "pencil pushers"?

The question stings because it is true. In most tech companies, the prestige gradient is unmistakable. Engineers sit at the top of the hierarchy. Product managers, designers, and customer success teams serve supporting roles. If coding were truly the easy part, this distribution of status and compensation would be inexplicable. Unless, of course, the people doing the dismissing have never actually done the hard part themselves.

Senko also punctures the cliché that "most work in software development is talking to stakeholders." He notes that he has met many programmers over his career, and very few of them want to talk to stakeholders. "Having clarity on the priorities," he writes, "boils down to 'just tell me what to do and don't switch it up every two days.'" The image of the engineer as a polymathic philosopher-king who intuitively understands both the customer and the kernel is, in his experience, a myth. Most engineers want clear requirements and a stable specification so they can do what they do best: build things that work.

## Cope at Both Extremes

Where the essay truly transcends a simple rant is in its diagnosis of the current moment. Senko identifies two opposite reactions to the AI revolution, and he dismisses both as "cope" — psychological defences against an uncomfortable reality.

On one side, there is the triumphalist camp. These are the people who proclaim that code is dead, that developers are now "managers of fleets of AI agents," that the only skill that matters is prompt engineering. Senko rejects this not because he doubts AI's capabilities, but because he doubts the human capacity to supervise something we no longer understand. "Don't outsource your understanding, judgement, empathy and taste to AI," he warns. "Don't abdicate your responsibility. Don't be a meat proxy."

On the other side, there is the reactionary camp. These are the people who insist that AI-generated code is "stolen slop garbage," that the bubble will pop soon enough, that if we just hold our breath long enough, the world will return to 2019. Senko rejects this too. "Recognise we're in the middle of an industry-wide tectonic change," he writes. The future will not be negotiated by denial.

The middle path, he argues, is harder than either extreme. It requires accepting that change is happening, understanding what is likely to change and what never changes, and adapting accordingly.

## What Never Changes

Senko is at his most persuasive when he steps back from the immediate controversy and looks at the structural constants of software. Software will always get more complex. It will always need maintenance — bit-rot is a fact of life, and entropy is the second law of thermodynamics applied to codebases. Users will always want more and pay less. They will never fully know what they want. The tension between the business and the customer will never be resolved. And there will never be a shortage of snake oil salesmen promising that the new technology du jour will finally make everything simple.

He illustrates this with personal history. He remembers valgrind. He remembers `mysql_real_escape_string()` from the PHP4 era. He narrowly missed the dBase, Clipper, HyperCard, and Access era — technologies that still haunt dusty beige midi-towers in shops and cafes across the world, running bespoke business solutions with no backups. Each generation of programmers has faced obsolescence. Each generation has survived by learning the next layer of abstraction.

The lesson is not that AI is harmless. The lesson is that technological displacement is not the same as professional extinction. Assembly programmers did not vanish when C arrived. They became systems programmers, kernel hackers, embedded specialists. The work changed. The craft endured.

## What Changes

The essay's final section is a survival guide, and it is strikingly practical. For senior developers, Senko advises broadening horizontally rather than burrowing deeper. Learn about user experience. Learn about customer interviews. Learn about business strategy in your domain. Not because you will become a product manager, but because understanding the full chain of value creation will make you harder to replace.

For junior developers, the advice is the opposite: go deeper. Learn how memory hierarchy works even if you write JavaScript. Learn about network protocols even if you build WordPress plugins. Do LeetCode. Study algorithms. Ask "why" and "how exactly" until you hit bedrock. The more fundamental your understanding, the more durable your career.

The reading list he provides is eclectic and excellent: *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs* for foundations, *The Mythical Man-Month* for organisational reality, *The Design of Everyday Things* for empathy, *The Mom Test* for customer discovery. The message is clear. The programmer of the future is not a code typist or an AI whisperer. The programmer of the future is someone who understands systems, understands people, and refuses to let either dimension atrophy.

## The Hacker News Verdict

The 400-comment thread on Hacker News revealed that Senko's essay had struck a nerve across the entire spectrum of developer experience.

Experienced engineers largely sided with the author. One commenter with thirty years in the industry wrote: "Coding is easy. Programming is the hard part." By this, they meant that the syntax and transcription of ideas into code is mechanical, but the architecture, debugging, and maintenance of real systems over time is where the real skill lives. Another noted that the distinction matters more than ever because LLMs excel at the first task and are still terrible at the second.

Corporate veterans offered a more nuanced view. Several commenters argued that in large organisations with multiple international customers, code is indeed not the hardest problem — alignment, consensus, and strategic clarity are. But even these commenters conceded that the original claim was too broad. "In a corporate setting, code is definitely not the hardest part," one wrote, "but that's why companies can sometimes make do with offshore engineers at $20 an hour. The harder part is building the right thing and designing it soundly to begin with."

The most poignant comments came from developers describing their own emotional responses. "If coding is easy, why do many now feel like their identity and professional purpose are being stripped away from them?" Senko had asked in the essay. The thread proved him right. Developers wrote about performance reviews that dismissed their technical contributions, about managers who could not distinguish between a hotfix and a refactor, about the creeping sense that their craft was being reframed as a commodity just as they had finally mastered it.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here is the uncomfortable truth that neither Senko nor his critics fully resolve: they are both right, and they are both wrong, because they are talking about slightly different things.

"Coding" — the act of translating a fully specified algorithm into a programming language — is, by historical standards, easier than it has ever been. Modern IDEs autocomplete your thoughts. Stack Overflow provides the pattern. Copilot fills in the boilerplate. In that narrow sense, the dismissive claim has merit.

But "programming" — the act of designing, building, debugging, and maintaining software systems that operate at scale in messy human organisations — has never been harder. The systems are more distributed. The requirements are more volatile. The security landscape is a permanent emergency. The expectations are higher. And the tools, despite their power, have introduced new classes of complexity that did not exist a decade ago.

The AI revolution does not eliminate either task. It bifurcates them. It makes the first one trivial and the second one existential. The developers who thrive will not be the ones who can type the fastest or prompt the most cleverly. They will be the ones who can hold an entire system — technical, organisational, human — in their heads, and make it coherent.

Senko's essay will not be the last word on this debate. It was never meant to be. It was meant to be a reminder that before we rush to declare an entire profession obsolete, we should at least understand what that profession actually does. And maybe — just maybe — we should ask the people doing it.

Because if code was never the hard part, then an awful lot of very smart people have wasted an awful lot of very hard years on something very easy. And that is the only claim here that is truly impossible to believe.
