# OpenAI Solved 80-Year Math Problem — Mathematicians Shocked

## The Problem That Stumped Generations

In 1946, the legendary mathematician Paul Erdős posed a deceptively simple question: if you place *n* points anywhere on a flat plane, what is the largest possible number of pairs that can be exactly one unit apart? You can draw it on a napkin. You can explain it to a child. And yet, for nearly 80 years, some of the brightest minds in mathematics have failed to crack it.

The question became known as the **planar unit distance problem**, and it sits at the heart of a field called **discrete geometry** — the study of how points, lines, and shapes behave when you count them rather than measure them continuously. The 2005 book *Research Problems in Discrete Geometry* calls it "possibly the best known (and simplest to explain) problem in combinatorial geometry." Princeton's Noga Alon, one of the leading combinatorialists alive, describes it as "one of Erdős' favorite problems." Erdős himself offered money for its resolution.

The reason it endured so long is not lack of effort. Mathematicians had tried everything from elementary geometric arguments to advanced analytic techniques. The best known construction, a rescaled square grid, gave a specific growth rate that everyone assumed was pretty much the ceiling. The best upper bound, proven by Spencer, Szemerédi, and Trotter in 1984, has stood largely untouched for four decades. The prevailing belief — the **Erdős conjecture** — was that no construction could significantly beat the square grid. The lower and upper bounds were close enough that many believed the conjecture was essentially true.

Then, this week, an AI model proved it was wrong.

## How a General-Purpose AI Cracked a Geometry Classic

The breakthrough came from an internal OpenAI reasoning model — not a specialized mathematics system, not a model fine-tuned on theorem provers, and not one scaffolded with exhaustive proof search. It was a general-purpose system, evaluated as part of a broader effort to test whether advanced models can contribute to frontier research.

OpenAI fed it a collection of Erdős problems. On this one, the model produced a complete proof that **disproved the conjecture**. It constructed an infinite family of point configurations yielding a polynomial improvement over the square grid: for infinitely many values of *n*, the number of unit-distance pairs is at least *n*^(1+δ) for some fixed δ > 0. In plain English: the AI found a way to pack more unit distances into a set of points than mathematicians had thought possible for nearly a century.

What makes the proof remarkable is not just the result, but the method. The AI did not use conventional geometric techniques. Instead, it imported ideas from **algebraic number theory** — a field that studies number systems built from roots of polynomials. It used **infinite class field towers** and **Golod-Shafarevich theory**, concepts well-known to number theorists but previously thought to have nothing to do with questions about points on a plane.

As the mathematician Thomas Bloom writes in the companion paper: "This shows that there is a lot more that number theoretic constructions have to say about these sorts of questions than we suspected; moreover, that the number theory required can be very deep."

## The Human Verification

Before anyone celebrated, the proof had to survive human scrutiny. OpenAI handed it to external mathematicians, including **Fields Medalist Tim Gowers**, who called it "a milestone in AI mathematics." They checked every step, wrote a companion paper explaining the argument and its significance, and identified how the AI's construction works.

The verification was not ceremonial. Mathematicians found the proof valid and the ideas genuinely novel. Princeton professor **Will Sawin** later refined the result to establish an explicit bound: one can take **δ = 0.014**. That may sound small, but in a field where the best construction had not improved in 80 years, any polynomial improvement is seismic.

Leading number theorist **Arul Shankar** put it bluntly: "In my opinion this paper demonstrates that current AI models go beyond just helpers to human mathematicians — they are capable of having original ingenious ideas, and then carrying them out to fruition."

## Why Mathematicians Are Stunned

The shock is not just that an AI solved the problem. It is *how* it solved it.

Machine learning systems have assisted mathematics before. DeepMind's AlphaTensor found faster matrix multiplication algorithms. AlphaGeometry solved Olympiad-level geometry problems. Lean-based proof assistants have formalized complex arguments. But in every prior case, the AI was either specialized for the task or working within a narrow, human-defined framework.

This is different. A general-purpose reasoning model, asked a broad question, came up with an answer that required importing techniques from a distant branch of mathematics that no human had thought to connect. It is the mathematical equivalent of a language model writing a sonnet in a style no one trained it on — except this sonnet has been peer-reviewed by Fields Medalists.

The result also reshapes how mathematicians think about the problem itself. The unit distance conjecture was not some obscure edge case; it was a central, well-loved open problem in an active field. Seeing it fall to a machine shifts the conversation from "Can AI help with math?" to "What other long-standing problems is AI about to crack?"

## What This Means for Science — and for AI

The implications stretch far beyond combinatorial geometry.

Mathematics is an unusually clean testbed for AI reasoning: problems are precisely stated, proofs can be mechanically checked, and a long argument only works if the logic holds from beginning to end. If a model can maintain coherence across a sophisticated proof, connect ideas from distant subfields, and produce work that survives expert scrutiny, those same capabilities will translate to biology, physics, materials science, engineering, and medicine.

OpenAI sees this as a milestone on the path to **automated research**: systems that can help scientists explore more ideas and pursue harder technical questions. But the company is also careful to note that expertise becomes *more* valuable, not less. AI can search, suggest, and verify. Humans choose the problems that matter, interpret the results, and decide what to pursue next. The collaboration the company envisions is not replacement — it is amplification.

This result also raises deeper questions about the nature of mathematical creativity. The model did not brute-force its way through a finite space of proofs. It synthesized an unexpected connection between two fields. Whether that counts as "understanding" is a philosophical question, but practically, it produced a correct proof that advanced human knowledge. That is what matters.

## The Road Ahead

AI is about to take a very serious role in the creative parts of research — and, perhaps most consequentially, in AI research itself. OpenAI explicitly warns that while this progress is not unexpected, it "reinforces the urgency we feel about understanding this next phase of AI development, the challenges of aligning very intelligent systems, and the future of human-AI collaboration."

For mathematicians, the coming months will likely see a flurry of activity. As Bloom predicted, "No doubt many algebraic number theorists will be taking a close look at other open problems in discrete geometry in the coming months." The AI has opened a bridge between two fields. Humans will now explore what else can cross it.

And for the rest of us, the message is clear: the ceiling of what AI can independently accomplish just moved higher. An 80-year-old problem, central to a living mathematical field, solved by a machine that was not even trying to be a mathematician.

The mathematicians are sitting down. The rest of us should probably pay attention too.
