# A German Court Just Ruled That Google Is Liable for What Its AI Says — And It Could Change Everything

For years, the legal question hovering over every AI product on the internet was deceptively simple: who is responsible when the machine gets it wrong?

If ChatGPT falsely accuses a lawyer of embezzlement, who gets sued? If Google's AI Overview links a small business to a scam it has nothing to do with, who pays? If Perplexity summarizes a medical study so badly that a reader makes a dangerous health decision, where does liability land?

The standard answer, until now, has been: nobody, really. AI companies pointed to the same legal frameworks that protect search engines, social media platforms, and web hosts. We don't create the content, they said. We just surface it. We're the messenger.

A court in Munich just called that bluff. And the ruling it handed down could reshape how every AI provider on Earth thinks about liability.

## The Case That Changed the Conversation

The story starts with two publishing companies in Munich. They noticed something disturbing: when people searched for their businesses on Google, the AI Overview at the top of the results page was making claims that were not just wrong, but actively damaging.

The AI declared, in Google's own words, that these publishers were "known for dubious business practices." It linked them to scams, subscription traps, and shady phone calls that never happened. It built a structure around the accusation — a summary, red flags, tips for users — all presented with the confident tone of an authority that had done its homework.

The problem? None of it was true. The AI had mixed up information about other, genuinely problematic companies and drawn connections that did not exist in any of the sources it cited. The publishers sent Google a cease-and-desist letter. Google did not respond appropriately. So they went to court.

The Regional Court of Munich — case number 26 O 869/26, if you want to look it up — issued a temporary injunction barring Google from spreading these false claims. But the ruling goes much further than this one case. It attacks the entire legal foundation that AI companies have been relying on.

## Why This Is Not a Search Engine Case

The core of the ruling is simple but devastating for Google's defense. The court found that AI Overviews are not search results. They are something entirely different.

A traditional search engine indexes web pages and presents them to users. It is a pipeline: content exists somewhere else, the search engine makes it findable. German courts have long shielded search engines from liability on this basis. The Federal Court of Justice ruled that search operators are only "indirect infringers" because they merely facilitate access to third-party content. Imposing a duty to check every result would break how search engines work.

The Munich court looked at AI Overviews and said: this is not that.

The AI does not just surface existing content. It rewrites it. It evaluates multiple sources, combines them, draws its own conclusions, and presents the result "in its own words and according to its own structure." In this case, the AI made claims that did not appear in any of the linked sources. The court called these "the defendant's own statements."

The distinction matters legally because it changes who is responsible. If you are just passing along someone else's words, you are a distributor. If you are generating new statements, you are a publisher. And publishers are liable for what they publish.

Google built the AI. Google trained the model. Google offers it to billions of users. "It alone has influence over the AI's offering and the algorithms with which the AI operates," the court wrote. Therefore, it alone is responsible for what the AI produces.

## Three Legal Protections Google Just Lost

The ruling dismantled three of the most important legal defenses AI companies have been counting on.

**First, the search engine liability shield.** The court ruled that existing protections for search engines and autocomplete do not apply to AI Overviews because the AI generates "independent, new, and substantive statements" rather than merely making third-party content findable.

**Second, the host provider protections under Europe's Digital Services Act.** The DSA generally shields platforms from liability for third-party content if they follow notice-and-takedown procedures. But if the AI Overview is Google's own content, not third-party content, those protections do not apply. The court found a "protection gap": victims could not sue the original sources (who never made the claims) and could not effectively sue Google under existing rules. The ruling closes that gap by making Google the liable party.

**Third, the "users can check for themselves" defense.** At the hearing, Google argued that users could verify the AI's claims by clicking through to the linked sources. Users generally know not to blindly trust AI, the company claimed. The court rejected this with two devastating observations. First, the possibility of disproving a statement through further research does not exempt you from liability for making the statement in the first place. Second, studies show that only about 1% of users ever click a source link directly from an AI Overview. The AI summary is designed to be "understandable on its own" with "independently understandable content." If users are not clicking the sources, the sources are not doing the work of verification that Google claims they do.

## The Free Speech Question

The ruling also takes a swing at one of the most contentious debates in AI law: does AI-generated speech deserve the same free speech protections as human speech?

The court's answer is no. An AI's output, it ruled, is "not the expression of an acquired conviction of the persons expressing it, but the result of an algorithm." Offering AI-powered search is "above all an expression of Google's business activities" and only "at most a secondary expression of an interest in being able to freely express one's opinion."

This is a genuinely novel legal argument. In the United States, courts have generally treated algorithmic speech as protected under the First Amendment, drawing parallels to editorial decisions by newspapers. The Munich court takes the opposite view: because an AI has no beliefs, convictions, or editorial judgment in the human sense, its outputs get less protection. When weighed against the privacy rights of individuals being falsely accused, Google had to take a back seat.

Whether this reasoning survives appeal — or spreads to other jurisdictions — is one of the most important legal questions in AI right now.

## The Scale Problem: 91% Is Not Good Enough

The most sobering part of the story is not what the court ruled. It is what the numbers say about the problem's scale.

An analysis by AI startup Oumi, reported in the New York Times, found that Google's AI Overviews with the current Gemini 3 model answer correctly 91% of the time. That sounds excellent. At Google's scale, it is catastrophic.

Ninety-one percent accuracy means 9% wrong answers. Google processes billions of queries per day. Even if only a fraction get AI Overviews, 9% of a large number is still millions of incorrect, potentially defamatory statements being served to users every single hour. And the Oumi analysis found something even more troubling: 56% of the "correct" answers could not actually be backed up by the sources Google linked. The AI is giving answers whose origins users cannot trace, even when it gets them right.

The Munich court identified exactly this problem. The AI makes its own claims that do not appear in any linked source, and the operator has to answer for them. Whether this reasoning holds up on appeal remains to be seen. But if it gains traction internationally, the fallout will hit not just Google but every AI provider whose systems paraphrase content from the web.

## What Happens Now

Google has not publicly commented on the ruling, which almost certainly means it is preparing an appeal. The court ordered Google to cover 80% of the legal costs, with the plaintiffs paying 10% each. Two minor requests were denied.

But the most important part of the ruling is not the damages. It is the reasoning. The court explicitly noted that the ruling may have "international reach." If other courts adopt Munich's logic — that AI-generated summaries are the platform's own content, not protected third-party speech — the implications are enormous.

ChatGPT's web browsing would be subject to the same analysis. So would Claude's citations, Perplexity's answer engine, Microsoft's Copilot search, and every other AI tool that reads the web and summarizes what it finds. The industry has built its products on the assumption that existing intermediary liability laws would protect them. That assumption is now in question.

For Google specifically, the risk of repeated violations remains acute. The court noted that nothing stops the algorithms from generating the same false statements again. Google has not issued a cease-and-desist declaration with a penalty clause, so the same AI could produce the same defamation tomorrow for a different search query.

The German ruling does not end the debate. But it redefines it. For the first time, a major court has said what many critics have been arguing for years: if you build an AI that speaks in your voice, on your platform, under your control, then those are your words. And you are responsible for them.
