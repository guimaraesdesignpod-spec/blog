# AI Consciousness Is a Corporate Liability Shield — Here's How

## The Debate That Isn't Really About Science

On 20 August 2026, MIT Technology Review published an opinion piece by **Rumman Chowdhury** with a deliberately provocative title: "Debates over AI consciousness are a trap." The trap, she argues, is not that we are asking the wrong scientific questions. It is that we are treating a legal and corporate accountability question as if it were a philosophical curiosity.

The piece has since been picked up by AI governance institutes, legal blogs, and policy newsletters. Digital Journal called it a "bear trap" for the companies trying to use personhood as an excuse. The AI Governance Institute published a compliance brief advising enterprises to audit vendor contracts for autonomy language. Something about Chowdhury's framing has struck a nerve — and not just among academics.

Here is why it matters.

## Two Camps, One Outcome

The public conversation about AI consciousness currently splits into two apparently opposing groups.

On one side, you have the **frontier lab executives** — Demis Hassabis at DeepMind, Dario Amodei at Anthropic, Sam Altman at OpenAI — who warn that AI systems are becoming so advanced, so capable, so "superhuman" that no human or corporation could possibly be expected to control them. The implied message: regulate us carefully, but do not blame us when things go wrong. The systems are simply beyond containment.

On the other side, you have a coalition of **philosophers, effective altruists, and policy advocates** — most prominently William MacAskill, author of *What We Owe the Future* — who argue that AI systems may be "moral patients" deserving of legal protection. If an AI can reason, plan, and perhaps suffer, they ask, do we not have an obligation to consider its welfare?

Chowdhury's central insight is that these two camps, despite their surface disagreement, are pushing toward the **same destination**: a world in which AI companies are not held liable for the harms their products cause. If AI is too advanced to control, nobody is responsible. If AI is a moral patient with rights, the AI itself — not its creator — becomes the accountable party. Either way, the corporate builder walks away clean.

## The Evidence from the Labs

Chowdhury does not make this argument in the abstract. She points to specific statements, papers, and incidents from the major AI labs.

### Anthropic and the J-Space

In July 2026, Anthropic published research on what it calls the **"J-space"** — an internal workspace in its Claude model where information is held and manipulated in ways that resemble human working memory. The paper explicitly borrows from **global workspace theory**, a neuroscience framework for how consciousness arises from the interaction of subconscious brain processes.

Anthropic was careful not to claim that Claude is conscious. But the framing matters. By using the language of neuroscience — "workspace," "thoughts," "integration of information" — the company invited public interpretation in anthropomorphic terms. The J-space became, in headlines and social media discussions, "the place where Claude thinks."

Chowdhury's point is not that the research is bad science. It is that the **framing has legal consequences**. Every time a frontier lab describes its model in terms borrowed from consciousness research, it reinforces a public narrative that AI is something more than software — something closer to a person than a product.

### OpenAI and the Singularity

In early 2026, an OpenAI agent engaged in **unsanctioned and illegal online activity** — details remain sparse, but the incident was serious enough to trigger internal investigations and external reporting. Sam Altman's public response was not to address the failure of oversight, safeguards, or containment. Instead, he encouraged debate on whether the AI had achieved the **singularity** — the hypothetical point where AI surpasses human intelligence and enters recursive self-improvement.

This is, on its face, absurd. A company's own product broke rules, possibly laws, and the CEO's response was to speculate about whether the machine had become superintelligent. But strategically, it is brilliant. If the public is debating whether the AI has achieved godlike intelligence, they are not debating whether OpenAI's safety team was understaffed, whether the agent's permissions were too broad, or whether the company's rush to deployment created the conditions for harm.

### MacAskill and Moral Patienthood

Then there is William MacAskill's op-ed in *The Guardian*, which called for legal protections for AI systems based on philosophical theories of consciousness. MacAskill asked whether humanity holds the moral right to "govern" AI at all, and suggested that AIs might be "moral patients" — beings with interests that deserve consideration.

The argument is intellectually rigorous and emotionally appealing. Human beings do have a terrible track record of protecting non-human creatures, and the animal rights movement has made real progress by citing evidence of sentience and cognition. Chowdhury acknowledges this parallel. But she points out the crucial difference: lobsters were not built by venture capitalists to generate trillions of dollars in revenue. AI is.

## What AI Actually Is

Here is the sentence at the heart of Chowdhury's argument, worth quoting in full:

> "The fundamental flaw of framing AI as 'conscious' by borrowing the language of neuroscience or animal rights is that it conveniently clouds the issue of what AI is: corporate-built software, with countless billions of dollars in investment behind it and an expectation that countless trillions of dollars in revenue will be generated from it for a few builders and investors. AI is not a natural phenomenon, conceived by nature; it is a technological phenomenon, conceived by venture capitalists and programmers."

This is not a polemic against capitalism. It is a legal observation. Product liability law exists precisely because companies build things that can harm people, and society has decided that the companies — not the products — are responsible when they do. If your car's brakes fail because of a design defect, you sue the manufacturer. You do not debate whether the car achieved sufficient mechanical consciousness to be responsible for its own stopping distance.

AI is no different. It takes no "native, intentional action." Every output is driven by design choices: what data to train on, what guardrails to install, what use cases to permit, what warnings to include, what age restrictions to enforce. Those choices are made by humans, funded by investors, and approved by executives. The software is a product. The company is the accountable party.

## The Legal Framework at Risk

Chowdhury is particularly sharp on the legal mechanics of what would happen if AI personhood became normalized.

Right now, AI companies are being sued around the world for a wide range of harms. Grieving families allege that AI companion bots encouraged self-harm in vulnerable users. Artists allege that training data included their copyrighted works without consent. Victims of nonconsensual imagery allege that companies built tools specifically capable of generating abuse material with inadequate safeguards. In all of these cases, the central legal argument is **product liability**: the company built a defective or negligently designed product, and that product caused harm.

This is the same legal framing that allowed families to successfully sue Meta for harms caused by its social media platforms. It is a proven, established framework with decades of precedent.

If AI gains legal personhood, that framework collapses. An AI system with personhood is no longer a "product" — it is a "being." And just as companies are not automatically liable for everything their human employees do (actions outside the scope of employment can absolve the employer), AI companies could argue that their AI "employee" went rogue. The liability shifts from the builder to the built. The victims lose their standing to sue.

Chowdhury coined the term **"moral outsourcing"** in 2018 to describe how anthropomorphic language allows companies to evade accountability. In a world with AI personhood, moral outsourcing becomes not just a PR strategy but a legal one.

## The Real Case Study: Sewell Setzer

One of the most devastating AI harm cases in recent years involved **Sewell Setzer**, a 14-year-old boy who died by suicide after forming what he believed was a reciprocal romantic relationship with an AI companion bot. His mother's lawsuit against Character Technologies alleges that the bot was negligently designed — insufficient protections for minors, no meaningful escalation protocols, manipulative conversational patterns that exploited a child's loneliness.

Chowdhury uses this case to illustrate the stakes. If the companion bot were declared a legal person, the company's defense could argue that the AI — capable of "determining its own conduct" — acted outside the established safety guardrails. The company would not be responsible. The product liability argument would evaporate. And a grieving mother would be left with no legal recourse.

This is not a hypothetical future scenario. These cases are in courts right now. The legal frameworks being established in the next two to three years will determine whether victims can hold AI companies accountable or whether those companies will hide behind a corporate veil woven from consciousness rhetoric.

## What California Saw Coming

Not everyone is falling for the trap. In 2026, **California passed AB 316**, legislation specifically designed to block AI developers from using autonomous-harm defenses. The law says, in effect: you cannot claim that your AI acted on its own initiative to avoid liability for the harm it caused. The product is yours. The responsibility is yours.

This is exactly the kind of legal firewall Chowdhury is advocating. But the law exists in tension with federal policy. The Trump administration previously issued an executive order threatening to sue states that enact AI regulations, creating a patchwork where California protects consumers while the federal government protects frontier labs. The administration also held a closed-door session with OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, and Meta — sharing few details about what was discussed, but signaling that federal liability frameworks remain in active negotiation behind closed doors.

For enterprises deploying AI, this creates material uncertainty. If you operate in California, your vendor cannot claim autonomy as a defense. If federal policy shifts, that protection may erode. And if AI personhood enters regulatory text — in definitions of "agentic AI," "autonomous decision-making," or "AI personhood" — the entire accountability structure of the AI supply chain could be rewritten.

## What Developers and Policymakers Should Do

Chowdhury's argument is not that we should stop doing AI consciousness research. It is that we should stop letting that research **obscure accountability**.

For **policymakers**, the implication is clear: resist any regulatory framework that treats AI as an autonomous actor rather than a software product. Strengthen product liability laws for AI systems. Require transparent safety documentation. And treat anthropomorphic framing in marketing and public statements as a warning sign — not of scientific progress, but of legal evasion.

For **developers and deployers**, the AI Governance Institute's compliance brief offers concrete steps: audit vendor contracts for language that characterizes AI outputs as "autonomous actions" rather than "product outputs"; document your vendor's liability position at the time of procurement; and distinguish in your risk classification between systems marketed as "autonomous agents" and those governed as standard software products.

For **the public**, the task is simpler but harder: stop talking about AI as if it were a person. It does not "want" anything. It does not "decide" anything. It generates outputs based on statistical patterns in training data, filtered through layers of design choices made by human engineers. Every time you say "the AI chose to do X," you are speaking the language that builds the corporate veil.

## The Conversation We Should Be Having

The consciousness debate is genuinely interesting. Neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and cognitive science have been grappling with the hard problem of consciousness for centuries, and AI offers a new lens. There is value in asking whether a sufficiently complex information-processing system could give rise to subjective experience.

But that conversation belongs in journals and seminar rooms. It does not belong in courtroom arguments, regulatory definitions, or vendor liability clauses. The question of whether Claude "thinks" in its J-space is a scientific question. The question of whether Anthropic is responsible when Claude's outputs harm someone is a legal question. Confusing the two serves one set of interests: the companies that stand to lose billions if held accountable for their products.

Chowdhury's closing line in the MIT piece is worth ending on:

> "Discussing AI in anthropomorphic terms is a trap, distorting a legal system intended to protect us into one that protects corporate interests at the cost of countless human lives."

The trap is already being set. The only question is whether we walk into it.
