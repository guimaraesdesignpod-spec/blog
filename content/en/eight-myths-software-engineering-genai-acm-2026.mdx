# Eight Myths About AI and Software Engineering That Are Costing Companies Real Money

If you have been anywhere near a technology conference, a LinkedIn feed, or a boardroom in the past two years, you have heard the pitch. Generative AI is going to make developers ten times more productive. It is going to let startups outpace enterprises. It is going to turn every junior engineer into a senior engineer and every senior engineer into a one-person army. Companies are spending millions on AI coding tool licenses, tracking lines of AI-generated code like it is revenue, and wondering why the transformation they were promised never quite arrives.

The reality, as a team of researchers from Microsoft, the University of Victoria, and elsewhere laid out in a recent ACM Queue article, is far messier and more interesting than the marketing suggests. The article, titled *Eight Myths on Software Engineering and GenAI*, is not a skeptical takedown of AI. The authors are applied scientists and UX researchers who work on AI-assisted developer tools for a living. They want the technology to succeed. But they also want the decisions organizations make about it to be grounded in evidence rather than enthusiasm.

What they found should give every engineering leader pause.

## Myth 1: Developers Spend Most of Their Time Writing Code

This is the foundational myth that makes all the other myths possible. If you believe developers spend their days typing furiously in an editor, then a tool that helps them type faster sounds like a game-changer. The problem is that it is not true.

A 2025 study of more than 450 engineers at Microsoft found that developers spend roughly 14% of their time writing code. On a good day, that figure climbs to 18%. On a bad day, it drops to 11%. The rest of the time is consumed by design discussions, meetings, standups, planning, code reviews, debugging, testing, and the hundred small context switches that make up a modern engineering job.

One senior developer quoted in the study put it plainly: "For me, at my level, I do spend a lot of time on design. So like coding is one aspect, but a lot of time is also spent on design and meetings." Another added: "There's kind of an upper bound on how much of my job I could use it for… The actual amount of time spent coding feels relatively small in a week."

If coding is only 14% of the job, then even a hypothetical AI tool that makes coding twice as fast would improve overall productivity by less than 15%. And that is before accounting for the new work AI creates.

## Myth 2: Writing Code Is the Bottleneck

Building on the first myth, many organizations have assumed that if they can accelerate code generation, they can accelerate software delivery. But the overall development cycle is only as fast as its slowest phase, and coding is rarely the slowest phase.

When AI enables developers to produce code faster, the pressure does not disappear. It moves downstream. More code means more code reviews. More code means more tests to write and maintain. More code means more integration work, more deployment pipelines to monitor, and more technical debt to manage. The researchers call this the difference between the "inner loop" of writing code in the IDE and the "outer loop" of actually shipping software. AI tools have been excellent at the inner loop. They have barely touched the outer loop.

A developer interviewed for the study summarized the experience: "The number of points in my job that are even touched by GitHub Copilot are relatively small." The code gets written faster. Everything else stays the same.

## Myth 3: Lines of Code Written by AI Is the Best Measure of Impact

In 2014, a paper titled *A Statistical Study of the Relevance of Lines of Code Measures in Software Projects* concluded that lines of code "fails to meet the specified validity tests and therefore has limited utility." That was eleven years ago. And yet, in 2026, organizations are still tracking lines of code — now with the added twist of counting how many were generated by AI.

Microsoft itself has publicly reported that up to 30% of its code is written by AI. The researchers are careful not to single out their own employer unfairly, but the broader point is clear: volume metrics are not just unhelpful. They are actively harmful. They incentivize developers to game the system, prioritize coding volume over collaboration, compromise on design quality, and accumulate technical debt faster.

As the researchers put it: "Software engineering companies do not have a goal to maximize the amount of code written." The goal is to deliver secure, maintainable, high-quality software. Lines of code measures none of that.

## Myth 4: AI Helps All Tasks and Engineers Equally

This is perhaps the most empirically destructive myth in the bunch. The research shows that AI effectiveness is wildly context-dependent. A 2024 Microsoft report found that familiar, well-understood tasks saw larger efficiency gains with Copilot than unfamiliar, complex tasks. A 2025 study of experienced open-source developers found that AI tools actually *increased* implementation time by 18% on average. The same tools that accelerate boilerplate writing can slow down architects working on novel system design.

Developer experience matters. Prompt-crafting skill matters. One study found that rewriting a prompt while keeping it semantically equivalent produced different code 46% of the time and changed correctness 28% of the time. Motivation matters too: developers who feel personally motivated to use AI perform better than those who feel forced.

The researchers' conclusion is blunt: "Right now, we are using AI as a hammer and treating code as a nail." But not everything is a nail.

## Myth 5: AI Will Turn Individual Developers into 10x Developers

The "10x developer" is one of Silicon Valley's most enduring fantasies — the lone genius who outperforms an entire team. The AI version of this myth suggests that with the right tool, anyone can become that genius. Controlled studies do show impressive individual productivity gains, sometimes in the range of 55%. But those studies measure isolated tasks, not real team environments.

In practice, software delivery is a coordination problem. Code has to be reviewed. Tests have to pass. Deployments have to be safe. Knowledge has to be shared. The researchers cite earlier work showing that much of the performance difference between developers is attributable to the task they are working on, not some innate ability. One developer might outperform another on one task and underperform on the next. Context swallows individual variance.

## Myth 6: It Is Up to Each Developer to Make AI Work

Most AI adoption strategies today amount to buying licenses and handing them to engineers. The researchers argue this is backwards. Historically, productivity gains have come not from individual tool adoption but from systemic organizational changes. Cal Newport, writing in *The New Yorker*, made the comparison explicit: "The assembly line didn't arrive in a flash of self-evident insight. Ford suffered through numerous false starts and incremental experiments." Organizations are now asking individual knowledge workers to optimize their own workflows while continuing to do all their existing work.

The researchers found that AI tools were often introduced with minimal guidance. Organizations invested millions in licenses without a clear understanding of how to extract value. The absence of dramatic productivity gains suggests that access alone is not enough. Teams need training, workflow redesign, cultural support, and time to experiment.

## Myth 7: High-Performing AI Tools Will Be Adopted Automatically

Even when the tools work, developers do not always use them. The researchers identify a web of barriers that have nothing to do with tool quality. A "competence penalty" means developers — especially women and older engineers — receive harsher evaluations for AI-assisted work even when the output is identical. Trust is low: while 80% of developers use AI tools, only 29% trust their accuracy. Many report spending more time debugging AI output than writing code themselves.

Ethical concerns about training data provenance and environmental impact play a role. So does fear of de-skilling. Engineers worry that overreliance on AI will erode their problem-solving abilities and make them less valuable over time. Organizational support, peer learning, and workflow integration matter more than feature checklists.

## Myth 8: With GenAI, Enterprises Can Innovate at Startup Speed

This myth is particularly expensive for large companies. The logic seems intuitive: if a startup can ship features in days using AI, why cannot a Fortune 500 company do the same? The researchers' answer is that structural differences make the comparison misleading.

Startups build on open-source components and widely documented frameworks — exactly the material that fills LLM training data. Enterprises rely on proprietary tools and legacy codebases that models have never seen. Startups face minimal compliance and regulatory constraints. Enterprises operate under security, privacy, and contractual requirements that apply only at scale. Startups can ship alpha versions with bugs. Enterprise customers expect production-ready solutions, and regulators demand them.

AI performs best in greenfield scenarios. Enterprise software must maintain backward compatibility and integrate with thousands of internal systems. Speed is visible. Complexity is not.

## What This Means for Engineering Leaders

The article is not an argument against AI in software engineering. The researchers are clear that AI can accelerate certain tasks, improve developer satisfaction, and reduce toil. But they are equally clear that the benefits are narrower, more conditional, and more structurally dependent than the industry narrative admits.

For engineering leaders, the practical implications are straightforward but uncomfortable. Stop measuring AI impact by lines of code or coding speed. Start measuring outcomes: delivery time, defect rates, system maintainability, and team well-being. Recognize that AI is not a uniform accelerator. It helps some developers more than others, some tasks more than others, and some organizational contexts more than others. Invest in training and workflow redesign, not just licenses. And above all, stop expecting individual tool adoption to deliver organizational transformation. The assembly line required more than handing workers new wrenches.

The Microsoft team's work is a reminder that in the rush to adopt generative AI, the most dangerous thing an organization can do is believe its own hype. The evidence is catching up. Leaders who listen to it will make better investments. Leaders who do not will keep wondering why their expensive AI tools are not producing the miracles they were promised.
