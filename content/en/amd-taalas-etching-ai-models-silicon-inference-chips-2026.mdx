# AMD Just Bought a Startup That Etches AI Models Into Silicon — and It Could Change Everything About How We Run AI

On August 6, 2026, AMD announced it had reached a definitive agreement to acquire Taalas, a Toronto-based startup that had been operating in near-stealth mode for two and a half years. The news landed quietly in some circles and like a thunderclap in others. Because what Taalas is doing is not an incremental improvement to AI hardware. It is a fundamentally different way of thinking about the relationship between models and the machines that run them.

The short version: Taalas does not store AI model weights in memory. It etches them into the actual transistors of the chip.

If that sounds radical, it is. And if it sounds limiting, it is that too. But the performance numbers are impossible to ignore, and AMD's decision to buy the company outright — rather than license the technology, rather than partner, rather than invest — signals that the House of Zen believes this architecture has a real place in the future of AI infrastructure.

## The Memory Wall Is the Problem

If you have been following the chip wars between Nvidia and AMD, you know that the central battleground is inference: not training models, but serving them to millions of users at scale. Every ChatGPT query, every Claude response, every GitHub Copilot suggestion, every Midjourney image — all of it runs through inference pipelines that are currently dominated by Nvidia GPUs.

The reason Nvidia dominates is not just software lock-in through CUDA. It is also that GPUs are general-purpose enough to handle whatever new model architecture arrives next month. They load weights from high-bandwidth memory (HBM), crunch numbers in parallel, and spit out tokens. Flexibility is their superpower.

But flexibility comes at a cost. Every time a GPU needs a weight value, it has to fetch it from memory. Even with HBM3E pushing over a terabyte per second of bandwidth, the memory wall — the physical limit of how fast you can move data between storage and compute — remains the bottleneck. Companies like Groq and Cerebras attacked this problem with dataflow architectures and wafer-scale engines that keep weights closer to compute. They made big improvements. But Taalas asked a more extreme question: what if we just got rid of the fetch entirely?

## What "Hard-Coded Inference" Actually Means

Taalas calls its approach Hard Coded Inference. The concept is disarmingly simple once you hear it: instead of storing model weights in a separate memory bank and fetching them during computation, you encode the weights directly into the chip's physical layout. The weights become part of the circuit itself.

The chip has two main regions. The first is the mask-ROM recall fabric, where the model weights are literally etched into silicon. The second is an SRAM recall fabric that handles the KV cache — the running memory of a conversation — plus any LoRA fine-tuning adapters you might want to apply. When a query comes in, it flows through the fixed circuit, and the computation happens at the physical level without ever needing to look up a weight value from memory.

In an interview with The Next Platform earlier this year, CEO Ljubisa Bajic — who founded Tenstorrent before starting Taalas, and who spent years as a chip architect at AMD and Nvidia — explained the density advantage in striking terms: "We can store four bits and do the multiply related to it — everything — with a single transistor." That is not a typo. One transistor, four bits, full multiply-accumulate operation. By comparison, a GPU needs an entire memory subsystem, cache hierarchy, and tensor core just to accomplish the same work.

Bajic was candid about how they got there: "What we invented is not particularly difficult, either. It is just a clever thing that nobody saw because nobody went down this path." The Taalas team, which includes his wife Lejla Bajic as COO and former AMD colleague Drago Ignjatovic as CTO, essentially went back to 1970s-style transistor-level design. Hand layouts. Custom everything. No off-the-shelf IP. The kind of chip design that large companies stopped doing decades ago because it does not scale to general-purpose computing — but that happens to be perfect for a fixed, known workload like running a specific AI model.

## The Numbers Are Real

In February 2026, Taalas revealed its first test chip, the HC1, fabricated on TSMC's 6nm process. It was a reticle-sized chip — meaning it pushed up against the physical limits of what can be printed in a single exposure — and it was designed to prove the concept rather than ship to customers.

The benchmark: running Meta's Llama 3.1 8B, a model that by now feels almost quaint but was state of the art when the chip was being designed. The result: 16,960 tokens per second.

At the time, that was roughly 48 times faster than the Nvidia GPUs commonly used for inference, and 8.5 times faster than Cerebras' wafer-scale accelerators. The HC1 could hold 8 billion parameters in its hard-wired section. The upcoming HC2, expected this summer, targets 20 billion parameters per chip.

Now, 20 billion parameters might not sound like much when frontier models are measured in trillions. But inference at scale does not happen on a single chip. You shard models across many accelerators using pipeline parallelism. At 20 billion parameters per chip, you would need only 50 Taalas accelerators to serve a trillion-parameter model. AMD's Helios rack-scale systems can comfortably accommodate that — and would use far less space and power than the equivalent Nvidia LPX configuration, which would require a few dozen GPUs and at least 2,000 Groq LPUs for the same model.

Paresh Kharya, Taalas' VP of products and former Google Cloud AI infrastructure lead, put it succinctly: "Even with trillions of parameters, we are talking about few tens of chips, which is a very, very small compared to anything else out there on the market today."

## The Catch — and It Is a Big One

If you are thinking, "This sounds too good to be true," you are right to be suspicious. There is a massive catch, and anyone in the industry will spot it immediately: once you hard-code a model into silicon, you are married to that model.

In the current AI landscape, new frontier models drop on a monthly cadence. Llama 3.1 is already ancient history. GPT-5.6, Claude 4, Gemini 3 — whatever your favorite lab shipped last month — would require an entirely new chip if you wanted to run it on Taalas hardware. And fabricating chips is not a weekend project. Even with modern semiconductor supply chains, a full respin takes months and costs millions.

Taalas argues that the situation is not as dire as it sounds. Because only the mask-ROM layer contains the model weights, and because those weights are implemented in just two metal layers, a respin does not mean starting from scratch. You change two layers, re-fabricate, and you have a new chip for the new model. The company claims this is 100 times less expensive than training a frontier model in the first place — which is true, but also a bit of an apples-to-oranges comparison.

Still, the inflexibility is real. This technology will not appeal to companies that want to experiment with the latest models every month. It will appeal to model developers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google — who know exactly which model they need to serve at massive scale, and who are willing to commit to it for a production cycle. It will appeal to hyperscale inference providers who run a small number of very large models for millions of users. And it will appeal to anyone for whom the cost of inference, measured in dollars per million tokens and megawatts of data center power, has become the dominant economic constraint.

## Why AMD Bought Them Instead of Licensing

AMD's acquisition strategy here is telling. The company did not strike a partnership. It did not sign a licensing deal like Nvidia's $20 billion arrangement with Groq last December. It bought Taalas outright.

The press release frames the acquisition as strengthening AMD's "long-term AI roadmap with differentiated inference technology and world-class engineering expertise." Vamsi Boppana, AMD's SVP of AI, said the company is "building a full-stack AI platform that gives customers the flexibility to deploy the right compute solutions for every AI workload."

That last word — flexibility — is doing a lot of heavy lifting. Because Taalas chips are the opposite of flexible. They are the most rigid compute architecture you can imagine. But AMD's bet seems to be on a disaggregated future where flexibility and specialization coexist.

The likely architecture, based on reporting from The Register and AMD's own statements: Helios racks use Instinct GPUs for the compute-heavy prompt-processing phase, where the model needs to understand and encode the user's query. Then, for the token-generation phase — the part that actually produces words, code, or pixels — the workload is offloaded to Taalas accelerators that can spit out tokens at a pace no GPU can match.

In other words, you get the best of both worlds. GPUs handle the messy, dynamic, model-agnostic front end. Taalas chips handle the repetitive, structured, model-specific back end. And because AMD owns both layers, it can optimize the handoff in ways no third-party integration ever could.

There is also a tick-tock possibility: customers deploy and validate models on Instinct GPUs first, and once they are confident the model is the right one for their workload, they transition to fixed-function Taalas accelerators for production serving. This gives enterprises a migration path without forcing them to bet the farm on an unproven architecture on day one.

## The Implications for Model Development

There is a second-order effect here that deserves attention. One of the most promising techniques for reducing AI hallucinations is test-time scaling — essentially letting the model "think longer" before responding, exploring multiple reasoning paths and selecting the best one. The catch is that this consumes vastly more tokens, making it expensive and slow.

If AMD's Taalas integration can drive down the cost per token by an order of magnitude while simultaneously increasing throughput by 10x or 20x, model developers may choose to extend reasoning time even further. A code assistant that currently thinks for five seconds might be able to think for a minute, exploring dozens of solution paths, and still deliver a faster user experience than today's systems. The quality of AI outputs could improve not because the base model got bigger, but because the hardware made it affordable to use the existing model more thoroughly.

That is a genuinely novel dynamic. For the past three years, the AI improvement curve has been defined by bigger models, more data, and longer training runs. Taalas opens the door to a different axis: using the same model more intelligently, because the cost of doing so has collapsed.

## The Team Behind the Chips

It is worth pausing on the people here, because the Taalas founding team reads like a reunion of AMD's greatest hits.

Ljubisa Bajic, the CEO, designed video encoders in the early 2000s, then spent years at AMD rising to architect and senior manager of hybrid CPU-GPU designs. He did a stint at Nvidia as senior architect, then returned to AMD, then left to start Tenstorrent in 2016. When Jim Keller was brought in to lead Tenstorrent in 2022, Bajic took six months off and then started Taalas with a completely different idea.

Lejla Bajic, the COO, was a senior engineer at ATI — the Canadian GPU maker AMD acquired for $5.4 billion in 2006 — and rose through AMD's engineering ranks before joining her husband at Tenstorrent and then Taalas.

Drago Ignjatovic, the CTO, was Bajic's successor as director of ASIC design at AMD before joining Tenstorrent as VP of hardware engineering, and then co-founding Taalas.

Paresh Kharya, VP of products, managed AI infrastructure at Google Cloud for three years before jumping to the startup world.

The 25-person engineering team includes alumni from Apple, Google, Nvidia, and Tenstorrent. These are not first-time founders with a PowerPoint and a dream. These are people who have shipped chips that power billions of devices.

## What Happens Next

The deal is expected to close in Q4 2026, subject to regulatory approval. When it does, AMD will gain not just a technology but a team with deep expertise in a style of chip design that has essentially been forgotten by the mainstream semiconductor industry.

Whether Taalas' approach becomes a niche solution for hyperscalers or a genuinely mainstream inference architecture depends on a few variables. Can the respin process be compressed from months to weeks? Will model developers stabilize around a smaller set of foundational models that are worth hard-coding? Can AMD's software stack — ROCm, still the underdog to CUDA — make the integration smooth enough that customers actually adopt it?

None of those questions have clear answers yet. But the direction of the industry is unmistakable. Inference is becoming the dominant cost in AI. Every major player — Nvidia with Groq, AMD with Taalas, Google with TPUs, Amazon with Trainium — is searching for ways to make it cheaper, faster, and less power-hungry. Etching models into silicon is the most extreme solution anyone has proposed so far. And AMD just bet that it is extreme enough to work.
