# ChatGPT 5.5 Pro Just Did PhD-Level Math in 90 Minutes. Here's Why Mathematicians Are Paying Attention

Last Friday, Sir Timothy Gowers — Fields Medalist, professor at Cambridge, and one of the most respected minds in combinatorics — sat down at his computer and did something he had been putting off for weeks. He opened ChatGPT 5.5 Pro, pasted in a description of an open problem from a recent paper in additive number theory, and asked the model to see what it could do.

Ninety minutes later, he had a preprint.

Not a rough sketch. Not a hallucinated proof with hidden errors. A genuine, verifiable improvement on a known mathematical bound, written up in LaTeX, confirmed as "almost certainly correct" by Isaac Rajagopal, the MIT graduate student whose own work the result built upon. Gowers described the achievement as "the level of a perfectly reasonable chapter in a combinatorics PhD." If a human had produced it, it would have been publishable.

This is not another story about AI passing a school exam. This is a story about the boundary between human and machine creativity in one of the most rigorous disciplines on Earth — and that boundary just moved.

## The Setup: Why Gowers Chose This Problem

For the past couple of years, large language models have been steadily climbing the ladder of mathematical difficulty. They have solved several Erdős problems from Thomas Bloom's celebrated database. At first, skeptics could dismiss those wins: the models were often spotting solutions already buried in the literature, or stitching together known results in obvious ways. The comfort was thin, but it existed.

Gowers wanted to test something harder. He picked a paper by Mel Nathanson titled *Diversity, Equity and Inclusion for Problems in Additive Number Theory*, published on arXiv in March 2026. The paper explores questions about sumsets — if you take a set of integers and add every element to every other, how many distinct sums do you get? Nathanson's paper asks a subtle follow-up: if you want to achieve *every* possible sumset size for a set of a given cardinality, what is the smallest "diameter" you need?

For the case where you add each element to every other element exactly once (the standard sumset), Nathanson gave an exponential bound. Gowers asked ChatGPT whether that bound could be tightened. After a little over 17 minutes of reasoning, the model came back with a quadratic bound — provably optimal. It then wrote up the argument as a LaTeX preprint in two minutes.

Gowers checked it. It was correct.

## The Escalation: From Exponential to Polynomial

Most people would have stopped there. Gowers did not.

Nathanson's paper also touches on a much harder generalization, studied by Isaac Rajagopal in his own recent work. Instead of looking at pairwise sums, you look at *h*-fold sums — adding *h* elements at a time. Rajagopal had proved an exponential upper bound for this general case. It was serious work, the kind that earns a graduate student real respect in the field.

Gowers fed the problem to ChatGPT 5.5 Pro and asked if Rajagopal's bound could be improved.

The sequence that followed reads like a compressed graduate seminar:

1. After 16 minutes, the model proposed an improvement from exponential to sub-exponential — an argument that, while building on Rajagopal's framework, introduced a genuinely new structural insight.
2. Gowers asked it to formalize the proof. Forty-eight minutes later, a preprint was ready.
3. Rajagopal read it and thought it looked correct.
4. Gowers, now greedy, asked if a *polynomial* bound was possible.
5. After another 13 minutes, the model said it was optimistic but needed to check two technical statements.
6. Nine minutes later, the checks were done.
7. Thirty-one minutes after that, the final preprint was complete.

Total time from first prompt to publishable mathematics: under two hours. Total human mathematical input: zero.

Rajagopal's verdict on the polynomial bound was striking. He called the key idea "original and clever" — the sort of thing he would be proud to come up with after a week or two of deep thought. ChatGPT found it in less than an hour.

## The Verification Problem

One of the recurring criticisms of AI-generated mathematics is that models can sound convincing while smuggling in subtle errors. Gowers was careful on this point. He did not merely accept the output. He sent the first preprint to Nathanson, who forwarded it to Rajagopal. The second, harder preprint was also vetted by Rajagopal, who confirmed its correctness not just line-by-line, but at the level of *ideas*.

That distinction matters. A line-by-line check catches calculation errors. An idea-level check catches conceptual nonsense. Passing both is what separates a language model's fantasy from actual mathematics.

Even so, the episode raises a practical question Gowers himself poses: what do we do with AI-generated results? arXiv has a policy against accepting AI-written content, which Gowers supports. Journals feel pointless when there is no human author seeking credit. Yet the result is real, useful, and answers a question posed by a human mathematician. Gowers suggests a moderated repository — perhaps with human certification or formal proof-assistant verification — as a possible middle ground.

## What This Means for the Future of Research

Gowers is blunt about the implications for training young researchers. One traditional way to help a beginning PhD student build confidence is to hand them a "gentle" problem — something tractable, interesting, and officially open. If frontier models can now solve gentle problems in an afternoon, that training path is closing.

The new floor for human contribution, Gowers argues, is not simply to prove something nobody has proved before. It is to prove something that *AI cannot prove*. Or, more realistically, to prove something in collaboration with AI that the model could not manage alone.

He qualifies this with two important caveats. First, students can still use LLMs as tools; the challenge becomes directing the tool toward problems it cannot solve unaided. Second, it is not yet clear how much this generalizes beyond combinatorics. Combinatorics is unusually problem-driven: you start with a concrete question and reason backward. Other areas of mathematics rely more on forward exploration — following a circle of ideas and discriminating between interesting and uninteresting observations. Whether LLMs can do that remains an open question.

## The Broader Context: Is Progress Slowing, or Accelerating?

The Hacker News discussion of Gowers's post quickly turned into a debate about the trajectory of LLM capabilities. Some commenters argued that model progress is slowing, pointing to S-curve dynamics and diminishing returns. Others noted that just a few years ago, these same models could not reliably solve high-school math problems. Now they are producing PhD-level preprints.

A physics professor chimed in to say that while Gemini has become an indispensable assistant for checking papers — it once spotted a missing imaginary unit that had eluded the professor for days — it still makes conceptual errors that only deep expertise can catch. LLMs, in this view, are not replacements for mathematicians or physicists. They are extraordinarily fast, well-read, and tireless collaborators who still need adult supervision.

The CritPt benchmark, a set of 71 unpublished, research-level physics problems designed by over 50 active researchers, offers a reality check. Even the best frontier models score around 30% on it. GPT-5.5 Pro reached that ceiling; it did not break it. The gap between AI and frontier human research still exists. But it is narrowing, and the rate of narrowing is what keeps researchers up at night.

## The Credit Question

Gowers closes with a thought experiment that cuts to the heart of the matter. Suppose a mathematician solves a major open problem through a long exchange with an LLM. The human plays a useful guiding role — asking the right questions, steering the exploration, rejecting dead ends — but the model does all the technical work and contributes the main ideas. Would we regard that as a major human achievement?

Gowers suspects the answer is no. And if the answer is no, then the nature of mathematical prestige, built over centuries on individual genius and original insight, is facing its most serious challenge since the invention of the computer.

## Not an Obituary, but a Recalibration

It is tempting to read stories like this as the beginning of the end for human mathematicians. That is almost certainly the wrong framing. What Gowers's experiment really shows is a recalibration of what humans are for.

The lower end of the research spectrum — careful literature review, routine bound improvements, verification of technical claims — is being absorbed by machines at remarkable speed. The upper end — asking the questions that matter, recognizing deep structural connections, guiding the moral and intellectual direction of a field — remains human territory. For now.

The question is not whether AI will replace mathematicians. The question is how quickly the middle ground between those two extremes will shift, and whether the next generation of researchers can learn to dance on a moving floor.
