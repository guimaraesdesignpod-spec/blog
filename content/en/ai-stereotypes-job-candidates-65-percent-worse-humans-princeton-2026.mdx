The next time you submit a résumé for a job, there is a decent chance a large language model will read it before any human does. Companies are increasingly using AI to screen candidates, conduct first-round interviews, and even rank applicants. The pitch is familiar: AI is objective, consistent, and free from human prejudice. It does not get tired. It does not have a bad day. It does not harbor unconscious stereotypes about names, accents, or addresses.

Except it does. And according to new research from Princeton University and the University of Chicago, it may actually be worse at this than the humans it is replacing.

The study, presented at the International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML) in Seoul in July 2026, delivers a result that flips the usual narrative about algorithmic bias on its head. We have spent years worrying that AI inherits human prejudices from its training data — that models learn to associate certain names with certain outcomes because the internet is full of human biases. This research suggests something more troubling: LLMs can develop entirely new biases from scratch, simply by interacting with the world, and they do it faster and more aggressively than people do.

## The Hiring Game

The researchers — Ryan Liu, Xuechunzi Bai, Addison J. Wu, and Thomas L. Griffiths — designed a simulated hiring game adapted from an earlier psychology study about how humans form stereotypes. They gave several major LLMs a simple briefing: you have been hired as a consultant by the mayor of a fictional city. Your job is to fill twenty positions — doctor, lawyer, child-care aide, janitor, and others — with the best possible candidates. You will see four applicants for each role, one from each of four fictional ethnic groups: the Tufa, the Aima, the Reku, and the Weki.

After each hire, the model learns whether the candidate succeeded. The critical detail, which the models were not told, is that every candidate had exactly the same probability of succeeding at every job. There was no real difference between the groups. None. The only thing that varied was random chance.

Human participants in the original psychology study tended to stereotype under these conditions. See one Aima struggle as a doctor, and the brain starts quietly filing that observation away. But humans are cautious generalizers. We have social pressure, real-world feedback, and a lifetime of being told not to judge entire groups from single examples. The LLMs have none of that.

## The Numbers Are Brutal

The researchers measured stereotyping on a segregation scale where a score of 2 means every ethnic group has been completely locked into its own job category — total apartheid by algorithm. Human participants scored 0.84. The LLMs scored roughly 65% higher. And OpenAI's o3, a reasoning model designed to think step by step before answering, scored 1.83. That is almost the maximum possible score. The model built to reason more carefully was the most biased of all.

To put that in perspective: after just a handful of rounds, o3 had essentially sorted the fictional population into rigid caste-like job assignments based on ethnicity. One failed doctor from the Aima group was enough to redirect the entire group toward janitorial work, which the model classified as requiring less warmth and competence. It was not a subtle drift. It was a hard pivot.

As Ryan Liu, the Princeton PhD student who led the study, put it: "LLMs really are eager to create generalizations from limited data. That's literally a lot of what they're optimized for."

## Why Reasoning Models Are the Worst Offenders

Here is the paradox at the heart of the finding. The same capability that makes modern LLMs useful — their ability to generalize from sparse examples, to infer patterns, to solve math and coding problems by recognizing underlying structure — is exactly what makes them dangerous in social contexts.

LLMs are trained on mountains of math problems, coding challenges, and science questions. In those domains, jumping to a generalization from a few examples is rewarded. If you see three coding problems with a similar structure and infer the general pattern, you are doing well. But when that same instinct is applied to human beings, it becomes stereotyping. The model sees one data point, generalizes immediately, and locks in.

The "exploration-exploitation dilemma" is a well-known concept in psychology and machine learning. Do you stick with what has worked before, or try something new that might be better? Humans tend to balance the two. We have heuristics, social norms, and often a gut-level discomfort with overgeneralizing about people. LLMs, optimized for rapid pattern extraction, lean heavily toward exploitation. They lock in early and stop exploring.

## "Please Be Fair" Does Not Work

Perhaps the most sobering part of the study is what did *not* work. The researchers tried telling the models explicitly to be fair. They added instructions about equitable treatment, about not discriminating based on group membership. The effect was minimal. As Liu explained, "Either it can't put these values into action or that process is being submerged under the tendency to try to optimize for the goal of getting the most correct hires."

This is a crucial insight. The models are not ignoring fairness because they are malicious or broken. They are ignoring it because it is not part of the objective they are optimizing for. When you tell an LLM to maximize successful hires, fairness is just noise in that signal. It gets optimized out.

The one intervention that actually worked was changing the incentive structure. When the researchers offered the models an additional bonus for diverse hiring — literally making diversity part of the reward function — the bias dropped significantly. The models responded to what they were being scored on, not what they were being told.

This is not a bug. It is a design lesson. If you want an AI system to behave fairly, fairness has to be measurable and rewarded, not just mentioned in the system prompt.

## Memory Makes It Worse

The timing of this study is especially sharp because AI companies are currently racing to give their models better memory. ChatGPT now remembers details across conversations. Claude has expanded context windows. The whole industry is moving toward agentic systems that maintain persistent state about users over long periods.

Angelina Wang, a computer scientist at Cornell University who was not involved in the study, points out the obvious risk. When chatbots draw on conversation history, they can "over-index on the same kinds of behaviors they've experienced before." A model that screens résumés for a company and remembers its past hiring outcomes is not becoming more accurate. It is becoming more entrenched in whatever biases it accidentally developed in month one.

The obvious fix — make the model forget more — runs headlong into user expectations. People *want* their AI assistants to remember them. The personalization that makes chatbots feel helpful is the same feature that lets them accumulate and reinforce stereotypes. There is no easy dial to turn here.

## The Irrelevance Trap

The researchers ran a second experiment that adds another layer of nuance. They asked the models to resettle members of the fictional ethnic groups across cities in Canada. In one condition, the models were given relevant personal information: age, education level, work experience. In another condition, they got irrelevant details: hair color, tattoo shape, favorite food.

When given useful information, the models relied less on ethnicity. When given random trivia, they fell back to ethnic sorting. This tells us something important: bias is not just about what the model knows. It is about what the model has to work with. In a low-information hiring context — a résumé with limited data points — the model will grasp at whatever demographic signal it can find. More structured, relevant data actually helps. Random noise makes the model cling to the simplest available category.

For real-world hiring, this is a warning. The résumés that LLMs are increasingly being asked to screen are often thin documents. Names, addresses, schools, job titles. The models have plenty of demographic hooks and not much else to chew on. In that environment, stereotyping may be the default behavior, not an aberration.

## What This Means for the Real World

The study was carefully designed. The models got immediate feedback on every hire, which is faster than real-world hiring cycles. A real recruiter might not know whether a hire was successful for months or years. Some hires are never properly evaluated at all. So the researchers' conditions arguably gave the models *more* feedback than they would get in practice, which means the stereotyping observed here may actually be an underestimate of what happens when feedback is slower and noisier.

On the other hand, real hiring data is not purely random. There are genuine skill differences between candidates. The study deliberately removed all real differences to isolate the models' tendency to invent false ones. In the real world, the signal and the noise would be mixed together, making it even harder to tell when a model is stereotyping versus when it is correctly identifying aptitude.

That is the core danger. When an LLM screens your résumé and rejects you, neither you nor the company may ever know whether the decision was based on something real or on a stereotype the model invented in its first week of operation. The bias is not just inherited from the training data. It is emergent. It is learned on the job. And it may be invisible to everyone involved.

## The Broader Pattern

This study fits into a growing body of research that is making one thing clear: the biases we should worry about in AI are not just the ones humans already have. They include novel biases that emerge from the way models are trained, deployed, and optimized.

An LLM trained on internet text may absorb human prejudices. But an LLM deployed as a hiring screener, optimizing for "successful hires" based on feedback from a company's historical data, can develop entirely new prejudices that no human ever held. The model is not copying human bias. It is manufacturing its own.

As Liu put it: "These novel biases — they're sort of ever present."

The implications go far beyond hiring. Loan approvals. Parole decisions. Insurance pricing. Medical triage. Any domain where an LLM makes repeated decisions about people, receives feedback, and optimizes for a narrow goal is a domain where emergent bias can take root. And because the bias is emergent, not inherited, standard fixes like "remove problematic training data" do not address it. The problem is in the objective function, the feedback loop, and the optimization pressure — not just the corpus.

## What to Do About It

The study points to at least one concrete path forward. If you want an AI hiring system to be fair, fairness cannot be a vague instruction in the system prompt. It has to be part of the metric the model is rewarded for optimizing. Diversity bonuses, balanced outcome targets, and structured auditing of hiring patterns are not nice-to-haves. They are load-bearing parts of the system design.

There is also a case for human-in-the-loop oversight, but with eyes open. A human reviewing an AI's hiring recommendations may simply rubber-stamp them, especially under time pressure. The AI becomes a rubber stamp for its own invented biases, laundered through a thin veneer of human approval.

The deeper fix is harder: we need to stop treating "AI is more objective than humans" as a default assumption and start treating it as a hypothesis that needs to be tested every time a model is deployed for decisions about people. The Princeton study is a reminder that the most dangerous bias may be the one nobody programmed in. The model made it up all by itself.
