# Virginia Just Banned the Sale of Your Location Data. Here's Why It Might Not Be Enough.

## Imagine Someone Bought a Map of Everywhere You've Been

Not just your home address. Not just your workplace. Every restaurant you visited last Tuesday. The clinic you drove to at 2 a.m. The protest you attended. The church, the bar, the friend's apartment. All of it, timestamped and sold, without your meaningful knowledge or consent.

This is not a dystopian thought experiment. This is the business model of an entire industry. Data brokers have been collecting, packaging, and selling precise geolocation data for years. Your phone apps report your coordinates. Cellular towers triangulate your position. Your car's infotainment system, your fitness tracker, your weather app — all of them generate location breadcrumbs that someone, somewhere, is monetizing.

Until now, this trade has operated with minimal friction in the United States. There is no comprehensive federal privacy law. The Federal Trade Commission can punish specific bad actors after the fact, but it cannot stop the industry from existing. Europe has the GDPR. The U.S. has a patchwork of state laws, most of which are too weak or too narrow to matter.

Virginia's new law is different. It is the latest — and perhaps the most strategically significant — attempt to draw a hard line around the sale of location data. But it is also riddled with the same compromises that have weakened every American privacy law before it. To understand whether this helps you, you need to look at what it bans, what it leaves untouched, and what comes next.

## What Virginia SB 388 Actually Does

On April 13, 2026, Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger signed Senate Bill 388 into law. The bill amends the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act — already one of the stronger state privacy statutes — to explicitly prohibit the sale of precise geolocation data.

The definitions matter here. "Precise geolocation data" means information derived from technology, including GPS coordinates and other mechanisms, that identifies a person's specific location within a radius of 1,750 feet. That is roughly five and a half football fields. It is broad enough to cover your neighborhood block but not your exact spot on a park bench.

The law defines "sale" as "the exchange of personal data for monetary consideration by the controller to a third party." That narrow definition is intentional and important. It means the law targets transactions where money changes hands. It does not necessarily cover data-for-service barters, affiliate sharing, or arrangements where a company "gives" your data to a partner in exchange for advertising services or analytics.

The ban applies to data controllers operating under Virginia law. That means if you are a Virginia resident and a company subject to the VCDPA sells your precise location data, that company is now breaking state law. The penalty framework is built into the VCDPA: up to $7,500 per violation, enforceable by the Virginia Attorney General.

## The Multi-State Momentum

Virginia did not arrive here in a vacuum. Maryland and Oregon already passed similar geolocation bans. In both cases, the definition of "sale" is broader than Virginia's — it includes exchange for "monetary or other valuable consideration," which catches more creative arrangements that companies use to claim they are not technically "selling" anything.

The California Attorney General launched an investigation into the location data industry in March 2025. California's legislature is now considering AB 322, which would restrict precise geolocation information. Massachusetts, Vermont, and Washington State have parallel bills in various stages of consideration.

This is starting to look like a national trend built state by state — the same pattern that produced cannabis legalization, marriage equality, and minimum wage increases. The U.S. federal system often works this way: states experiment, the experiments succeed or fail, and eventually pressure builds for national action.

The FTC has also been active. In 2024, it reached a settlement with a major data broker that banned the company from selling precise geolocation data. That case established a template. The FTC's complaint laid out how location data brokers create products that reveal where people live, work, worship, and seek medical care. The settlement did not end the industry. But it signaled that regulators see this as a genuine consumer protection problem, not a niche tech issue.

## The Enforcement Problem Nobody Talks About

Here is where the optimism needs a brake. Virginia can pass any law it wants. Enforcing it against companies with no physical presence in Virginia is a different challenge entirely.

Hacker News commenters raised this immediately when the story broke. What happens if a Delaware-incorporated data broker, with no employees or servers in Virginia, sells location data that includes Virginians? Can Virginia reach across state lines? The answer is complicated and uncertain.

Under U.S. civil procedure, Virginia could file suit in its own courts or try to establish jurisdiction in federal court. But data brokers are skilled at legal arbitrage. They incorporate in business-friendly states, host servers wherever cloud providers allow, and structure contracts so that the actual "sale" happens in a jurisdiction with weaker laws.

The presence of major cloud data centers in Northern Virginia — Amazon's us-east-1 region is famously located there — adds an ironic layer. Some of the infrastructure that processes and stores location data sits literally inside Virginia. Whether that gives Virginia jurisdiction over out-of-state data controllers is a question courts would need to sort out.

Then there is the narrower definition of "sale" in Virginia's law compared to Maryland and Oregon. A company could theoretically structure a deal as a "data sharing partnership" where no money changes hands directly, but one partner pays inflated prices for unrelated services. The law does not cover "sharing" or "disclosure" — only "sale." That is a deliberate carve-out, and companies will exploit it.

## The Loopholes That Matter

The biggest gap in Virginia's law is right there in the word "precise." The law bans selling location data accurate to within 1,750 feet. It does not ban selling location data that is fuzzier, aggregated, or inferred.

As one Hacker News commenter noted: companies can just sell fuzzy geolocation data. If you cannot pinpoint a person within 1,750 feet, but you can say they were "in downtown Richmond on Thursday evening," that information is still commercially valuable. Combine it with demographic data, purchase history, and device identifiers, and you have a profile that is nearly as actionable as GPS coordinates.

Another gap: the law does not ban collection. Your weather app can still request location permissions. Your cellular carrier can still triangulate your position for network management. Your car can still log where you drive. All of that data can still be held internally, used for first-party advertising, or shared with affiliates under vague "service provider" exemptions.

The law also does not create a private right of action. Only the Virginia Attorney General can enforce it. If you discover that a company sold your location data, you cannot sue them yourself. You can file a complaint and hope the state decides to act. That is a significant power imbalance between consumers and corporations.

## Why This Still Matters

Despite the gaps, Virginia's law is not meaningless. It does something important that previous privacy legislation has avoided: it names a specific category of data and bans its sale outright.

Most U.S. privacy laws have operated through transparency and opt-out frameworks. Companies must disclose what they collect and give consumers a way to object. The problem is that almost no one reads privacy policies, and the opt-out mechanisms are deliberately burdensome. You do not opt out of location tracking; you opt out of the entire service, or you navigate a maze of settings that reset with every app update.

A sale ban is different. It removes the consumer from the equation entirely. The company simply cannot do it. That is a much stronger regulatory approach, and it is the one Europe adopted with the GDPR. The fact that a U.S. state is moving in that direction — even incompletely — is notable.

The law also puts pressure on the business model itself. Data brokers operate on volume. If Maryland, Oregon, Virginia, and potentially California and Massachusetts all ban the sale of precise geolocation data, the addressable market shrinks dramatically. Companies that built their entire revenue stream on selling location data may find that stream drying up faster than they anticipated.

## What You Should Actually Do

Laws are slow. Technology is fast. If you live in Virginia, you should not assume you are now protected from location tracking.

Check your phone's location permissions. Go through every app that has requested location access and revoke it from apps that do not genuinely need it. Your weather app does not need to know where you are every minute. Your flashlight app certainly does not. On iOS, use "While Using the App" instead of "Always." On Android, set location access to "Ask every time" or "Deny" for anything non-essential.

Understand that cellular carriers can track your location regardless of your phone settings. The towers know where you are because physics requires it. You can opt out of carrier data sales in some cases, but the process varies by provider and is often buried in account settings.

If you are a developer or founder building location-dependent services, this law matters to your business model. The era of collecting location data by default and monetizing it through third-party sales is ending. Build your product assuming that location data is a liability, not an asset. Collect only what you need, store it securely, and never sell it. That is not just good compliance. It is increasingly good business.

## The Road Ahead

Virginia's law is a step. It is not a destination. The real test will be whether other states follow, whether Congress ever passes a federal privacy standard, and whether enforcement actually reaches the data brokers who operate in the shadows of the digital economy.

The Hacker News discussion around this story revealed something telling. Even in a community of technically sophisticated users, many were surprised to learn how trivially their location data was being bought and sold. The revelation that your precise coordinates could be purchased by any company willing to pay was, for some, genuinely unsettling. That shock is the beginning of political demand.

What happens next depends on whether that demand translates into more laws, better enforcement, and ultimately a shift in how the tech industry treats personal data. Virginia has opened a door. Whether others walk through it — and whether the door stays open — remains to be seen.
