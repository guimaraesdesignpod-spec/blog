# The AI Jobs Hysteria Is Mostly Wrong — Here's What the Data Actually Says

Haven't you heard? White-collar jobs are going away, decimated by artificial intelligence. The headlines have been relentless. Waves of tech layoffs at Coinbase, Meta, and Cisco are framed as the opening act of a broader bloodbath. Axios warned of an "AI jobs apocalypse" that will destroy much of white-collar work. The New York Times raised the specter of a "permanent underclass" displaced by machines. Even Dario Amodei, co-founder of Anthropic, predicted AI would wipe out 50 percent of white-collar jobs within five years.

Before you trade your laptop for a plumber's wrench, it is worth asking a simple question: what do the actual numbers say?

The short answer is: not what the headlines claim.

MIT Technology Review published a comprehensive reality check this week, diving deep into the Bureau of Labor Statistics data, Stanford research, and Federal Reserve studies. The picture that emerges is far more nuanced — and far less apocalyptic — than the discourse suggests. AI is indeed reshaping work. But it is not eating the labor market alive. At least not yet.

## The Unemployment Paradox

Here is a fact that should give pause to anyone predicting mass white-collar unemployment. According to an analysis by the Economic Innovation Group, the unemployment rate for jobs most exposed to AI is actually *lower* than the rate for occupations less exposed to the technology. That is right: the very jobs supposedly on the chopping block are, in aggregate, doing better than safer ones.

Yale's Budget Lab came to a similar conclusion. Their research tracking the impact of AI on the labor market found no signs that large numbers of people are fleeing threatened occupations for supposedly safer manual-labor jobs. If AI were truly decimating white-collar work, you would expect a migration pattern. Workers would be moving. The data shows they are not.

"All of the available evidence to date suggests that AI's impact on current labor market conditions is likely small right now," says Erika McEntarfer, a labor economist who headed the Bureau of Labor Statistics until late 2025. McEntarfer, now a fellow at Stanford's Institute for Economic Policy Research, points out that only one in five US companies are using AI in any business function according to Census data. "The data are a great reality check on the fear that AI will be enormously disruptive," she says. "It could be. It likely will be disruptive, but the data is telling us right now that disruption is not yet here, and that we have time to plan."

That does not mean the job market is healthy. It is not. Recent college graduates face an unemployment rate of roughly 5.6%, the highest since the pandemic. Hiring rates have been dismal. Young people trying to enter the workforce in tech feel like no one is hiring. The question is whether AI is the primary culprit — or just one factor among many in a sluggish, post-pandemic labor market shaped by higher interest rates, reduced venture capital, and broader economic caution.

## The Young Are Getting Hit First

If there is one place where AI's footprint on employment is visible, it is at the entry level.

Researchers at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab — Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen — analyzed a massive dataset from ADP, the world's largest payroll provider. Their paper, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts About the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence," is the most granular look yet at how AI is affecting specific age groups and occupations.

Their findings are striking. For 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations — primarily software development and customer service — employment has dropped by approximately 16% since late 2022, when ChatGPT was released. The decline for 26-to-30-year-olds is smaller but still present. Meanwhile, older workers in the exact same occupations show no meaningful change in employment trends. The effect is concentrated almost entirely on the youngest workers.

Why the young? One explanation centers on the distinction between codified knowledge and tacit knowledge. Codified knowledge is what you learn from textbooks, courses, and formal education. Tacit knowledge is what you accumulate through experience, judgment, and doing the work. AI is exceptionally good at codified knowledge. It can write entry-level code, draft standard customer responses, and perform routine analytical tasks. What it cannot yet replicate is the accumulated wisdom of someone who has spent years debugging complex systems, navigating ambiguous client requirements, or knowing which corners can be cut and which cannot.

As a result, entry-level tasks that once served as apprenticeship — the grunt work that taught young developers how systems actually work — are increasingly automatable. The earn-while-you-learn career ladder may be breaking, at least in some fields. That is a serious problem. But it is not the same as mass unemployment.

## Coder Jobs Are Still Growing

The Federal Reserve Board published a detailed study in March 2026, "AI and Coder Employment: Compiling the Evidence," that brings rigorous econometrics to the question. Using linked O*NET and Current Population Survey data, researchers Leland Crane and Paul Soto identified coding-intensive occupations and tracked their employment trends before and after ChatGPT's November 2022 release.

Their finding: aggregate coder employment growth decelerated by roughly 3% annually post-ChatGPT. But here is the critical detail: *overall employment for coders continues to grow*. It is just growing more slowly than it did before 2022. The researchers controlled for industry-level shocks and still found an occupation-specific effect, confirming that something real is happening to coding jobs. But that something is a slowdown, not a collapse.

This aligns with data from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas. Their February 2026 analysis showed that employment in computer systems design declined about 5% since late 2022, while the broader US economy added roughly 2.5% to total employment. Yet wages in the computer systems design sector rose 16.7% since fall 2022, versus just 7.5% nationwide. The AI-exposed sectors are hiring fewer people but paying the remaining ones more.

The Dallas Fed economists — Scott Davis and Tyler Atkinson — argue this pattern makes sense if you distinguish between automation and augmentation. When AI automates routine tasks, fewer entry-level workers are needed. When it augments experienced workers, their productivity rises and so does their value. The result is a bifurcation: fewer junior roles, higher pay for seniors, and an overall sector that is shrinking in headcount but not in economic importance.

## The Historical Pattern Repeats

There is another reason for skepticism about the AI jobs apocalypse: we have been here before. And we were wrong then too.

MIT Technology Review's David Rotman, who wrote the 2013 article "How Technology Is Destroying Jobs" — one of the early canonical warnings about AI-driven unemployment — notes that none of those predictions came true. In 2016, President Obama's outgoing economic advisors warned that automated vehicles could eliminate 2.2 to 3.1 million US jobs. Geoffrey Hinton, a pioneer of deep learning, said people should "stop training radiologists" because AI would replace them. Today, there are more radiologists than ever. The driverless truck fleets are still not here.

David Autor, the MIT labor economist who has studied technology and employment for decades, published new research this week in the *Annual Review of Economics* examining how technology historically creates new work. Autor and his colleagues found that about 60% of US jobs from 1940 to 2018 were in specialties that did not broadly exist before 1940. New technology does not just eliminate jobs. It invents new categories of work.

Autor's data shows that new work disproportionately benefits young, educated workers in urban settings. It also shows that the wage premium for new work fades over time as expertise becomes common knowledge. Driving a car was once a scarce skill. So was using Microsoft Word. Eventually, both became baseline expectations. The same will likely happen with AI-assisted work. Today's "prompt engineer" or "AI operations specialist" may become tomorrow's standard job requirement.

## What We Actually Don't Know

If the honest economists agree on one thing, it is this: we are flying blind.

David Deming, a Harvard economist, has been surveying thousands of workers every three months since 2024, asking basic questions about generative AI use. His findings are instructive: roughly 40% of workers use AI at work, adoption varies enormously by sector, and the productivity gains are real but modest. "We're sort of flying blind," Deming told MIT Technology Review. The existing government surveys were never designed to track AI's impact on specific tasks within jobs. We know AI is being used. We do not know precisely how, or what it is replacing, or what new tasks it is creating.

Erik Brynjolfsson, the Stanford economist who is arguably more optimistic about AI's economic potential than anyone, warns that a lack of data is severely limiting our visibility. "We're not investing even 1% of that on understanding the transition," he says, referring to the hundreds of billions spent on AI infrastructure. At a time when the technology is reshaping industries, our measurement tools are stuck in the 20th century.

This matters because policy depends on data. Two decades ago, the "China shock" devastated manufacturing communities because policymakers did not see the damage in real time. It took years for researchers to document what free trade had done to specific towns. The AI transition, if it comes, will be far larger. Without better data, we risk repeating the same mistake — just with white-collar workers instead of factory towns.

## The Real Risk Is the Transition, Not the End of Work

Here is where the data and the doomsayers partially converge. Even if mass unemployment is not here, the transition could still be brutal.

Jed Kolko, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics and former undersecretary of commerce, puts it clearly: "Even if there is not mass or even increased unemployment, the transition could still be very difficult. It means people losing jobs, or people's jobs being redefined in ways that make those jobs pay worse or be less meaningful. And some people whose jobs are threatened may not be able to adapt."

That is the real story. Not a sci-fi scenario where algorithms replace entire professions overnight, but a grinding, uneven transition where entry-level paths narrow, experienced workers get paid more for doing different work, and some people fall through the cracks entirely. The 22-year-old who cannot get their first coding job. The mid-career analyst whose tasks are automated but who lacks the skills for the new AI-augmented role. The customer service worker who is not fired but whose job is hollowed out until it becomes unrecognizable.

McEntarfer's most important point is about speed. "If it happens at the normal pace of technological change, labor markets will have time to adapt. If there is a sudden and severe disruption, then that will be a big challenge for policymakers," she says. "That's really the most important question facing us right now: how rapid this transformation is going to be."

The data so far suggests we have time. But time is only useful if we use it.

## What the Numbers Mean for You

If you are a young worker in an AI-exposed field, the message is not comforting, but it is actionable. The traditional career ladder — learn basics in school, get an entry-level job, accumulate experience — is fraying at the bottom rung. You will likely need to develop skills that are harder to automate: judgment, client interaction, cross-functional coordination, domain expertise that goes beyond textbook knowledge.

If you are an experienced worker, the news is better but not complacent. Your tacit knowledge is your moat. But moats erode. The radiologists who thrived were the ones who adapted to AI as a tool rather than treating it as a threat. The same will be true in software, finance, law, and every other knowledge profession.

And if you are a policymaker, business leader, or educator, the clearest takeaway is this: invest in measurement. We are making trillion-dollar bets on AI's economic impact with survey tools designed for a pre-digital economy. The BLS, the Census, and academic researchers need better, more granular data. Without it, we are planning for a transition we cannot see.

The AI jobs apocalypse is not here. The AI jobs transition is. And the difference between those two things — between panic and preparation — may determine who thrives and who struggles in the decade ahead.
