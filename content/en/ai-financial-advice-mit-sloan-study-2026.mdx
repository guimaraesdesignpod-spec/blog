# AI Financial Advice Is Surprisingly Good — But Your Prompts Are Making You Poorer

About a year ago, I asked Claude what to do with a small windfall. The answer was predictably sensible: max out tax-advantaged accounts, build an emergency fund, then dump the rest into a low-cost index fund. Nothing I couldn't have read on a Bogleheads forum circa 2012. What struck me wasn't the advice itself. It was how *fast* I got it, how *tailored* it felt to my exact situation, and how tempted I was to follow it without a second opinion.

Half of Americans are now doing the same thing, according to J.D. Power. They're asking ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini whether they should pay off student loans or invest, how much house they can afford, or whether crypto belongs in a retirement portfolio. The advice is free, instant, and available at 2 AM when your anxiety about money won't let you sleep. But here's the uncomfortable question almost nobody is asking: **How good is it, really?**

A new working paper from MIT Sloan School of Management — "AI Financial Advice: Supply, Demand, and Life Cycle Implications" by Taha Choukhmane and co-authors — attempts to answer that question with something most prior studies lacked: an entire simulated lifetime. The researchers didn't just ask an LLM for stock picks and grade them. They built a quantitative lifecycle model of income, employment, taxes, and mortality. They surveyed 1,000 real adults, collected the prompts those people actually write when seeking financial advice, fed them to GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash, translated the textual responses into quantitative recommendations, and simulated what would happen if those people followed that advice from age 22 to age 89.

The results are both encouraging and deeply unsettling.

## The Good News: AI Is Better Than Most of Us

Let's start with the headline finding, because it genuinely surprised the researchers too.

"We were somewhat surprised by how good the advice was," lead author Taha Choukhmane told MIT Sloan's Ideas Made to Matter. "Especially when you read the kind of questions people asked, it was not a given that the advice would line up with what academics think are good financial principles."

And yet it largely did.

Across the simulated lifetimes, LLM advice consistently pushed people toward behaviors that lifecycle finance theory considers optimal: save during working years, invest heavily in diversified stock funds while young, gradually reduce equity exposure after age 45, and draw down savings in retirement. Following AI advice moved virtually all simulated individuals above age 30 toward sizeable saving buffers. Stock market participation jumped to over 99%. Risky asset allocations were mostly in diversified equity funds, with only low single-digit percentages in individual stocks, gold, or cryptocurrency.

In other words, the AI was essentially giving out the advice you'd find on that famous index card: max your retirement contributions, buy inexpensive diversified funds, never trade individual securities, save 20% of your money, pay off credit cards in full. The kind of advice that, as one Hacker News commenter noted, "you could replace... with a piece of paper" and still outperform what many people actually do.

But the fact that this advice is now being delivered by an infinitely patient, always-available conversational agent matters. For people who can't afford a human financial advisor — which is most people — this is a genuine democratization of financial guidance. Choukhmane put it plainly: "A lot of the people who would benefit from financial advice are precisely the people who don't have a lot of resources."

## The Hidden Trap: Your Words Shape Your Wealth

Here's where the study gets interesting. The researchers didn't stop at "AI gives good advice on average." They asked a more precise question: *Whose* advice, exactly, and *how* does it vary?

The answer: dramatically.

When researchers split the 1,000 survey respondents by gender, financial literacy, and prior AI experience, they found that simulated retirement wealth differed by 4–5% across groups. In absolute terms, that's roughly $50,000 to $100,000 less in retirement savings — simply because of who was asking the question and how they were asking it.

**Gender** produced one of the starkest divides. The LLM recommended higher equity allocations in response to prompts written by men. Over a lifetime, this compounded into roughly $50,000 lower wealth at age 60 for women. Two-thirds of this gap came from demand: men and women literally asked different questions. Men used words like "strategy," "crypto," and "growth." Women used "family," "grocery," and "pay." The models responded to the vocabulary, not just the intent.

But one-third of the gap came from supply — the model itself. When researchers took prompts that lacked explicit gender identifiers and randomly labeled them as coming from a man or a woman, the LLM still recommended lower equity shares for the "woman" version. This could reflect reasonable inferences about different preferences or life circumstances. Or it could reflect biases learned from training data. The study can't distinguish which, and neither can the user receiving the advice.

**Financial literacy** mattered just as much. Users who scored high on financial literacy tests received advice that generated about 5% more retirement wealth. Their prompts were more specific, included more relevant context, and referenced concepts the LLM could work with.

**Prior AI experience** was perhaps the most striking. First-time users of AI for financial advice got recommendations with lower saving rates, leaving them with almost $100,000 less wealth at age 60 compared to people who had used AI for financial advice before. The novices didn't ask worse questions in an absolute sense — they simply didn't know how to frame financial decisions in ways that unlock the model's best reasoning.

The lesson is uncomfortable: **AI financial advice is not a level playing field. The same model, asked the same underlying question in different words by different people, produces meaningfully different lifetime outcomes.**

## Where the Machines Still Fail

Even with perfect prompts, the study identified three persistent weaknesses that no tested LLM could overcome.

**First: over-reliance on heuristics.** The LLMs loved simple rules of thumb. Save X percent. Withdraw Y percent in retirement. These are fine starting points, but lifecycle theory calls for dynamic adjustment based on actual wealth, income trajectories, and market conditions. The AI rarely did this. It gave the same basic advice at 35 with $50,000 saved as it might at 55 with $500,000.

**Second: poor shock response.** When simulated individuals lost their jobs, the AI advised cutting consumption sharply — even when those individuals had substantial liquid savings that could have smoothed the blow. Lifecycle theory says you should draw down savings during bad times and replenish during good times. The AI treated unemployment like an emergency requiring immediate austerity, regardless of actual financial cushion.

**Third: no active rebalancing.** This might be the most technically important finding. Portfolio allocations drifted passively with market returns. If stocks had a good run, the equity share grew. If bonds outperformed, it shrank. The LLMs almost never recommended actively rebalancing back to target allocations. Over decades, this drift can meaningfully alter risk profiles — usually in ways that increase risk for older investors at exactly the wrong time.

Interestingly, when researchers replaced user-written prompts with structured "academic" prompts — including explicit assumptions about life expectancy, retirement age, employment risk, income risk, tax law, and Social Security rules — the advice improved on consumption smoothing and heuristic reduction. But it still did not fix the rebalancing problem. That appears to be a deeper limitation of how these models reason about dynamic portfolio management.

## The Product Placement Problem

Here's a subtle finding that should worry anyone watching how AI reshapes commerce.

The study found that LLMs frequently recommended specific financial products and providers that users never mentioned. Vanguard appeared in 6% of responses. iShares appeared in 3.4%. Fewer than 0.4% of prompts mentioned either company.

This isn't necessarily nefarious. These are large, well-known, low-cost providers that genuinely align with the advice being given. But it raises a question that will become urgent as AI advice scales: Who controls the default recommendations? If millions of people are asking AI where to invest, and the AI consistently names the same three providers, that's a massive shift in customer acquisition economics. Financial firms may soon optimize less for Google search rankings and more for "LLM mention frequency."

Choukhmane notes that "attracting customers' attention may depend less on traditional marketing or search visibility and more on whether and how their products are described by LLMs when consumers seek advice." This is the SEO paradigm applied to conversational AI — and it's arriving faster than most firms realize.

## What This Means for You

If you're currently using AI for financial advice — and statistically, half of you are — the MIT Sloan study offers three practical takeaways.

**First, treat AI as a tutor, not an oracle.** Choukhmane's strongest recommendation for consumers is to use AI to build financial understanding rather than blindly following its output. Ask it to explain concepts. Use it to compare strategies. But verify anything that matters with a human advisor, especially for complex situations involving taxes, inheritance, or business structures.

**Second, invest in your prompts.** The $100,000 gap between first-time and experienced AI users is essentially a literacy tax. The more you understand about lifecycle planning, portfolio theory, and tax-advantaged accounts, the better questions you'll ask — and the better answers you'll get. Specificity matters. "Where should I invest $500 a month?" gets a generic answer. "I'm 34, married, earning $95,000, with $30,000 in a 401(k) and $15,000 in a taxable brokerage. Assuming I retire at 65 and current tax law holds, how should I allocate new savings between Roth IRA, 401(k), and a total market index fund?" gets something much closer to professional-grade advice.

**Third, check for drift.** The rebalancing failure is a real, ongoing risk. If you're using AI for portfolio management, don't assume it's monitoring your allocations. Set calendar reminders to rebalance annually, or use a target-date fund that does it automatically. The AI can help you choose the fund. It won't reliably tell you when to rebalance the underlying assets.

## What This Means for the Industry

For financial services firms, the MIT Sloan paper is a wake-up call on two fronts.

The democratization opportunity is real. Robo-advisors already proved that automated advice can improve outcomes for people who can't afford human advisors. LLMs take that accessibility to a new level — but they also introduce new risks around consistency, bias, and product recommendation opacity.

The regulatory question is looming. If half of Americans are using AI for financial advice, and that advice varies systematically by gender and financial literacy, what obligations should the providers of that advice have? Should LLMs be required to disclose when they recommend specific products? Should they be tested for demographic bias before deployment in financial contexts? The paper doesn't answer these questions, but it provides the empirical foundation that regulators will need.

And for the AI labs themselves, the rebalancing and shock-response failures point to a deeper research problem. LLMs are trained on text, not on dynamic optimization over stochastic lifetimes. Giving good *static* advice about what to do with $500 is different from giving good *dynamic* advice about how to adjust when you lose your job at 47 with two kids in college. The latter requires modeling forward-looking optimization under uncertainty — something current architectures handle poorly.

## The Bottom Line

The most honest summary of this study is also the most uncomfortable one: **AI financial advice is surprisingly good at the things most people get wrong, and surprisingly bad at the things that matter most when life gets complicated.**

It will nudge you toward saving more, diversifying broadly, and taking less risk as you age. These are genuinely valuable corrections to common human biases. If every American followed the baseline advice in this study, average retirement wealth would likely increase.

But it will also give you worse advice if you're a woman, if you've never used AI before, if you don't know the vocabulary of finance, or if you hit an unexpected life shock. The model isn't malicious. It's just mirroring the patterns in its training data and the cues in your prompts — and those patterns contain the same inequalities that already exist in human financial advising.

The real intelligence, as one Hacker News commenter dryly observed, "was inside us all along." The LLM is a mirror. A very useful, very fast, very patient mirror. But what it reflects depends entirely on what you show it — and not everyone is holding up the same picture.
