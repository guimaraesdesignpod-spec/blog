# The EU Chat Control Resurrection: How an Expired Law Came Back from the Dead

## The Vote That Was Supposed to End It

On 26 March 2026, the European Parliament held what many assumed would be the final word on one of the most controversial surveillance proposals in EU history. The vote was on whether to extend Chat Control 1.0 — a temporary regulation that had allowed platforms like WhatsApp and Meta Messenger to voluntarily scan private messages for child sexual abuse material.

The result was razor-thin: 307 votes against extension, 306 in favour, 24 abstentions. The critical Amendment 34, which rejected automated assessment of unknown photos and texts, passed by a single vote: 307 to 306. The civil-liberties committee had already rejected the draft extension 38 to 28. The Parliament's compromise position — extend only to 2027, with targeted detection of known content, no scanning of end-to-end encrypted communications, and judicial oversight — had been ignored by the Council. On 4 April 2026, the derogation expired. Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Snap stated they would continue scanning anyway, but the legal basis for voluntary mass surveillance was gone.

That should have been the end of the story. It wasn't.

## The Council's End-Run

In late June 2026 — just three days before the final scheduled trilogue on the permanent Chat Control 2.0 regulation — EU ambassadors moved to resurrect the expired Chat Control 1.0. Because an expired regulation cannot technically be extended, the Council proposed a formally "new" law with identical content, pushed through an expedited procedure.

Patrick Breyer, the former Member of the European Parliament who has tracked this issue for years, called the move "a blatant disregard for democratic processes." The attempt is unprecedented in EU legislative history: a body of national governments trying to revive a law that a directly elected parliament had just voted to kill, using procedural tricks to bypass the normal democratic machinery.

The timing was not accidental. By reviving the temporary scanning regime just before the final negotiations on the permanent regulation, the Council creates pressure on Parliament negotiators. The message is clear: if you don't accept our terms on Chat Control 2.0, we'll keep the temporary regime alive indefinitely.

## Two Laws, One Goal: Understanding the Double Threat

Nearly every news headline about "Chat Control" blurs two separate proposals together. Keeping them distinct is essential to understanding what is actually happening.

**Chat Control 1.0** is the temporary derogation — a time-limited exception that permits voluntary scanning. It has expired and is now the subject of an attempted resurrection.

**Chat Control 2.0** is the permanent CSA Regulation — the big one. This would make scanning a legal obligation enforced through "detection orders," not a voluntary choice. This is the text currently being fought over in trilogue negotiations between Parliament, Council, and Commission.

The confusion is deliberate. When supporters say "Chat Control protects children," they are talking about 2.0. When critics say "Parliament already rejected Chat Control," they are talking about 1.0. When someone tells you "Chat Control passed" or "Chat Control is dead," your first question should always be: which one?

## Why Encryption Cannot Survive Client-Side Scanning

Here is the technical reality that every argument about Chat Control eventually runs into. If a message is end-to-end encrypted, nobody in the middle — not the network, not the platform — can read it in transit. That is the entire point of encryption. So to satisfy a scanning mandate, the inspection has to move to where the message is still readable: your device.

There are two approaches that keep surfacing in the negotiations:

**Client-side scanning (CSS)** installs software on your phone that analyses content before it is encrypted and sent. The encryption technically stays intact, but the private moment — the instant before the lock closes — gets inspected. Critics argue this hollows out end-to-end encryption entirely and bolts a permanent surveillance layer onto every phone.

**"Risk mitigation" obligations** are softer language that appears even in diluted drafts. They keep age-verification mandates and a broad enough "risk mitigation" obligation to push encrypted services toward weakening their own protocols — without ever writing the word "encryption" into the ban itself.

The line that matters: over 500 scientists and cryptographers have publicly called client-side scanning "technically infeasible." You cannot build a scanning mechanism that only ever catches criminals. Anything that can inspect content before encryption can be repointed at other content later. It is not a backdoor. It is a front door that is wired to stay open, and you do not control who walks through it next.

Signal, the end-to-end encrypted messaging app, has already said it would leave the EU market rather than comply. Meredith Whittaker, president of the Signal Foundation, put it bluntly: "If we were given the choice of either undermining the encryption that billions of people depend on or leaving the European market, we would leave."

## The Germany Question

Blocking a proposal in the EU Council requires a blocking minority — at least four countries representing more than 35% of the EU population. That math is the whole game, and Germany is the linchpin.

Germany alone represents roughly 19% of the EU's population. When Berlin says no, a blocking minority becomes reachable. When Berlin wavers, the proposal is instantly back in play.

The countries pushing for scanning — Denmark, Ireland, Spain, Italy, and others — have been relentless. The countries resisting — Germany, Poland, Austria, Estonia, Slovenia, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Finland, Czech Republic — have held the line so far, but the pressure is intensifying.

German Federal Justice Minister Stefanie Hubig stated in October 2025 that "Germany will not agree to" the proposal. But Germany has wavered before under pressure, and the Commission's Home Affairs Unit — described by digital rights groups as a "notorious and illegal lobbyist for its own proposal" — has been pulling out all the stops.

## What the Permanent Regulation Actually Says

The Chat Control 2.0 text — the CSA Regulation — has evolved through years of negotiation, but its core remains unchanged. Service providers would be required to detect, report, and remove child sexual abuse material. Detection orders would be issued by authorities requiring specific platforms to scan for known CSAM, new CSAM, and "grooming" indicators.

The Parliament's position, adopted back in November 2023, ruled out mandatory client-side scanning of encrypted messages. The Council's position, endorsed in late 2025, maintains what critics call coercive "risk mitigation" obligations that would effectively pressure platforms into scanning anyway.

The fifth and expected-final trilogue was held on 29 June 2026. As of early July, no confirmed "deal reached" or "talks collapsed" announcement has surfaced. If a political agreement was struck, formal adoption by Parliament and Council is expected this month.

## The Stakes: Not Just Privacy, But Competitive Architecture

The Chat Control debate is usually framed as a privacy-versus-security trade-off. That framing is incomplete. The stakes extend to the entire competitive architecture of European digital services.

If client-side scanning becomes mandatory, every messaging app, every cloud storage service, every photo-sharing platform in the EU must build surveillance infrastructure into its product. This is not a compliance checkbox. It is a fundamental redesign of how software works on European devices.

Small providers, unable to build their own scanning systems, get nudged toward third-party scanning tools — which centralises a sensitive capability in even fewer hands. Foreign competitors, operating outside EU jurisdiction, face no such requirement. The result is a European digital market where domestic services are surveilled by default and foreign competitors are not, creating exactly the kind of competitive disadvantage that the EU's Digital Markets Act was designed to prevent.

And then there is the chilling effect. Lawyers, journalists, doctors, whistle-blowers, activists, people seeking reproductive healthcare — anyone who depends on confidential digital communication — must now factor in the possibility that an AI filter is reading their messages before they are sent. The presumption of privacy inverts into a presumption of inspection.

## What Happens Now

As of 8 July 2026, three things are happening simultaneously:

First, the Council's attempt to resurrect Chat Control 1.0 through an expedited "new" temporary law is still moving. Whether it succeeds depends on procedural manoeuvring and whether Parliament can block it.

Second, the final outcome of the 29 June trilogue on Chat Control 2.0 has not been publicly confirmed. If a deal was reached, formal adoption moves quickly. If talks collapsed, the proposal stalls — but the Council's 1.0 resurrection attempt gives them leverage to force Parliament back to the table.

Third, public pressure continues. The advocacy against Chat Control has been unprecedented — over 500 scientists, digital rights groups like EDRi and its Stop Scanning Me campaign, and ordinary citizens contacting MEPs. That 307–306 vote in March did not happen by accident. It happened because enough people made enough noise that wavering MEPs felt pressure.

The EU prides itself on being a regulatory superpower — the Brussels Effect, where EU standards become global standards. If the EU mandates client-side scanning, other jurisdictions will follow. If the EU preserves end-to-end encryption, it sets a global precedent for digital privacy.

What happens in July 2026 will determine which precedent the EU sets. And the law that Parliament thought it had killed is sitting up in its coffin, waiting to see whether anyone stops it from walking back out.
