# Apple Sues OpenAI for Industrial Espionage: The Dark Side of the AI Talent War

On July 10, 2026, Apple filed a 41-page lawsuit in the Northern District of California that reads less like a standard trade-secret complaint and more like a Silicon Valley spy thriller. The Cupertino giant accused OpenAI of orchestrating a months-long scheme to steal confidential information, recruit moles inside Apple's hardware division, and fast-track development of the AI device that Jony Ive has been quietly designing.

The allegations are damning. The evidence, if it holds up, could reshape how the tech industry thinks about talent mobility, corporate ethics, and the increasingly cutthroat race to build AI hardware.

## From Partnership to Courtroom

Just two years ago, Apple and OpenAI were partners. ChatGPT was woven into Siri. OpenAI got distribution to a billion Apple users. Apple got a veneer of AI credibility without building its own frontier model.

That partnership has been unraveling for months. In May, Bloomberg reported that OpenAI was considering legal action against Apple, claiming the Siri integration had failed to deliver the user growth OpenAI had been promised. Apple, meanwhile, was preparing to open its platform to rival AI providers — Anthropic, Perplexity, and others — diluting OpenAI's exclusivity.

Now the relationship has gone nuclear.

Apple's lawsuit accuses OpenAI of "trade secret misappropriation to free-ride off Apple's decades of innovation." It names two former Apple employees as central figures in what Apple describes as a culture of theft normalized at OpenAI's highest levels.

## The Alleged Scheme

The lawsuit centers on Tang Yew Tan, OpenAI's hardware lead and a former Apple designer, and Chang Liu, a former Apple electrical engineer who joined OpenAI's hardware team.

According to Apple, Tan used his internal knowledge of Apple's exit procedures to help departing employees covertly deliver information to OpenAI. The company claims Tan obtained an internal Apple managers' document marked "Need to Know" that describes security protocols for employee departures — and then shared it with new hires before they gave notice to Apple.

The text messages cited in the complaint are blunt. Tan allegedly warned recruits not to tell Apple they had taken jobs at OpenAI, so they could "stay at Apple as long as they can." Apple's investigation found a pattern of OpenAI-bound employees emailing themselves confidential information, bringing physical hardware components to interviews, and taking steps to evade the security processes intended to protect Apple's intellectual property.

In one instance, Apple says evidence on an employee's work-issued device shows Tan instructed her to "bring some parts" she worked on to an interview — suggesting she show OpenAI batteries, SIPs, logic boards, and other hardware. It was not, Apple claims, an isolated incident.

## The Laptop That Wouldn't Die

If the allegations against Tan sound like calculated corporate espionage, the claims against Chang Liu read like something out of a bad heist movie.

Liu allegedly failed to return an Apple-issued laptop after departing the company. Then, while already working at OpenAI, he exploited a vulnerability in Apple's systems to download dozens of confidential documents. When he realized he still had access to Apple's network storage, he didn't report it. He texted a former colleague who was still at Apple: "LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny."

That same colleague, Yu-Ting "Alyssa" Peng, allegedly continued to provide Liu with updates on Apple's projects, vendor decisions, and engineering details after he left — effectively operating as a mole inside Apple's hardware division.

Apple says it discovered the pattern in February and attempted to contact OpenAI. The company claims OpenAI did not respond, prompting Apple to investigate further and eventually file suit.

## Tricking the Supply Chain

Perhaps the most audacious allegation involves Apple's suppliers.

Apple claims OpenAI used confidential Apple hardware information when approaching Apple suppliers — and in at least one case, tricked a company into using a "specific trade secret metal-finishing technique" for an OpenAI device by falsely claiming it had Apple's permission to do so.

This is not just theft of documents. It is, if true, the misappropriation of manufacturing processes and vendor relationships built over decades — the kind of industrial infrastructure that cannot be replicated simply by hiring smart engineers.

Apple's lawsuit frames OpenAI's hardware ambitions as fundamentally compromised: "OpenAI's nascent hardware business now rests on the shakiest of foundations, rotten to its core by its illegal reliance on misappropriated trade secrets."

## Context: The AI Hardware Race

OpenAI's push into hardware has been an open secret. The company acquired io Products, Jony Ive's design firm, to develop what industry observers expect will be a dedicated AI device — something between a smartphone and an ambient companion. Prior reports suggested the device had been delayed to 2027, with OpenAI struggling to define what a post-phone AI gadget should actually do.

Apple's lawsuit adds a darker dimension to that delay. If OpenAI's hardware team was under enough pressure to allegedly steal supplier information and metal-finishing techniques, it suggests the company's ambitions may have outpaced its ability to execute legitimately.

More than 400 former Apple employees now work at OpenAI, according to the lawsuit. In an industry where talent poaching is routine, Apple is careful to note that it is not targeting OpenAI's recruitment of its staff. What it is targeting is the alleged systematic extraction of confidential information — a distinction that matters for both the legal case and the broader narrative about Silicon Valley's culture.

## What This Means for Silicon Valley

The lawsuit lands at a moment when the tech industry is already grappling with the ethics of AI development. Elon Musk's $134 billion lawsuit against OpenAI was dismissed on a technicality in May, but the underlying tension — between nonprofit ideals and for-profit execution — has not gone away. Now Apple is raising a parallel question: what happens when the race to build AI hardware creates incentives for corporate espionage?

For engineers, the case is a reminder that talent mobility has limits. Bringing your expertise to a new employer is standard practice. Bringing your former employer's confidential documents, supplier relationships, and manufacturing processes is not. The text messages cited in Apple's complaint suggest the defendants knew the difference — and chose to ignore it.

For OpenAI, the stakes are existential. If Apple wins an injunction barring OpenAI from using the allegedly stolen technologies, the company's hardware roadmap could face years of delays. If the reputational damage is severe enough, it could also affect OpenAI's ability to recruit from Apple and other top hardware shops in the future.

For Apple, the lawsuit is as much about deterrence as damages. The company has built its brand on secrecy and vertical integration. Allowing those walls to be breached without a fierce legal response would signal weakness to every competitor eyeing its supply chain.

## The Road Ahead

Apple is seeking an injunction to stop OpenAI from possessing, using, or disclosing its technologies, plus damages "in an amount to be determined at trial." It is also suing Tan and Liu individually for breach of contract.

Jony Ive is not named in the suit. Sam Altman is referenced but not named as a defendant. Apple's statement to MacRumors made clear that the lawsuit is about protecting employees, not ending the ChatGPT-Siri partnership — though it is hard to imagine that partnership surviving this level of acrimony.

The case will take years to resolve. But the filing itself has already changed the conversation. In an industry that prides itself on moving fast and breaking things, Apple is asking a federal court to decide just how many things are allowed to break — and who gets to pick up the pieces.
