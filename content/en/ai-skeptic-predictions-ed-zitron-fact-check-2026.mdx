# When the Skeptic Becomes the Hype: Fact-Checking Ed Zitron's AI Predictions

There is a moment in every technology wave when the skeptics start to look a lot like the boosters they claim to oppose. Not because they start believing in the technology, but because they begin making the same mistakes: overstating their case, cherry-picking data, and building an audience on emotion rather than evidence.

Dan Luu just did something that very few people in tech discourse have bothered to do. He sat down and checked the receipts.

Luu, a systems engineer who writes some of the most thorough technical analysis on the internet, took the predictions of Ed Zitron — probably the most widely cited AI skeptic in the English-speaking tech world — and compared them against what actually happened. The result is not just a takedown of one commentator. It is a case study in how contrarianism can become its own form of industrial-grade misinformation.

## The Skeptic Who Became a Fixture

If you have spent any time on Hacker News, LinkedIn, or tech Twitter in the past two years, you have seen Ed Zitron's work. His newsletter *Where's Your Ed At* and his podcast *Better Offline* have carved out a massive audience by telling a simple, seductive story: generative AI is a scam, the companies building it are burning money they will never recover, and the people who believe in it are either dupes or profiteers.

Zitron's appeal is real. In an environment where every venture capitalist and founder seems to be promising AGI by Tuesday, a voice that says "slow down, this might not work" serves a genuine purpose. The problem, as Luu discovered, is that Zitron does not stop at "this might not work." He predicts specific outcomes, cites numbers to back them up, and delivers those predictions with a level of confidence that would make a weather forecaster blush.

And he has been wrong. Systematically, repeatedly, and often spectacularly wrong.

## The Numbers That Weren't

Let's start with the big one. In November 2024, Zitron gave a talk where he declared that Meta, Google, and Microsoft were "dying" companies that "don't know how to grow anymore." He described their investment in AI as a desperation move — something they were shoving into everything because they had run out of ideas.

Here is what the actual financials looked like:

**Meta's revenue and operating income:**
- 2023: $135 billion in revenue, $47 billion in profit
- 2024: $165 billion in revenue, $69 billion in profit
- 2025: $201 billion in revenue, $83 billion in profit
- First half of 2026: $117 billion in revenue, $42 billion in profit

**Alphabet's numbers:**
- 2023: $307 billion in revenue, $84 billion in profit
- 2024: $350 billion in revenue, $112 billion in profit
- 2025: $403 billion in revenue, $129 billion in profit
- First half of 2026: $230 billion in revenue, $80 billion in profit

**Microsoft:**
- 2023: $228 billion in revenue, $101 billion in profit
- 2024: $262 billion in revenue, $118 billion in profit
- 2025: $305 billion in revenue, $143 billion in profit
- First half of 2026: $173 billion in revenue, $79 billion in profit

These are not the numbers of dying companies. Meta's revenue grew by nearly 50 percent in two years. Alphabet and Microsoft posted consistent double-digit growth across both revenue and profit. The AI investments that Zitron characterized as desperate flailing were, by any conventional measure, delivering exactly the kind of returns that justify capital expenditure.

But Zitron did not just get the outcome wrong. As Luu demonstrates in exhausting detail, the *reasoning* was wrong at every step. To make the case that Meta was dying, Zitron cited alleged monthly active user drops from a third-party analytics service called Similarweb. Luu, who has experience with these tools, notes that third-party tracking numbers are "quite inaccurate and generally useless for anything other than a rough order of magnitude comparison." Meta's own reported numbers showed no such decline.

For Google, Zitron pinned the blame on a single executive — Prabhakar Raghavan — whom he called "truly evil" and "a computer scientist class traitor." The claim was that Raghavan had done grievous damage to Google Search. But Google search engineers who commented on Zitron's rant did not seem to agree with this narrative. And even if Raghavan had caused problems for search, Google has multiple other major revenue engines — YouTube, Google Cloud, advertising networks — that could drive growth regardless.

This is the pattern Luu identifies across Zitron's work: minor, often mischaracterized issues are inflated into evidence for world-historical corporate collapse. It is the rhetorical equivalent of pointing to a cracked window and declaring the building condemned.

## The Complete Record of Wrongness

Luu went through Zitron's public predictions with the patience of an accountant auditing a fraudulent ledger. The results are almost comical in their consistency.

In February 2024, Zitron wrote: "I believe we're reaching the upper limits about what generative AI can do and how accurate its outputs can be." By any measure of model capability in 2024 and 2025, this was wrong.

In March 2024, he published "Have We Reached Peak AI?" with another prediction that hallucinations meant AI progress was limited to then-current levels. Wrong.

In April 2024: "As I previously warned, artificial intelligence companies are running out of data." Wrong — synthetic data, improved training techniques, and larger-scale compute all pushed capabilities forward.

In June 2024: OpenAI growth was stalling and would lead to "some kind of collapse." OpenAI's revenue continued to grow rapidly, and the company is still standing.

In July 2024: "Generative AI models aren't getting more energy-efficient, nor are they getting more 'powerful' in a way that would increase their functionality." Wrong on both counts.

September 2024: "o1 shows that OpenAI is both desperate and out of ideas." The reasoning models that followed o1 represented a genuine architectural advance.

October 2024: OpenAI's forecast of $3.7 billion in 2024 revenue and $11.6 billion in 2025 was "a statement so egregious that I am surprised it's not some kind of financial crime to say it out loud." OpenAI exceeded those forecasts.

January 2025: "I believe we're at peak AI." Wrong.

January 2025: "DeepSeek has commoditized the [LLM]." Wrong — OpenAI and Anthropic maintained significant pricing power.

February 2025: Google's goal of 500 million Gemini users by end of 2025 was "a number so unrealistic that someone at Google should have been fired." Gemini hit 750 million users.

February 2025: "Sam Altman deputizing Orion from GPT-5 to GPT-4.5 suggests that OpenAI has hit a wall." GPT-5 was a substantial improvement.

August 2025: "These models have clearly hit a wall where training is hitting diminishing returns." Wrong.

October 2025: Asked when the AI bubble would pop, Zitron answered "No later than Q2 2026." We are now in September 2026. The bubble has not popped.

November 2025: "These models aren't getting better. What we're seeing today is pretty much what they're always gonna be like." This statement alone, given the improvements from late 2025 through 2026, is almost impressive in its wrongness.

That is twenty-plus major predictions, and the scorecard is essentially a clean sweep of incorrect calls. Not just slightly off. Not arguable either way. Wrong in the specific direction Zitron was most confident about.

## The Spreadsheet That Lied

One of the most damning details Luu uncovered was not a prediction but a piece of analysis. When Zitron tried to compute Anthropic's revenue for 2025, he came up with $3.6 billion and suggested "some funny business" was going on. Another analyst, Timothy B. Lee, looked at Zitron's actual spreadsheet and found basic arithmetic errors: Zitron did not count February 1-10, counted March 1-10 twice, counted a two-month period as one month, and somehow included February 30.

When you fixed the spreadsheet errors, the numbers worked out fine. There was no funny business. Just a guy who could not build a reliable revenue model accusing a company of financial impropriety.

This is emblematic of a broader issue. Zitron is constantly treated as someone who "has looked at the numbers." But looking at numbers and understanding numbers are different skills. Luu, who has worked at companies like Google and has a depth of experience in systems and data analysis, can see where the numbers do not connect to a coherent argument. Most readers cannot. They see spreadsheets and revenue figures and assume the analysis is sound.

## The Gish Gallop Machine

Why has no one done this fact-check before? Luu has an answer, and it comes from Juho Snellman, a respected systems engineer who had already identified the same problems.

Snellman described Zitron's writing as a "10k word gish gallop" — a reference to a rhetorical technique where someone floods their opponent with so many cheap, weak arguments that it is impossible to refute them all. "Every article he writes is a 10k word gish gallop," Snellman wrote. "I've tried debunking key points a few times in HN comments for just one of the intentional mistakes he makes, and people complain about the reply being too long."

Luu confirms this from personal experience. He closed Zitron's November 2024 talk after 90 seconds because "there was so much nonsense that it didn't seem worth the time to go any further." He estimates he could write 5,000 words on the first 90 seconds alone. With time to look up exact facts, it would probably take double or triple that.

This creates a brutal asymmetry. Zitron can produce false claims cheaply and quickly. Correcting them requires expertise, research, and a willingness to write thousands of words that most people will not read. The result is that the false claims circulate while the corrections languish in unread comment threads.

## The Confidence Trap

Perhaps the most illuminating part of Luu's analysis is his observation about confidence calibration.

Zitron does not make modest predictions. He delivers them with rhetorical maximum force. OpenAI's revenue forecast is not just optimistic — it is "a statement so egregious that I am surprised it's not some kind of financial crime to say it out loud." Google's user target is not ambitious — it is "so unrealistic that someone at Google should have been fired." The AI bubble is not coming eventually — it will pop "no later than Q2 2026."

Luu, who has made many predictions himself and tracks his calibration, notes that he has *never* been wrong about a prediction delivered with this level of confidence. "Given the stated level of confidence, even a single incorrect prediction would be a sign of an extremely high degree of overconfidence. One should effectively never be wrong about a prediction delivered with that level of confidence but Zitron is routinely wrong about predictions he makes with what is rhetorically pretty much the highest possible degree of confidence."

There is something almost admirable about this consistency. It takes a special kind of intellectual posture to be wrong at maximum volume, repeatedly, for two and a half years, and never recalibrate.

## The Paul Ehrlich Problem

Luu draws a comparison that might seem extreme but becomes less so the more you think about it: Paul Ehrlich, the biologist who wrote *The Population Bomb* in 1968 and predicted mass starvation, resource depletion, and societal collapse on a timeline that never materialized.

Ehrlich was wrong about India's ability to feed itself. Wrong about England ceasing to exist by 2000. Wrong about the extinction of marine life within a decade. When these predictions failed, he did not recant. He wrote *The Population Explosion* in 1990 and continued making the same apocalyptic claims for another three decades.

"It appears the only reason he's not saying that today is that he died earlier this year," Luu notes dryly.

The comparison is not that Zitron and Ehrlich are equally important or equally wrong about matters of life and death. It is that both found a niche, built an identity around it, and discovered that the niche was more durable than the facts. There is a positive feedback loop in contrarianism: provocative stances get attention, attention reinforces the identity, and eventually the identity becomes impossible to change without destroying the brand.

As Michał Zalewski (lcamtuf) observed in a quote Luu includes: "The surest way to build a popular following is to articulate positions that are crisp, strong, and leave no room for doubt... In fact, there's a positive feedback loop. If you take a provocative, edgy stance, you get more attention and likes, so you sort of... self-radicalize? At some point, it's no longer an opinion that can be changed. It's an identity, a personal brand."

## Why This Matters for AI Discourse

It would be easy to read Luu's post as a simple dunk on a guy who got some predictions wrong. That would be missing the point.

The AI conversation is already polarized enough. On one side, you have boosters who treat every new model release as a step toward superintelligence. On the other, you have skeptics who treat every improvement as illusion and every investment as fraud. What Luu reveals is that the skeptic side has its own hype cycle, its own unreliable oracles, and its own audience rewards for extreme claims.

This is dangerous for two reasons. First, it means people who want a balanced, evidence-based view of AI have fewer places to turn. The boosters are obviously biased by financial incentives. The skeptics are supposed to be the corrective. If the most prominent skeptics are just as careless with evidence as the boosters they criticize, the whole discourse becomes a hall of mirrors.

Second, it means that when Zitron *is* right about something — and even a stopped clock is right twice a day — his credibility on that specific claim is tainted by his broader record. If you have been claiming "the bubble will pop by Q2 2026" since late 2025, and then something genuinely problematic happens in the AI industry in late 2026, your prior false alarms make it harder for anyone to take you seriously.

## The Better Skeptics

Luu ends with a crucial point: there *are* well-reasoned anti-AI takes. He specifically names whitequark, a security researcher whose skeptical arguments about AI he finds intellectually serious even when he disagrees with them. The difference is that whitequark does not build an audience on anger, does not make specific predictions with false confidence, and does not misrepresent data.

"For anyone looking for well-reasoned anti-AI takes, I find whitequark to be quite good," Luu writes, "but of course whitequark doesn't draw the kind of big audience that Zitron does."

That is the trade-off, and it is an old one. Nuance does not go viral. A 10,000-word essay carefully weighing the evidence does not get shared 10,000 times on LinkedIn. A guy saying "this is all a scam and everyone involved is evil" does.

## What to Do With This Information

If you are reading this, you are probably someone who cares about understanding AI accurately — neither as a miracle technology nor as a fraud, but as a set of real capabilities with real limitations and real economic consequences.

Dan Luu's post is a gift to that project. It is not a defense of AI companies. Luu explicitly states that he has no particular pro-AI position, that he has previously written about how people underestimate AI's job displacement potential, and that he believes Google Search has serious quality problems that are getting worse.

What Luu is defending is something more basic: the idea that when you claim to have "looked at the numbers," the numbers should actually support your argument. That when you say a company is "dying," you should mean something that a reasonable person with access to financial statements would recognize as true. That when you predict the future, you should track whether your predictions come true and update your beliefs accordingly.

These are not high bars. They are the minimum requirements for participating in a serious conversation about one of the most consequential technologies of our time.

Ed Zitron has failed to clear that bar, repeatedly, over two and a half years, while building one of the largest audiences in tech skepticism. The next time you see someone cite him as a "data-driven" voice of reason, you might want to ask: has that person actually checked the data? Or are they just impressed that someone is angry about the right things?

Because anger, it turns out, is not analysis. And being loud is not the same as being right.
