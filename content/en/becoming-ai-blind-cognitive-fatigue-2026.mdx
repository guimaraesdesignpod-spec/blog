# Becoming AI-Blind: The Cognitive Fatigue We Didn't See Coming

A few days ago, developer Rafał Cymerys published a short essay that hit a nerve.

He described a creeping, almost embarrassing habit he had developed at work: someone would send him a document — a design spec, a marketing deck, a technical requirements sheet — and his brain would simply refuse to process it. He would read the words, but the meaning wouldn't stick. He would end up in an endless back-and-forth with the sender, asking questions about things that were already covered in the document he had supposedly just read.

When he sat down to analyze these moments, he found the common denominator. Every single one of those documents carried a strong trace of AI.

Not plagiarism. Not laziness, necessarily. Just the unmistakable residue of a large language model: the slightly too-polished phrasing, the odd lingo that doesn't quite match the team's vocabulary, the generous use of phrases like "This cuts just through it" or "The Redis backbone redefines the product." The verbose explanation of a simple concept wrapped in the uncertain, hedging tone of a model trying to sound confident while it's actually just guessing.

Cymerys realized his brain had done something remarkable without asking for permission. It had learned to spot the statistical signature of synthetic text and, like a spam filter, had started moving it straight to the mental junk folder.

He called it **AI-blindness**.

## The Banner Blindness of Our Generation

If you have been on the internet long enough, you remember banner blindness. It was the phenomenon, first documented in the late 1990s, where users subconsciously learned to ignore rectangular advertisements at the top and sides of web pages. Eye-tracking studies showed that people's gazes literally jumped over ad banners. The brain had created a mental model: "Rectangle at the top with flashy colors? Not content. Skip."

AI-blindness is the same mechanism, but applied to text.

The human brain is an extraordinary pattern-matching machine. It is constantly looking for shortcuts to conserve energy. Reading is a cognitively expensive task. When the brain detects a text that follows the repetitive, probabilistic patterns of an LLM — the fake contrasts, the absurd certainty, the rhythmically perfect paragraphs, the desperate attempt to make every checkbox sound like a paradigm shift — it makes a snap judgment. "Low signal-to-noise ratio. Not worth the glucose."

And it moves on.

This is not about being anti-technology. Cymerys himself is careful to say that he is typically very impressed by AI. The problem is not the technology. The problem is the *volume* and the *homogenization*.

When a tool makes it trivially easy to generate a twenty-page marketing strategy deck, the natural human response is not to marvel at the efficiency. It is to become suspicious of anything that looks like it might have been produced that way. And suspicion, in the economy of attention, is fatal. Once a reader suspects a document is synthetic, they stop reading for content and start reading for "tells." The game changes from comprehension to detection. And the message is lost.

## The Tells We Can't Unsee

Security researcher Florian Roth wrote about this earlier in the year, calling it the "AI voice." He pointed out the stylistic fingerprints that are increasingly hard to hide.

The first is **fake contrast**. The AI loves to frame things as binary showdowns. "This is not X, this is Y." "It's not a feature, it's a paradigm shift." Real human thought is messier. It lives in the gray areas. It qualifies. It contradicts itself sometimes. AI text, trained on a corpus of startup blogs and LinkedIn thought leadership, defaults to a weirdly smug, final-sounding certainty that feels synthetic because it is.

The second is **absurd certainty**. Always. Never. Everyone. No one. "This changes everything." Real people hesitate. They leave wiggle room because they know the world is complicated. AI, which is fundamentally rearranging probability distributions, tries to mask that uncertainty with declarative confidence. It sounds like a management consultant who has just read one book and is ready to restructure your entire company.

The third is the **rhythm**. Short hook. Big claim. Three neat support points. Dramatic conclusion. The text starts to sound assembled rather than written. The sentence groups become too symmetrical. "No vendor. No black box. No negotiation." It is the literary equivalent of stock photography.

These are not flaws in the model. They are artifacts of the training data and the optimization function. The model is trained to produce text that is statistically similar to human writing. But "statistically similar" is not the same as "genuine." It is a compression algorithm for human expression, and like all compression, it loses the subtle frequencies that carry meaning.

When your brain has been exposed to enough of this — and in 2026, if you work in tech, your brain has been exposed to *a lot* — it builds a classifier. And that classifier starts firing before you have even finished the first paragraph.

## The Productivity Paradox

Here is the cruel irony.

AI was sold to us as a productivity multiplier. Write your emails faster. Draft your documentation in seconds. Generate meeting summaries automatically. And it works, in a sense. The documents are produced faster than ever.

But what happens when the recipient is AI-blind?

Cymerys describes exactly this. He receives an AI-generated document. His brain filters it out. He asks the sender clarifying questions about things already in the document. The sender, who may have spent mere seconds prompting the model, now has to spend minutes in a back-and-forth to repair the communication breakdown. The net time saved is zero, or negative.

The AI has created a new form of work: the work of overcoming AI-blindness. It is the work of adding a human voice to a synthetic draft. It is the work of trimming the fluff, removing the hallucinated architecture diagrams, and injecting the specific, contextual nuance that the model could not possibly know. It is the work of sending a follow-up message that says, "Sorry, I didn't read that properly because it read like AI output. Can you just tell me what you actually need?"

This is not a bug in the user. This is a systemic mismatch between the ease of production and the difficulty of consumption.

Economists have a concept called **Jevons paradox**. It states that as technology makes the use of a resource more efficient, demand for that resource can actually increase because it is now used in more contexts. AI is creating a Jevons paradox for attention. By making text production infinitely cheap, it is flooding the channel with low-signal content. And human attention, which is fixed, is responding by becoming more selective, more skeptical, and more exhausted.

## The Quiche on the Menu

In his essay, Cymerys includes a photograph that perfectly captures the absurdity of the moment.

He is on vacation, walking past restaurants on the Baltic coast. One menu has a photograph of a quiche. His brain, now hyper-trained on pattern detection, immediately flags it. "That looks like AI-generated food photography." He ignores it and walks past. A minute later, something nags at him. "Wait. Did they really put up a photo of quiche with mold?"

He walks back. The quiche is covered in what looks like actual mold. It is not a synthetic image. It is a real, disgusting, poorly photographed slice of quiche. But his AI-blindness classifier had misfired, labeling a real thing as synthetic noise because it shared surface-level similarities with the kind of uncanny, slightly-off imagery that generative models produce.

This is the risk. When our filters become too aggressive, we start throwing away signal along with the noise. The AI-blindness that protects us from low-effort slop can also blind us to genuine, if clumsy, human communication. A poorly written but heartfelt email from a junior colleague might get classified as "probable AI output" and ignored. A rough first draft of a good idea might be dismissed because it shares structural DNA with a ChatGPT essay.

The filter becomes the problem.

## Can We Fix This?

There are two broad approaches to solving AI-blindness, and neither is simple.

The first is **better detection and labeling**. If AI-generated content were transparently labeled — if every document came with a clear watermark or metadata tag saying "Produced with assistance from Claude 4.5" — the reader's brain might not have to do the guessing game. The suspicion could be replaced with informed skepticism. We know the ad is an ad because it is marked as one. We might learn to engage with labeled AI text differently than we engage with text we suspect is trying to trick us.

But this requires industry-wide standards and honest disclosure, which are in short supply. The incentive for the sender is to hide the AI assistance, because the stigma is growing. We are entering an era where "AI-generated" is becoming synonymous with "low effort." Nobody wants to admit they used a ghostwriter, especially when the ghostwriter is a server farm.

The second approach is **better output**. The models will get better. The tells will become subtler. The fake contrasts and absurd certainties will be dialed down. In a year, the difference between a good human writer and a good AI assistant may be nearly invisible. But this creates its own problems. If we cannot tell the difference, we lose the ability to assign credit, to trust provenance, to value the human effort that goes into genuine insight.

There is a third path, and it is the one Cymerys is quietly advocating for in his essay: **human curation**.

Use the AI as a first draft. Then edit it. Aggressively. Inject your own voice. Remove the lingo. Add the specific detail that only you know. Make it weird. Make it human. The AI is a tool, not an author. The author is you. And if you abdicate that role, do not be surprised when your readers abdicate the role of paying attention.

## The Deeper Question

AI-blindness is a symptom of a larger shift in how we relate to information.

For most of human history, the production of text was expensive. It required time, expertise, and effort. That expense was a signal. A book, a letter, a well-researched report — these things commanded attention because they represented a real investment by the sender.

Generative AI has collapsed that signal. It has made the production of text as cheap as breathing. And when everything is cheap, nothing signals value anymore. The market for attention becomes a screaming bazaar where every vendor is using a megaphone, and the only defense is to put in earplugs.

The question is not whether AI is good or bad. The question is whether we, as individuals and as a culture, can develop new heuristics for sorting the valuable from the vacuous in a world where the old heuristics — length, polish, grammatical correctness, even coherence — no longer work.

Cymerys's brain has already started building that heuristic. It is a crude one. It misfires. It is exhausting. And it is probably necessary.

Because the alternative — reading every AI-generated twenty-page marketing concept deck with genuine openness — is a recipe for cognitive burnout. The brain is doing what it has always done: protecting itself from overload. The only difference is that the overload is no longer coming from too much information. It is coming from too much *synthetic* information, indistinguishable at a glance from the real thing.

We are not just becoming AI-blind. We are becoming AI-cynical. And that cynicism, however rational, comes with a cost. It erodes trust. It slows communication. It makes us suspicious of genuine human expression that happens to sound a little too polished.

The fix is not in the model. It is in the sender. And in the culture we choose to build around these tools.

If you want someone to read what you wrote, you still have to write it. The AI can help you think. It cannot think for you. And if you let it try, do not be surprised when your audience lets their eyes slide right past it, searching for something that sounds like it came from a human who actually cares.

In a world drowning in synthetic text, authenticity is the only signal left. And authenticity, it turns out, is still very hard to fake.
