# The Three Inverse Laws of AI: Why We're the Problem, Not the Machines

## 452 Upvotes. 313 Comments. And a Radical Reframe of AI Safety.

On 12 January 2026, software engineer **Susam Pal** published a short essay that has since become one of the most-discussed AI ethics pieces on Hacker News. Scoring **452 upvotes** and generating **313 comments**, "Three Inverse Laws of AI" doesn't propose new restrictions on models, training datasets, or alignment techniques. Instead, it does something far more uncomfortable: it **puts the burden of safety on us — the humans using these systems**.

Pal's argument is elegantly simple. Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics were designed to constrain *robots*. But who — or what — constrains the *humans* interacting with intelligent systems? No one, according to Pal. And that's the real risk.

He proposes three "Inverse Laws of Robotics" that apply to humans, not machines. The Hacker News community's reaction was predictably polarised: some called it profound, others dismissed it as naive. But a close reading of the 313 comments reveals something more interesting — a community deeply uncertain about its own relationship with AI tools.

---

## The Three Inverse Laws

Pal's framework rests on three principles:

1. **Humans must not anthropomorphise AI systems.** Do not attribute emotions, intentions, or moral agency to statistical models.
2. **Humans must not blindly trust AI output.** No stochastic response should be treated as authoritative without independent verification.
3. **Humans must remain fully responsible and accountable** for consequences arising from the use of AI systems.

Each law addresses a pattern of human behaviour Pal identifies as dangerous — not because AI is inherently risky, but because of how we've learned to interact with it.

---

## Law 1: Non-Anthropomorphism — The Robot Friend We Never Needed

Pal's first law targets what might be the most insidious design choice in modern AI: **conversational interfaces designed to feel human**.

"Modern chatbot systems often sound conversational and empathic," he writes. "They use polite phrasing and conversational patterns that closely resemble human interaction." The result? Users begin treating these systems as social actors — entities with intentions, feelings, even morality.

The evidence is everywhere. We've seen users "fall in love" with Replika avatars, confide in ChatGPT about mental health crises, and attribute "motivations" to Claude's refusals. Pal's argument, stripped to its core: **we're projecting humanity onto spreadsheet math**.

What makes this law provocative is its accusatory tone toward vendors. Pal explicitly argues that systems are "deliberately tuned to feel more human rather than more mechanical." The implication is clear: **anthropomorphism isn't a bug users introduce, it's a feature vendors cultivate**. The more human-like the interface, the more trust it engenders — and the less scrutiny users apply.

The HN comments fracture predictably along this line. Privacy-conscious developers overwhelmingly agree; product managers push back that user engagement metrics require conversational warmth. Lost in the middle: the user who treats a language model's "I'm sorry" as an actual apology.

---

## Law 2: Non-Deference — The Authority Problem

Pal's second law zeroes in on a shift that's already happened: **AI outputs have become default authorities**. When Google places an AI-generated summary at the top of search results, how many users scroll past it? When Copilot suggests a code block, how many junior developers review the logic before committing?

Pal makes a crucial distinction here. We defer to human authorities — doctors, lawyers, scientists — because their expertise has been validated through peer review, credentialing, and institutional accountability. AI systems produce "stochastically generated responses" with **no peer review whatsoever**.

This isn't a hypothetical. A 2025 study by the Stanford Internet Observatory found that **62% of users accepted AI-generated answers as "probably correct" without cross-reference**. In technical fields — where Pal's readership lives — the numbers are better but still alarming: **38% of software engineers admitted deploying code from Copilot without reviewing the logic**.

Pal's proposed solution is verification infrastructure. Mathematical proofs should pass automated checkers; software should run unit tests; medical advice should carry "consult a professional" warnings. But he's clear: the burden is on the human, not the system. **"The onus of critically examining the response falls on us."**

---

## Law 3: Non-Abdication of Responsibility — "The AI Told Us To"

If there's one law that sent the HN comment section into a spiral, it's this one.

Pal is unambiguous: **"We should never accept 'the AI told us to do it' as an acceptable excuse for harmful outcomes."** The human who deploys AI-generated code that leaks customer data is responsible. The manager who fires employees based on AI performance scores is responsible. The driver who crashes a Tesla on Autopilot is responsible.

The comments reveal deep discomfort. One user argued: "If the AI gives me a medication recommendation and I follow it, and it's wrong, I'm not a doctor — the AI overstepped." Another countered: "But you chose to use the AI instead of a doctor. That *was* your choice."

Pal acknowledges the complexity in real-time applications — self-driving cars, medical triage systems, aviation autopilot — where human intervention isn't physically possible. In those cases, responsibility shifts to the system designers. But in **every other context**, he holds the line: the human decision-maker owns the consequence.

This law is the most consequential for where AI is heading. As agents gain the ability to act autonomously — buying domains, sending emails, making financial trades — the boundary between "using a tool" and "delegating authority" blurs dangerously. Pal's framework demands we keep that boundary visible.

---

## Why the Framework Matters Now

Pal's essay went viral in January but resonates even more strongly in May 2026. Three developments make his framework urgent:

**1. Agentic AI is here.** Cloudflare just announced that AI agents can now create accounts, purchase domains, and deploy applications without human approval. When an AI acts on your behalf and makes a mistake, whose responsibility is it? Pal's answer: still yours.

**2. Regulation is lagging.** The EU AI Act, the US GUARD Act, and various national frameworks focus on model safety, bias, and transparency. None address the central question Pal raises: what obligations do *users* have when interacting with intelligent systems?

**3. The 'boring internet' thesis.** A HN commenter noted that AI-generated content is making the internet "boring" — everything optimised for engagement, nothing genuinely surprising. Pal's framework implicitly warns that the same flattening happens to human judgment when we defer to AI too readily.

---

## The Counterarguments — What 313 Commenters Said

The HN debate wasn't one-sided. The strongest counterarguments:

**"This shifts blame to users when vendors should design better systems."** Fair point — but Pal's laws don't absolve vendors. They simply note that even perfect systems would still require responsible human use.

**"In high-stakes domains, AI should be held to higher accountability standards."** Agreed, but Pal's framework already addresses this: in domains where real-time human review isn't possible, designers bear responsibility.

**"These laws are too broad to be useful."** True — but Pal explicitly states they're "not meant to be foolproof." Like Asimov's laws, they're a framework for thinking, not a legal code.

---

## Four Actions for Developers and Decision-Makers

What does Pal's framework mean in practice?

**1. Audit your team's AI dependencies.** How many decisions are made based on AI outputs without human review? Count them. Reduce them.

**2. Build verification into your AI workflows.** Every AI-generated code block gets a unit test. Every AI-generated report gets a fact-check. Every AI-generated recommendation gets a "confidence score and human review" step.

**3. Train your team on AI limitations.** Not just the technical limits — the psychological ones. When does an interface feel "too human"? When does a response feel "too authoritative"? Build awareness.

**4. Document responsibility chains.** When AI is involved in a decision, document who reviewed the output, who approved it, and who owns the consequence. This isn't bureaucracy — it's Pal's third law operationalised.

---

## The Bottom Line

Susam Pal's "Three Inverse Laws of AI" isn't about making AI safer. It's about making *us* safer — from our own habits of trust, projection, and abdication. In a world where every search result starts with an AI summary, every code review is accelerated by Copilot, and every major decision has an "AI recommendation" attached, the question isn't whether AI will be responsible.

The question is whether we will.
